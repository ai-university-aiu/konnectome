PART ONE. THE WAKING STATES

Waking is not one state but a family of operating points on the arousal and attention axes, and this part catalogues four of them.

At one corner sits the desynchronised, task-engaged configuration that nearly every cognitive experiment silently assumes.

Beside it sits the alpha idle, the eyes-closed configuration in which the mind wanders and quietly generates.

Above both sits focused absorption, the flow configuration, waking tuned to its optimal gain.

And below all three sits drowsy waking, the descending platform, the genuine intermediate on the way down to sleep, and the state in which vigilance failures kill drivers.


CHAPTER 1. ALERT TASK-ENGAGED WAKING (The Desynchronised Online Configuration) (The Working Day) (COG)

Open your eyes, pick up a task, and every catalogue in this corpus snaps into one coordinated posture: thalamic relays to tonic transmission, cortical sheets to fast asynchronous chatter, muscles to ready tone, hormones to daytime baseline.

This is the state the whole architecture exists to serve, the configuration in which the organism computes on the world in real time.

It is also the great unexamined default, the operating point at which nearly every cognitive experiment ever run was conducted.

Working names what the organism does in it: perception, decision, action, and learning, all online, all against the clock of a moving world.

Day names its scheduling: this is the state the circadian day programme books for the sixteen hours of light.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Alert task-engaged waking is the global state in which the ascending arousal systems run at full daytime output, the cortex is desynchronised, and processing is dominated by the task-positive attention networks rather than the inward-looking default mode network, the set of midline brain regions active during rest and self-referential thought.

In it the organism samples the world, holds goals in working memory, selects actions, and writes the day's episodic record into the hippocampus, the memory-indexing structure of the medial temporal lobe.

Every online cognitive job in the corpus is scheduled here.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The electroencephalogram (EEG), the recording of the brain's electrical rhythms from the scalp, shows low-voltage fast activity: desynchronised beta at 13 to 30 cycles per second and gamma at 30 to 60 and above, with amplitudes of roughly 10 to 30 microvolts, and posterior alpha suppressed whenever the eyes are open and a task is running.

Muscle tone is high, the electromyogram is active, and the eyes make voluntary saccades, the rapid jumps of gaze, at roughly 2 to 4 per second during visual work.

Heart rate runs near 60 to 100 beats per minute with respiratory sinus arrhythmia present, breathing near 12 to 20 breaths per minute, core temperature on its daytime plateau near 37 degrees Celsius.

Cortisol, the adrenal stress and mobilisation hormone, sits on its declining daytime curve after the morning peak of roughly 10 to 20 micrograms per decilitre; melatonin, the darkness hormone, is near zero.

Pupil diameter, the most convenient external gauge of arousal tone, holds a mid-range 3 to 5 millimetres under constant light.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The state is thrown and held by the ascending arousal nuclei of the Layer 9 catalogue firing at their waking maxima: the locus coeruleus (noradrenaline) at a tonic 1 to 3 spikes per second with task-locked phasic bursts, the basal forebrain and pedunculopontine cholinergic populations (acetylcholine) driving cortical and thalamic activation, the raphe (serotonin), the tuberomammillary nucleus (histamine), and the midbrain dopamine groups.

The sleep-wake flip-flop of this layer's Part Three sits latched to its wake pole, and the lateral hypothalamic orexin system holds the latch, its excitatory drive onto all of the arousal nuclei preventing inappropriate flips into sleep.

The state ends when Process S, the homeostatic sleep pressure, and Process C, the circadian drive, together tip the flip-flop, or when a stronger state of Part Four seizes the machinery.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• Layer 9 regional modes: cortex to desynchronised online processing; hippocampus to theta-coded encoding mode, with sharp-wave ripple replay suppressed; thalamus to faithful relay; cerebellum to real-time predictive correction; basal ganglia to action selection under dopamine.

• Layer 8 motif modes: thalamocortical loops to tonic relay rather than spindle or delta oscillation; winner-take-all attention circuits enabled; central pattern generators available on demand; the flip-flop motifs of the state machinery latched to wake.

• Layer 7 junctional regimes: glutamatergic synapses to high-fidelity transmission; plasticity permitted and steered by acetylcholine and noradrenaline toward task-relevant circuits; neuromuscular junctions fully enabled.

• Layer 6 cellular modes: thalamic relay cells to tonic single-spike mode, depolarised out of burst mode; cortical pyramidal cells to sustained asynchronous firing; arousal-nucleus cells to their waking discharge profiles; astrocytes to activity-coupled metabolic support.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The state is announced on all five aminergic and cholinergic channels at once, at their waking maxima, over seconds to minutes: noradrenaline from the locus coeruleus, acetylcholine from basal forebrain and brainstem, serotonin, histamine, and dopamine, with orexin as the stabilising sixth.

Extracellular acetylcholine and noradrenaline in cortex run several-fold above their slow-wave sleep levels, and this joint elevation is the physical definition of the waking broadcast.

Cortisol and thyroid hormone announce the same state on the endocrine timescale of hours.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Perception under attention: sensory cortex processes with gain set by the attention networks, so that attended signals are amplified and unattended ones suppressed, measurable as a roughly 20 to 30 percent firing-rate modulation in single cells.

Working memory and executive control: the prefrontal cortex holds goals against distraction under moderate noradrenaline and dopamine tone.

Action: the full perception-decision-movement loop of the motor catalogues, at reaction times of roughly 200 to 400 milliseconds.

Encoding: the hippocampus writes the day's episodes in theta-gamma code, building the store that the sleep states of Part Two will consolidate.

Vigilance: continuous background threat scanning, ready to hand control to the phasic arousal state of Part Four within a heartbeat.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Waking as a whole occupies roughly sixteen hours of the adult day, and the alert task-engaged configuration fills whatever fraction of it demand requires, typically several hours in bouts of minutes to a few hours.

Sustained attention within a single unbroken task degrades measurably within 20 to 30 minutes, a limit every vigilance study confirms.

The circadian drive schedules the state's quality: alertness dips in the early afternoon and collapses in the pre-dawn hours if waking is extended.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry from sleep is through sleep inertia, minutes to half an hour of degraded performance while the broadcasts climb; entry from relaxed wakefulness takes only the second or two needed for alpha to desynchronise when the eyes open or a task begins.

Exit is downward through relaxed wakefulness and drowsy waking as arousal tone falls, or sideways into focused absorption when engagement deepens, or upward into the arousal and stress states of Part Four when the interrupt machinery fires.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Daytime configuration: cortisol on its post-peak decline, growth hormone near its daytime trough, melatonin suppressed, thyroid axis at daytime tone, insulin and glucagon tracking meals.

The endocrine system in this state serves the brain as a fuel-logistics service, holding blood glucose near 4 to 7 millimoles per litre for a brain that burns roughly 20 percent of the body's energy.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Balanced sympathetic and parasympathetic tone with a mild sympathetic lean during task effort: heart rate variability preserved, blood pressure near 120 over 80 millimetres of mercury, digestion permitted between efforts, sweating and pupil size tracking cognitive load.

Task effort measurably co-opts the autonomic channel: pupil diameter dilates by roughly 0.2 to 0.5 millimetres with working-memory load.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

This is the most expensive state per hour: whole-brain energy use runs at its maximum, and the waking hours accumulate the very costs, adenosine, synaptic potentiation, and metabolic waste, that Process S counts and sleep discharges.

Glymphatic clearance, the brain's fluid waste-washing system, runs at its minimum here; maintenance is deferred, not performed.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The newborn holds this state only in fragments of minutes; consolidated daytime waking assembles over the first years as the circadian and orexin machinery matures.

Adolescents enter it late in the morning by circadian phase delay; in old age the state fragments again, with daytime napping returning and sustained attention shortening.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The desynchronised waking state is as old as the vertebrate arousal systems that throw it, conserved from fish to mammals, and the corpus reads it as the ancestral online configuration on which the specialised sleep states were later installed.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

All mammals and birds show an equivalent desynchronised waking state; dolphins famously run it in one hemisphere at a time.

Between humans, stable trait differences in alertness, chronotype, and sustained-attention capacity span roughly twofold, with moderate heritability.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is a disorder of this state's gain control; narcolepsy type 1, the loss of the orexin stabiliser, makes the state collapse without warning into sleep; delirium is the state running with its broadcasts deranged; severe sleep deprivation degrades it until microsleeps of the drowsy state intrude.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Circadian alerting drive from the suprachiasmatic nucleus; homeostatic sleep pressure (opposing); sensory salience streams; orexin stabilising tone.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Waking broadcast on all aminergic and cholinergic channels; mode assignments to every layer below; behaviour on the skeletomotor channel.

• STATE.

◦ Arousal tone scaled 0 to 1, near 0.6 to 0.8 here; attention allocation vector; flip-flop latched to wake.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Locus coeruleus tonic rate 1 to 3 spikes per second; cortical acetylcholine severalfold above sleep levels; EEG dominated by 13 to 60 cycles per second; pupil 3 to 5 millimetres.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Desynchronisation on eye opening under 1 second; phasic alerting 0.1 to 0.5 seconds; sustained-attention decay 20 to 30 minutes; state duration minutes to hours.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Narcolepsy type 1: orexin neuron loss above 90 percent, and the state becomes unstable, with sleep attacks and cataplexy.

◦ Delirium: acute broadcast derangement, commonly cholinergic failure, producing fluctuating inattention; affects roughly one in four older hospital inpatients.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

An implementation registers this state as online_task_state, the default foreground scheduler state: it subscribes to the two-process governor and the interrupt lines on the global configuration bus, and publishes the full waking mode assignment to every layer.

The nearest engineering analogue is a computer's full-power performance state with the interactive scheduler running; the biological difference is that the hardware itself is retuned by the broadcast, every cell shifting mode, rather than merely being clocked faster.

ENGLISH READABLE CODE:

The purpose of this procedure is to show attention-gated processing under the high neuromodulator gain of alert waking, in which attended signals are amplified and unattended signals are suppressed.

Create a list variable called input signals holding the signal strengths of four channels.

Create a list variable called attention weights holding one weight per channel, summing to one.

Create a number variable called arousal gain with initial value 2.0.

Create a list variable called outputs with initial value empty.

For each channel from the first to the fourth, perform the following actions.

    Calculate the product of the channel's signal strength, the channel's attention weight, and arousal gain.

    Append the result to outputs.

End the loop.

Find the channel with the largest output.

Display that channel and its output to the console, because that channel wins the competition for working memory.

The procedure is complete.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because the point is a one-line gain equation applied across channels, and Python shows the competition and its winner without ceremony.

# Attention-gated processing in alert task-engaged waking.
# Attention multiplicatively modulates sensory gain (Reynolds and
# Heeger, Neuron 2009); arousal broadcast sets the overall gain.
signals = {"target": 0.6, "distractor": 0.9, "background": 0.4, "noise": 0.2}
attention = {"target": 0.7, "distractor": 0.1, "background": 0.1, "noise": 0.1}
AROUSAL_GAIN = 2.0        # waking broadcast, versus about 0.5 when drowsy

def process(signals, attention, gain):
    return {ch: s * attention[ch] * gain for ch, s in signals.items()}

for gain, label in ((2.0, "alert waking"), (0.5, "drowsy")):
    out = process(signals, attention, gain)
    winner = max(out, key=out.get)
    print(f"{label:12s} outputs=" +
          ", ".join(f"{ch}={v:.2f}" for ch, v in out.items()))
    print(f"{label:12s} winner: {winner}")
# Note the weaker raw distractor loses to the attended target,
# and the whole competition flattens when gain falls.

WHAT MAKES IT UNMISTAKABLE:

A desynchronised low-voltage fast electroencephalogram with open, purposefully moving eyes, full muscle tone, all six arousal broadcasts at maximum, and an organism that answers when you call its name: the configuration every other state in this layer is defined against.


CHAPTER 2. RELAXED WAKEFULNESS (The Posterior Alpha Configuration) (The Alpha Idle) (COG)

Close your eyes without intending to sleep and, within a second or two, the back of your head begins to hum at ten cycles per second.

That hum, the alpha rhythm, was the first signal Hans Berger ever pulled from a human scalp in 1924, and it announces a genuine global state: awake, unoccupied, and idling.

But the idle is not off: while the posterior cortex hums, the default mode network comes forward, and the mind wanders, simulates, remembers, and incubates.

Alpha names the rhythm that signs the state.

Idle names the operating point: engine running, clutch out, ready to engage in under a second.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Relaxed wakefulness is the awake global state in which external task demand is withdrawn, posterior alpha dominates the electroencephalogram (EEG), the scalp recording of brain rhythms, and processing shifts from the task-positive networks to the default mode network, the midline and lateral parietal system active during rest, self-reflection, and imagination.

In it the organism does inward work: mind-wandering, autobiographical memory, prospection (imagining futures), and the incubation from which solutions surface unbidden.

The corpus admits it as a cognitive state precisely because idle is secretly generative.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The EEG shows the alpha rhythm, 8 to 13 cycles per second and typically near 10, at 20 to 60 microvolts over occipital and parietal scalp, strongest with eyes closed and abolished within a second by eye opening or mental arithmetic, the classic alpha blocking reaction.

Muscle tone is present but reduced; the eyes, if closed, are still or drift slowly; heart rate eases toward 55 to 75 beats per minute and breathing toward 10 to 15 breaths per minute.

Functional imaging shows default mode network activity elevated above task levels, and EEG studies show alpha power higher during self-reported mind-wandering than during on-task moments.

Hormones remain in daytime configuration; the signature is neural and autonomic, not endocrine.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The arousal nuclei remain on but ease their output: locus coeruleus tonic firing drops toward roughly 0.5 to 1 spike per second, and cholinergic drive to cortex relaxes.

The alpha rhythm itself is generated in thalamocortical loops between the visual thalamus (the pulvinar and lateral geniculate) and occipital cortex, the Layer 8 oscillator motif engaging as soon as sensory drive and top-down demand withdraw.

The state holds as long as nothing demands engagement, and ends the instant the arousal machinery is called, which is why it is the most easily exited state in the layer.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• Layer 9 regional modes: occipital and parietal cortex to alpha idling with sensory gain reduced; default mode network to foreground generative mode; hippocampus toward retrieval and recombination rather than encoding; attention networks to standby.

• Layer 8 motif modes: the thalamocortical loop motif to its 10 cycles per second alpha oscillation; winner-take-all attention circuits disengaged; the flip-flop still latched to wake.

• Layer 7 junctional regimes: sensory relay synapses to rhythmically gated, reduced-fidelity transmission within alpha; associative cortico-cortical synapses free-running, permitting loose recombination.

• Layer 6 cellular modes: thalamic relay cells partway toward burst mode, hyperpolarised enough to resonate at alpha; pyramidal cells of the default mode network to elevated firing; arousal-nucleus cells to reduced tonic rates.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The same waking channels carry the announcement, at reduced amplitude: noradrenaline and acetylcholine ease down over seconds to tens of seconds while remaining well above sleep levels.

No hormone specifically marks the state; its broadcast is the partial withdrawal of the alerting broadcast, a lowered flag rather than a new one.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Mind-wandering and spontaneous thought: the default mode network generates a stream of self-relevant simulations, occupying by experience-sampling studies somewhere near 30 to 50 percent of waking thought.

Autobiographical retrieval and prospection: past episodes are replayed and recombined into imagined futures, the constructive-memory operation the hippocampus and default mode network run together.

Incubation: unsolved problems left simmering here yield delayed solutions at rates reliably above continuous-work controls in insight experiments.

Opportunistic early consolidation: quiet waking rest permits hippocampal replay events otherwise reserved for sleep, and post-learning rest measurably improves retention.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Bouts run from seconds to tens of minutes, entered dozens of times across a waking day whenever demand withdraws; no dedicated scheduler books it, which is exactly what makes it the default of the demand-free moment.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry from task engagement takes a second or two of disengagement, signed by alpha's return; exit upward to task engagement is equally fast, signed by alpha blocking.

Exit downward is the important boundary: if sleep pressure is high, alpha slows, fragments, and gives way to the drowsy state of Chapter 4 without any decision being taken.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Unchanged daytime configuration; the state is too brief and too shallow to move any hormonal axis, though relaxation measurably lowers circulating adrenaline relative to task stress.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

A parasympathetic lean: heart rate and blood pressure ease, heart rate variability rises, digestion proceeds, pupils narrow toward 2 to 4 millimetres.

This is the autonomic posture that relaxation techniques deliberately cultivate.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain energy use falls only a few percent below task levels, the famous costliness of the resting brain, because the default mode network spends what the sensory systems save.

The state performs no structural maintenance, but the rest it affords measurably restores subsequent attention.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The posterior dominant rhythm matures slowly: roughly 4 cycles per second in infancy, 8 by age three, and the adult near-10 by adolescence.

In healthy ageing it slows by roughly half a cycle to a cycle per second, and marked slowing below 8 is a clinical warning sign.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Alpha-like idling rhythms appear across mammals, and the corpus reads the state as the natural resting point of a thalamocortical system built to oscillate whenever drive is withdrawn, secondarily colonised by the primate default mode network for simulation.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Individual alpha frequency is a stable trait spanning roughly 8 to 12 cycles per second between adults, moderately heritable, and about 10 percent of healthy adults show little scalp alpha at all.

Dogs, cats, and monkeys show equivalent relaxed-waking rhythms; the generative uses claimed for the human state are, honestly, harder to verify comparatively.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Diffuse alpha slowing marks the encephalopathies and the dementias, and posterior alpha that fails to block with eye opening is a classic sign of a damaged visual cortex.

Ruminative disorders, depression chief among them, are in part disorders of this state's content: the default mode network generating negatively looped simulation instead of useful wandering.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Task-demand withdrawal; eye closure; circadian and homeostatic tone (setting how close beneath drowsiness waits).

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Alpha-gated sensory attenuation; default mode network output stream; readiness signal permitting sub-second re-engagement.

• STATE.

◦ Arousal tone near 0.4 to 0.6; alpha power scaled 0 to 1; default mode network engagement scaled 0 to 1.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Alpha frequency 8 to 13 cycles per second, individual mean near 10; alpha amplitude 20 to 60 microvolts; locus coeruleus tonic rate 0.5 to 1 spike per second.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Alpha onset after eye closure about 1 second; alpha blocking under 0.5 seconds; bout length seconds to tens of minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Depressive rumination: the generative idle captured by a negative attractor, default mode network hyperconnectivity documented in major depression.

◦ Encephalopathy: posterior rhythm slowing below 8 cycles per second, a graded marker of failing cortical function.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

An implementation registers this state as generative_idle_state: it subscribes to the task-demand line on the global configuration bus and publishes recombination products to the memory and planning services.

The nearest engineering analogue is an idle process that speculatively precomputes and prefetches; the biological difference is that the idle work is not discarded on wake but is the organism's imagination, and no engineered idle loop yet owns that job.

ENGLISH READABLE CODE:

The purpose of this procedure is to show default mode generative recombination during idle, in which stored memory fragments are recombined into novel candidate thoughts and occasionally one is good enough to surface.

Create a list variable called memory store holding several stored experience fragments.

Create a number variable called surfacing threshold with initial value 0.8.

Repeat the following actions ten times.

    Choose two different fragments from memory store at random.

    Combine the two fragments into a candidate thought.

    Calculate a usefulness score for the candidate between 0 and 1.

    If the usefulness score is greater than surfacing threshold is true, then perform the following actions.

        Display the candidate thought and its score to the console, because it has surfaced into awareness as an idea.

    End the conditional block.

End the loop.

The procedure is complete.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because random recombination over a small store is three lines of standard library, and the occasional surfacing of a scored pairing is the entire phenomenon.

# Default-mode generative recombination in the alpha idle.
# Rest-state spontaneous thought recombines stored elements; incubation
# yields insight above continuous-work baselines (Christoff et al.,
# Nature Reviews Neuroscience 2016; Sio and Ormerod, Psych Bull 2009).
import random
random.seed(11)

store = ["locked door", "spare key", "neighbour", "window latch",
         "morning routine", "coat pocket", "bicycle", "rain"]
AFFINITY = {("locked door", "spare key"): 0.95,
            ("spare key", "coat pocket"): 0.9,
            ("locked door", "window latch"): 0.85}

def usefulness(a, b):
    return AFFINITY.get((a, b), AFFINITY.get((b, a), random.uniform(0, 0.6)))

THRESHOLD = 0.8
for step in range(12):                      # a dozen idle moments
    a, b = random.sample(store, 2)
    score = usefulness(a, b)
    marker = "  <-- surfaces as an idea" if score > THRESHOLD else ""
    print(f"{step:2d}  {a:14s} + {b:14s}  {score:.2f}{marker}")

WHAT MAKES IT UNMISTAKABLE:

An awake, responsive person with closed or unfocused eyes and a scalp humming at ten cycles per second over the back of the head, whose rhythm vanishes within half a second of eye opening: no other state pairs full wakefulness with a dominant posterior alpha.


CHAPTER 3. FOCUSED ABSORPTION (The Optimal-Gain Configuration) (The Flow Configuration) (COG)

There is a state in which a difficult task becomes effortless, time distorts, the self falls silent, and performance climbs to the top of its range: musicians, surgeons, climbers, and programmers all report it, and psychology names it flow.

The corpus admits it as a global state with a moderate-confidence note, because its boundaries are less crisp than the classical stages, but its configuration is real and measurable: the locus coeruleus held at optimal mid-tonic gain, and the self-monitoring circuits of the prefrontal cortex transiently quietened.

Flow names the felt continuity of action.

Configuration names the corpus's claim: this is not a mood but a coordinated whole-system operating point, alert waking tuned to the peak of its own performance curve.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Focused absorption is the high-engagement, low-self-monitoring variant of alert task-engaged waking, entered when a well-learned skill meets a challenge closely matched to it.

In it the organism performs at the top of its trained range: perception, decision, and action fuse into a single loop, feedback is absorbed instantly, and the inner commentator, the self-referential monitoring stream, goes quiet.

Everything that runs in alert waking runs here, faster and cleaner, minus the overhead of watching oneself do it.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The electroencephalogram (EEG), the scalp recording of brain rhythms, shows a task-engaged fast background with, in several studies, elevated frontal midline theta at 4 to 8 cycles per second, the signature of deep task focus, and reduced markers of self-referential processing.

Functional imaging supports transient hypofrontality: reduced activity in parts of the prefrontal cortex serving self-monitoring, alongside sustained activity in the sensorimotor circuits doing the work; the evidence is convergent but moderate in confidence.

Pupil diameter sits mid-range and stable, the established correlate of intermediate locus coeruleus tonic firing; heart rate is moderately elevated with breathing entrained to the task's rhythm.

Subjective time distortion and loss of self-consciousness are the behavioural hallmarks, reliably measured by the standard flow questionnaires.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The pivotal governor is the locus coeruleus, the pontine noradrenaline nucleus of the Layer 9 catalogue, held in its intermediate tonic band, roughly 1 to 2 spikes per second, where phasic task-locked bursting is largest: the adaptive gain regime of Aston-Jones and Cohen, in which the system exploits the task in hand rather than exploring away from it.

Dopamine from the midbrain groups sustains engagement by rewarding each cycle of the action loop; the challenge-skill match is what keeps prediction errors small but nonzero, feeding the loop.

The state is held by continuous task feedback and collapses when the match breaks: too hard, and tonic firing climbs into distractible over-arousal; too easy, and it falls toward the idle of Chapter 2.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• Layer 9 regional modes: sensorimotor and task-relevant cortex to maximal-gain processing; the self-referential prefrontal and default mode circuits to transient hypofrontality, their monitoring mode suspended; basal ganglia to automatised skill execution; cerebellum to full predictive control.

• Layer 8 motif modes: winner-take-all attention circuits locked to a single task channel; cortico-basal-ganglia loops to chunked, sequence-level execution; thalamocortical loops to tonic relay.

• Layer 7 junctional regimes: task-pathway synapses at optimal noradrenaline gain, the peak of the inverted U; plasticity focused narrowly on the practised circuit.

• Layer 6 cellular modes: locus coeruleus cells clamped mid-tonic with strong phasic bursts; task-circuit pyramidal cells to high signal-to-noise firing; prefrontal monitoring populations to reduced rates.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Noradrenaline at intermediate tonic, strong phasic; dopamine at sustained engagement levels; acetylcholine focusing cortical gain on the attended channel; all on the seconds timescale, with no distinct hormonal announcement.

The broadcast difference from ordinary alert waking is not which channels are on but where on their curves they are held: this state is a tuning, thrown from above like every other.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Peak skilled performance: the perception-action loop at its trained maximum, with expert reaction advantages of tens of milliseconds and error rates at their floor.

Implicit, automatised control: the basal ganglia execute chunked sequences without deliberative supervision, which is why introspection during flow degrades performance.

High-rate procedural learning: each loop iteration delivers immediate feedback under focused neuromodulation, near-optimal conditions for sharpening the skill in use.

What does not run matters equally: explicit self-evaluation, mind-wandering, and background worry are suspended, and their absence is the felt effortlessness.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Bouts typically run from a few minutes to an hour or two, bounded by the sustained-attention budget and by fuel; no internal scheduler books the state, since it must be earned by a challenge-skill match, making it the only state in this part that cannot simply be commanded.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is from alert task engagement, typically after 10 to 20 minutes of uninterrupted work as monitoring falls away; interruption resets the climb, which is why fragmented environments rarely permit it.

Exit is by task completion, by interruption, by the match breaking in either direction, or by fatigue; return to ordinary self-monitoring waking takes seconds to minutes and often carries a mild disorientation about elapsed time.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Daytime configuration with a mild engagement signature: modest catecholamine elevation without the cortisol surge of the stress states, which is the endocrine line between absorbed challenge and threat.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Moderate sympathetic activation harnessed to the task: heart rate elevated 10 to 30 beats per minute above rest in physical flow, stable mid-range pupils, breathing entrained to task rhythm; a mobilised but unpanicked posture, distinct from both the idle and the surge.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Energetically this is alert waking with its budget re-allocated, not enlarged: the savings from silenced self-monitoring are spent on the task circuits.

Long bouts spend the same attention and glucose budgets as hard task work, and end in genuine fatigue; maintenance is deferred as in all waking.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The state requires an automatised skill, so it is scarce before middle childhood and grows with expertise; it remains available across the lifespan wherever a practised skill survives, and reported flow frequency is roughly stable from adolescence through old age.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The corpus reads flow as the exploit pole of an ancient explore-exploit controller: the adaptive gain machinery of the locus coeruleus is conserved across mammals, and a state that locks a predator onto a pursuit, or a toolmaker onto a strike sequence, pays for itself directly.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

The underlying gain machinery is general-mammalian, but flow as measured is a human report; individual proneness to absorption is a stable, moderately heritable trait, and experts in any domain reach the state more readily than novices, since only they possess circuits automatised enough to run without supervision.

Admission of this chapter carries the outline's moderate-confidence note: the state's reality as a distinct configuration is supported, its boundaries remain debated.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

No disease is a disorder of flow itself; the pathologies are of access and of misuse.

Attention deficit and anxiety disorders block entry, the first by unstable gain, the second by monitoring that will not stand down; behavioural addictions, gambling and gaming foremost, are engineered capture of the state's machinery by manufactured challenge-skill ladders.

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Continuous task feedback; challenge-skill match estimate; freedom-from-interrupt condition on the arousal lines.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Peak-rate skilled output; procedural learning updates; suppression command to self-referential circuits.

• STATE.

◦ Arousal tone near 0.5 to 0.7, clamped; self-monitoring engagement near 0; single locked attention channel.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Locus coeruleus tonic rate 1 to 2 spikes per second, phasic bursts large; frontal midline theta 4 to 8 cycles per second elevated; challenge-skill ratio near 1.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Entry climb 10 to 20 minutes; loop iteration 0.1 to 1 second; bout minutes to about two hours; recovery on exit seconds to minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Over-arousal collapse: tonic firing climbs past optimum and performance slides down the right flank of the inverted U into distractibility, the everyday form of choking under pressure.

ENGLISH READABLE CODE:

The purpose of this procedure is to show the inverted U relating arousal gain to performance, and why the flow configuration holds gain at the peak.

Create a number variable called optimal gain with initial value 0.6.

Create a number variable called width with initial value 0.25.

Create a list variable called gain levels holding values from 0.1 to 1.0 in steps of 0.1.

For each gain level in gain levels, perform the following actions.

    Calculate the difference of the gain level and optimal gain.

    Calculate the square of the difference divided by the square of width.

    Set performance to the exponential of the negative of that result.

    Display the gain level and performance to the console.

End the loop.

Report that the largest performance occurs at the gain level nearest optimal gain, which is the operating point the flow configuration clamps.

The procedure is complete.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because the inverted U is one Gaussian expression and a printed bar chart makes the peak, and the two ways off it, visible at a glance.

# The optimal-gain performance curve: the inverted U of Yerkes and
# Dodson (1908), implemented by locus coeruleus tonic gain in the
# adaptive gain theory (Aston-Jones and Cohen, Annu Rev Neurosci 2005).
# Flow clamps the system at the peak.
import math

OPTIMUM, WIDTH = 0.6, 0.25          # mid-tonic gain, curve width

def performance(gain):
    return math.exp(-((gain - OPTIMUM) / WIDTH) ** 2)

labels = {0.2: "drowsy / idle", 0.6: "FLOW (clamped here)",
          1.0: "over-aroused, distractible"}
print("gain  performance")
for i in range(1, 11):
    g = i / 10
    p = performance(g)
    bar = "#" * round(p * 40)
    note = "  <-- " + labels[g] if g in labels else ""
    print(f"{g:4.1f}  {bar}{note}")
best = max((performance(i / 10), i / 10) for i in range(1, 11))
print(f"peak performance {best[0]:.2f} at gain {best[1]:.1f}")

CHAPTER 4. DROWSY WAKING (The Vigilance-Failing Intermediate) (The Descending Platform) (NON-COG)

Between waking and sleep there is a platform, and the descending traveller stands on it longer than the textbooks admit: alpha slowing and fragmenting, eyelids drooping in slow rolls, the eyes beginning to drift.

It is a real state with a real signature, not merely a boundary crossed in an instant.

And it is the most operationally lethal entry in this part, because the organism standing on the platform still believes it is awake, while seconds-long microsleeps have already begun to intrude.

Descending names the direction of travel: this is waking with the arousal broadcasts throttling down.

Platform names its genuine stability: the system can hold here for many minutes, neither committing to sleep nor recovering full alertness.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Drowsy waking is the global state in which the arousal broadcasts have fallen far enough that wakefulness can no longer be held continuously, but the sleep-wake flip-flop has not yet committed to sleep.

The organism does progressively less in it: reaction times stretch, lapses appear, and behaviour continues on automatised momentum while supervision decays.

It is tagged (NON-COG) because no computation is scheduled here; it is the corridor in which computation fails.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The electroencephalogram (EEG), the scalp recording of brain rhythms, shows the posterior alpha rhythm slowing by roughly 1 cycle per second, fragmenting, and shifting forward on the head, with theta at 4 to 7 cycles per second intruding; microsleeps appear as abrupt seconds-long theta and delta episodes with the eyes open or closing.

The eyes show the diagnostic pair: slow rolling movements at well under 1 cycle per second, and drooping lids measured as PERCLOS, the percentage of eyelid closure over the pupil across a time window, with values above roughly 80 percent closure marking severe drowsiness and predicting microsleep intrusion.

Blink duration lengthens, saccades, the rapid gaze jumps, slow and shrink, heart rate eases and its variability rises, breathing slows, muscle tone dips, and the posture begins to sag in nodding cycles.

Psychomotor vigilance testing shows the behavioural signature: lapses, reactions slower than 500 milliseconds, multiplying severalfold.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The state is thrown by the joint pressure of Process S, the adenosine-linked homeostatic sleep drive, and an unfavourable phase of Process C, the circadian alerting waveform, throttling the arousal nuclei of the Layer 9 catalogue: locus coeruleus tonic firing falls below roughly 0.5 spikes per second and cholinergic drive wanes.

The ventrolateral preoptic sleep-promoting population begins firing against the weakening arousal side, and the flip-flop enters the contested middle band its design exists to forbid.

The platform holds because orexin tone and behavioural effort, posture, light, noise, and interest, keep partially recharging the waking side; it ends either in recovery, if demand or stimulation returns, or in sleep onset, often without the subject registering the crossing.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• Layer 9 regional modes: cortex to unstable idling with local sleep intrusions, patches slipping briefly offline while the rest wakes on; hippocampus out of reliable encoding mode; attention networks to degraded, lapsing vigilance.

• Layer 8 motif modes: thalamocortical loops sliding from alpha toward theta; the sleep-wake flip-flop to its contested mid-band; winner-take-all circuits failing to hold any winner.

• Layer 7 junctional regimes: sensory relay fidelity falling; neuromuscular drive to postural muscles decaying in nodding cycles.

• Layer 6 cellular modes: thalamic relay cells hyperpolarising toward burst mode; cortical cells showing the first local slow-wave behaviour; arousal-nucleus cells at minimal waking rates.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The announcement is a fade: noradrenaline, acetylcholine, histamine, and serotonin all declining together over minutes, with rising extracellular adenosine, the wake-cost metabolite, as the anti-broadcast pressing them down.

Melatonin, if the hour is late, adds its circadian permission on the tens-of-minutes timescale.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Nothing is scheduled here, and that is the point: encoding, working memory, and vigilance all degrade, while only automatised momentum continues.

Its one legitimate function is transitional, giving the flip-flop a loaded position from which sleep onset can complete.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Minutes to tens of minutes per episode, appearing on schedule at the circadian afternoon dip and across the late evening, and off schedule whenever sleep debt commands; under sleep deprivation it invades the whole waking day.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is by drift from relaxed wakefulness as the broadcasts throttle; no decision marks it, and self-assessment reliably lags the physiology, the fact that kills drivers.

Exit upward requires stimulation or demand and succeeds within seconds but does not last while sleep pressure stands; exit downward is stage N1 sleep onset, often unnoticed.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Late-day configuration where naturally scheduled: cortisol near its trough, melatonin beginning its evening rise roughly two hours before habitual sleep; when drowsiness is forced by deprivation at other hours, the endocrine day-state persists and the mismatch is felt as the peculiar misery of fighting sleep at noon.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

A progressive parasympathetic shift: heart rate and blood pressure easing, heart rate variability rising, pupils constricting toward 2 millimetres with slow oscillations in diameter, and thermoregulation beginning distal skin warming, the heat-loss prelude to sleep onset.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Brain energy use eases modestly below alert levels; no maintenance runs yet, and the state's whole energetic meaning is that the accumulated costs of waking, counted by adenosine, have won the argument.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Infants cross this platform many times a day; healthy adults, ideally twice; in old age it broadens and re-invades the day as sleep consolidates poorly, and daytime drowsiness in the elderly is a marker worth clinical attention.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Every animal with distinct wake and sleep must cross between them, and a graded, recoverable intermediate is the safe design: the corpus reads the platform as the necessary cost of making the flip-flop recoverable rather than hair-triggered.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

All mammals show behaviourally equivalent drowsiness; individual vulnerability to drowsy lapses under sleep loss is a stable, trait-like difference spanning severalfold between adults, and it does not correlate well with self-rated sleepiness, which is the operational hazard.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Drowsy driving is implicated in roughly one fifth of fatal road crashes by conservative estimates; obstructive sleep apnoea, the disorder of repeated nocturnal airway collapse, is the leading medical cause of pathological daytime drowsiness; narcolepsy makes the platform appear without warning; and shift work institutionalises it.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Homeostatic sleep pressure (adenosine); circadian alerting phase; environmental stimulation and posture; orexin tone.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Degraded vigilance output; sleep-readiness signal to the flip-flop; observable warning signs, slow lids, rolling eyes, nodding posture.

• STATE.

◦ Arousal tone near 0.2 to 0.4 and falling; flip-flop in contested mid-band; microsleep counter rising.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Alpha slowed by about 1 cycle per second and fragmenting; theta 4 to 7 cycles per second intruding; PERCLOS above 80 percent at severe risk; psychomotor lapses over 500 milliseconds multiplying.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Microsleeps 0.5 to 15 seconds; nodding cycles seconds; episode minutes to tens of minutes; stimulation rescue seconds, but unstable.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Vigilance failure in charge of a vehicle: the microsleep at speed, a leading documented cause of fatal single-vehicle departures.

◦ Narcolepsy and severe apnoea: the platform appearing irresistibly and out of schedule.

PART TWO. THE SLEEP STATES

Sleep is not one thing that happens when waking stops; it is a scheduled tour through four qualitatively distinct whole-organism configurations, each with its own electrical signature, its own chemistry, and its own catalogue of jobs to run.

The night runs these configurations in a repeating loop of roughly ninety minutes, four to six times before morning, and the order of the loop is itself functional rather than incidental.

Three of the four sleep stages are grades of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, and by the standard sleep-staging convention they are named N1, N2, and N3 (N for non-rapid-eye-movement, or non-REM), from lightest to deepest.

The fourth is rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep, the paradoxical state in which the cortex runs awake-like while the body is paralysed.

This part catalogues the four stages plus two constructs that are not themselves states but that govern them: the ultradian cycle that sequences the stages, and the sleep inertia that follows the exit from sleep and proves that transitions at this layer carry real time constants and real costs.


CHAPTER 5. STAGE N1 (SLEEP ONSET, THE HYPNAGOGIC THRESHOLD) (NON-COG)

Lie still in a dim room at the end of a long day and there is a threshold you cross without ever catching yourself crossing it, a vestibule between the lit hall of waking and the deep rooms beyond.

In this vestibule the images come unbidden, faces and colours and falling sensations, and the outside world thins to a rumour.

It lasts only minutes, and its most famous product is the sudden whole-body jolt that yanks you back from the edge.

N1 names the first and shallowest grade of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep.

Sleep Onset names its office: it is the doorway the system passes through on the way down, the state in which wakefulness is relinquished but deep sleep is not yet held.

The Hypnagogic Threshold names its inner experience: hypnagogia is the dreamlike drift of images and sensations that occurs at the boundary between waking and sleep, and a threshold is a place one is meant to cross, not to dwell in.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Stage N1 is the lightest stage of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, the brief transitional configuration the brain enters as it lets go of settled wakefulness and begins its descent toward deeper sleep.

The organism in N1 is disengaging: it stops tracking the outside world, muscle tone begins to fall, and the mind starts to generate its own imagery rather than process the room.

It is a genuine operating state and not merely a boundary line, but it is an unstable one, easily reversed by a small noise and normally passed through rather than held.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The electroencephalogram, abbreviated EEG, is the scalp recording of the brain's summed electrical rhythms, and its N1 signature is the replacement of the relaxed-waking alpha rhythm (8 to 12 cycles per second, or hertz, abbreviated Hz) by lower-voltage mixed-frequency activity dominated by theta waves (4 to 7 Hz).

The eyes show slow, rolling, pendular movements rather than the darting of waking or REM.

Muscle tone measured by the electromyogram, abbreviated EMG (the recording of muscle electrical activity), is high relative to deeper stages but beginning to relax.

Vertex sharp waves, brief high-amplitude negative deflections over the top of the head, appear late in the stage, and a hypnic jerk (a sudden involuntary whole-body muscle contraction at sleep onset) is often coupled to one.

N1 typically occupies only about 2 to 5 percent of a healthy adult night, with moderate confidence, and the transition to sleep is usually scored at the first appearance of this pattern.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The throw into N1 is the first tilt of the sleep-wake flip-flop, the mutual-inhibition switch (catalogued in Part Three) between the sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic neurons of the hypothalamus and the ascending arousal nuclei of the brainstem and hypothalamus.

Rising homeostatic sleep pressure (Process S, driven by accumulated adenosine, the fatigue-signalling molecule that builds across waking) plus the falling evening arm of the circadian arousal drive (Process C) together push the switch past its tipping point.

N1 is the weakly held state precisely because the switch has only just begun to tip: arousal nuclei are quietened but not silenced, and the loss of the stabilising orexin drive (the wake-holding signal from the lateral hypothalamus) is only partial, so a small stimulus flips the system back to waking.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

N1 broadcasts a partial, still-settling assignment, which is why its signature is mixed rather than clean.

• Layer 9 (regions): it begins to withdraw the thalamus from faithful relay toward the rhythmic mode, and lets the default-mode and imagery-generating cortex run loosely, producing hypnagogic images.

• Layer 8 (microcircuit motifs): it starts to engage thalamocortical oscillator motifs but has not yet locked them into the regular spindling of N2.

• Layer 7 (junctions): it lowers the gain of sensory relay synapses, beginning the sensory disconnection that deep sleep completes.

• Layer 6 (cells): it begins to shift thalamic relay cells away from steady tonic firing toward the burst-prone mode that fuller sleep will assign, without yet holding them there.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The neuromodulators are the physical announcement of the state, and at N1 they are in mid-fall rather than at a new set-point.

Noradrenaline (the alerting signal from the locus coeruleus), serotonin (from the raphe nuclei), and histamine (from the tuberomammillary nucleus) are all declining from their waking levels, and orexin drive is easing.

Acetylcholine (the cortical-activation signal) remains moderate, which helps keep the EEG only lightly slowed and the state reversible.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that this state or machinery serves a governing, protective, or metabolic role rather than a cognitive-computational role, and is tagged (NON-COG); N1 is a transit corridor whose job is to hand the system safely to N2, not to run consolidation.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

A single N1 bout at the start of the night lasts roughly one to seven minutes before N2 begins, and across the whole night N1 sums to about 2 to 5 percent of total sleep time.

It recurs briefly at stage transitions and after awakenings, and its amount rises with age, with fragmented sleep, and with sleep disorders, so an excess of N1 is a marker of poor sleep continuity.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is from drowsy waking, marked by the dropout of alpha and the onset of slow rolling eye movements.

Exit downward is into N2, marked by the first sleep spindle or K-complex; exit upward is a return to waking, which a hypnic jerk or an external stimulus can trigger.

The transition is deliberately quick because a stable switch should not linger at its unstable midpoint.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

N1 carries no distinctive endocrine set-point of its own; it inherits the evening decline of cortisol (the stress and wake hormone) from its circadian low and precedes the growth-hormone pulse that deep sleep will trigger.

Melatonin (the darkness hormone from the pineal gland) is already elevated, having helped open the sleep gate.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The autonomic nervous system, the involuntary controller of heart, vessels, and glands, begins its shift toward parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) dominance.

Heart rate and blood pressure edge down, breathing slows and steadies, and core body temperature continues the fall that helped initiate sleep.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain energy use is only slightly below quiet waking in N1; the large metabolic savings of sleep arrive with the slow waves of N3, not here.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

N1 is present across the lifespan but grows as a fraction of the night with age, reflecting lighter and more fragmented older-adult sleep.

In infancy the clean adult staging is not yet present, and N1-like light sleep emerges as the sleep EEG matures over the first months and years.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

A brief, reversible light-sleep vestibule is widespread across mammals and is the ancestral safe way to relinquish vigilance in stages rather than all at once.

The hypnic jerk may be an old reflex, perhaps a brainstem misfire during the tone transition or a vestigial arboreal safeguard against falling; both accounts are plausible and neither is settled.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

The proportion of N1 varies widely between people and rises with insomnia, sleep apnoea, and ageing.

At least 80 percent of people report having experienced a hypnic jerk, and up to about 10 percent experience them nightly, so the state's most vivid event is nearly universal but unevenly distributed.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

An abnormally high proportion of N1 is a general marker of disrupted, non-restorative sleep across many disorders.

Frequent or violent hypnic jerks can themselves impede sleep onset, and prominent hypnagogic hallucinations, when combined with sleep-onset REM and cataplexy, point toward narcolepsy (the disorder of unstable state boundaries catalogued in Part Three).

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Homeostatic sleep pressure (Process S) high; circadian arousal drive (Process C) falling; melatonin elevated; ambient stimulation low.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Theta-dominant low-voltage EEG; slow rolling eye movements; falling muscle tone; occasional vertex sharp waves and hypnic jerks.

• STATE.

◦ Flip-flop position just past the wake side; partial neuromodulator withdrawal; sensory gain lowered but not closed.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ EEG 4 to 7 Hz theta over mixed low-voltage activity; about 2 to 5 percent of the night; bout length about 1 to 7 minutes.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Seconds to a few minutes; among the fastest and least stable of the sleep states.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Excess N1 as fragmentation in insomnia and sleep apnoea; sleep-onset hypnagogic hallucinations with cataplexy in narcolepsy.

CHAPTER 6. STAGE N2 (SPINDLED LIGHT SLEEP) (COG)

For nearly half of every night you are in a state most people would call simply asleep, unremarkable and dreamless, and yet the electrical record shows it to be anything but idle.

Twice a minute or more, a brief accelerating burst of rhythm sweeps the cortex like a wave crossing a still pond, and every so often a single giant deflection rings out like a struck bell.

These are not noise; they are the packaging events of memory, and their nightly count tracks what you learned that day.

N2 names the second grade of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, the plurality state of human sleep by time.

Spindled names its defining event, the sleep spindle, a short waxing-and-waning burst of fast rhythm named for its tapered shape on the recording.

Light Sleep names its depth: it is deeper and steadier than N1 but shallower than the slow-wave sleep of N3, an intermediate that is nonetheless doing serious work.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Stage N2 is the intermediate, spindle-bearing stage of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep in which the sleeper is fully disengaged from the environment yet not in the deepest slow-wave sleep.

The organism in N2 holds a stable, sensory-gated configuration and uses it to run memory consolidation in discrete bursts, tied to its two signature events.

Far from being a mere corridor between light and deep sleep, N2 is a working state: the sleep spindle is the thalamocortical mechanism that gates the transfer of memory traces, and spindle density (the number of spindles per minute) tracks overnight learning.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The EEG (electroencephalogram, the scalp recording of the brain's summed electrical rhythms) shows a theta background punctuated by two hallmark graphoelements.

Sleep spindles are bursts of 11 to 16 Hz rhythm (most typically 12 to 15 Hz), lasting about 0.5 to 3 seconds, generated in the thalamus.

K-complexes are large biphasic deflections, a sharp negative wave followed by a slower positive one, occurring spontaneously and in response to stimuli.

Eye movements are largely absent, muscle tone (measured by the electromyogram, the recording of muscle electrical activity) is low and stable, heart rate and breathing are slow and regular, and core temperature is at or near its nightly decline.

N2 occupies roughly 45 to 55 percent of total sleep time in healthy adults, the largest share of any stage, with high confidence.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

N2 is entered when the sleep-wake flip-flop has tipped firmly to the sleep side and the thalamocortical system settles into its spindling regime.

The spindle itself is generated by the thalamic reticular nucleus, a thin shell of inhibitory (activity-damping) neurons wrapped around the thalamus, whose rhythmic bursts of inhibition onto thalamocortical relay cells produce the waxing-and-waning 12 to 15 Hz volley that the cortex then broadcasts.

The state is held by low aminergic tone (low noradrenaline, serotonin, and histamine) together with a quietened but not silenced arousal system, and the K-complex acts as a sleep-protective response that can either abort an arousal or usher in a spindle.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

N2 broadcasts the assignment that turns the thalamocortical loop into a spindle generator and opens the consolidation window.

• Layer 9 (regions): it assigns the thalamus to its rhythmic spindling mode, the hippocampus to intermittent sharp-wave-ripple replay coupled to spindles, and sensory cortex to receive spindle-timed reactivation.

• Layer 8 (microcircuit motifs): it engages the thalamic reticular-nucleus oscillator motif that manufactures the spindle and the coupling motif that nests hippocampal ripples inside spindle troughs.

• Layer 7 (junctions): it sets cortical synapses to a plasticity-permissive regime during the spindle, so that spindle-timed reactivation can strengthen the right connections.

• Layer 6 (cells): it places thalamic reticular cells into rhythmic burst firing and thalamocortical relay cells into the alternating burst-and-pause that carries the spindle.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Acetylcholine is intermediate, lower than in waking or REM; noradrenaline, serotonin, and histamine are low; and orexin drive is off.

This low-aminergic, moderate-cholinergic climate is what permits stable spindling and the plasticity window it opens, and it is announced over the whole cortex on a timescale of tens of seconds to minutes.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

N2 runs spindle-gated memory consolidation, and this is the heart of the chapter.

During a spindle the thalamocortical loop briefly opens a plasticity-permissive window, and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples (fast bursts that replay recently learned patterns) are timed to fall inside it, so that the neocortex receives the memory trace exactly when it is most able to strengthen the relevant synapses.

Because spindles recur many times per minute, N2 delivers consolidation as a stream of discrete, well-timed transfer events rather than as one continuous process, and the density and number of these events predict how much of the day's learning survives the night.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

N2 appears in every cycle and grows as a fraction of each successive cycle across the night, so most late-night sleep is N2.

Its total is about 45 to 55 percent of the night, and spindle density is highest in later cycles, tracking the shift from deep-sleep-dominated early sleep to lighter, spindle-rich late sleep.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry from N1 is scored at the first sleep spindle or K-complex.

Exit downward into N3 is scored when high-amplitude slow waves come to occupy at least a fifth of the recording epoch; exit upward or into REM occurs at later cycle boundaries.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

N2 shares the general nocturnal endocrine climate, elevated melatonin and low cortisol, without a signature hormone of its own; the large growth-hormone pulse belongs to the N3 that N2 leads into.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone dominates: heart rate and blood pressure are low and stable, breathing is regular, and heart-rate variability is high, though K-complexes are accompanied by brief autonomic upticks.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain metabolism is reduced below waking but above the deep trough of N3, consistent with an intermediate depth.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Sleep spindles emerge in the first months of life and mature through childhood, and their features are highly individual and heritable, almost a fingerprint.

Spindle density and fast-spindle activity decline with age, and this decline is associated with age-related weakening of overnight memory consolidation.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Spindles and spindle-like thalamocortical rhythms are found across mammals, and the thalamic reticular nucleus that generates them is an old and conserved structure, consistent with an ancient role for this state in offline processing.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Spindle frequency, density, and topography vary substantially between individuals and are stable within a person, and higher spindle activity is associated with better sleep-dependent memory and, in some studies, with measures of cognitive ability.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Reduced spindle density is reported in schizophrenia, where it is linked to impaired sleep-dependent memory, and in several neurodegenerative and ataxic conditions.

Because N2 is so large a share of the night, disorders that fragment it, such as sleep apnoea, broadly degrade consolidation.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Recently encoded hippocampal memory traces; thalamic reticular-nucleus rhythm; low aminergic tone; sleep-pressure and circadian state.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Sleep spindles at 12 to 15 Hz; K-complexes; spindle-timed cortical reactivation; strengthened cortical memory traces.

• STATE.

◦ Thalamocortical loop in spindling mode; plasticity window opening once or twice per minute; sensory gate closed.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Spindle band 11 to 16 Hz; spindle duration 0.5 to 3 seconds; about 45 to 55 percent of the night; spindle density roughly 2 to 5 per minute of non-REM sleep.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Spindle 0.5 to 3 seconds; inter-spindle interval seconds; the plasticity window per spindle sub-second to a second.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Reduced spindle density and impaired consolidation in schizophrenia and ageing; fragmentation of N2 in sleep apnoea.

It subscribes to a queue of freshly encoded hippocampal traces and to the thalamic spindle clock, and it publishes discrete transfer-commit events on the global configuration bus, each gated to a spindle window.

The nearest engineering analogue is a batched, clocked write from a fast cache to slower durable storage, committing only during scheduled windows; the biological difference is that the window itself briefly changes the plasticity of the target, so the write and the medium's writability are one event.

The purpose of this procedure is to run memory consolidation as a stream of discrete transfer events, each of which is allowed to happen only while a sleep spindle holds the plasticity window open.

Create a list called pending traces and fill it with the memory items encoded during the day, each with a priority number.

Create a number variable called spindle open with initial value of false.

Create an empty list called consolidated.

Begin a loop that steps through the night minute by minute.

    Decide whether a spindle occurs this step by comparing a spindle probability to a random draw, and set spindle open to true if it occurs and false otherwise.

    If spindle open is true, then perform the following actions.

        Select from pending traces the item with the highest priority.

        Strengthen that item by a fixed consolidation amount.

        If the item's strength now exceeds the transfer threshold, then move it from pending traces to consolidated.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because the point is that consolidation arrives in discrete spindle-gated bursts rather than as a smooth flow, and a short loop shows that clearly.

# Spindle-gated transfer events in stage N2.
# Each sleep spindle briefly opens a plasticity window; the highest-priority
# hippocampal trace is strengthened only inside that window and is transferred
# to cortex once it crosses threshold (Antony et al.; Diekelmann and Born, 2010).
import random

random.seed(2)
pending = {f"trace_{i}": {"priority": random.random(), "strength": 0.0}
           for i in range(8)}
consolidated = []
SPINDLE_RATE = 0.05      # probability of a spindle per 1-second step
GAIN = 0.15              # strengthening delivered inside one spindle window
THRESHOLD = 1.0          # cortical transfer threshold

for step in range(600):                      # ~10 minutes of N2, per second
    spindle_open = random.random() < SPINDLE_RATE
    if spindle_open and pending:
        name = max(pending, key=lambda k: pending[k]["priority"])
        pending[name]["strength"] += GAIN    # plasticity only while spindle is open
        if pending[name]["strength"] >= THRESHOLD:
            consolidated.append(name)
            del pending[name]

print("transferred to cortex:", len(consolidated), "of 8")
print("order:", consolidated)

CHAPTER 7. STAGE N3 (SLOW-WAVE SLEEP, THE DEEP WORKS) (COG)

There is one stretch of the night, mostly in its first third, when the whole cortex falls into step, a million-fold chorus rising and falling together once a second, slower and vaster than any rhythm the waking brain can make.

This is the deepest sleep, the hardest to wake from, and the state a sleeper robbed of it will make up first the next night, before any other.

It is also the busiest maintenance shift the body runs: the memory that was packaged in lighter sleep is here rebuilt into the cortex for good, the synapses of the day are renormalised, growth hormone is poured out, and the brain's own drainage system runs at full flow to wash the day's waste away.

N3 names the third and deepest grade of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep.

Slow-Wave Sleep names its electrical hallmark, the large slow oscillation of the whole cortex.

The Deep Works names its double character: it is the deepest sleep, and it is the works, the plant, where the night's heaviest rebuilding and cleaning are done.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Stage N3, also called slow-wave sleep or deep sleep, is the deepest stage of non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, defined by high-amplitude slow oscillations sweeping the entire cortex.

The organism in N3 is at its most disconnected from the world and hardest to rouse, and it uses this deep, stable, low-metabolism configuration to run its heaviest offline programmes at once.

Those programmes are systems consolidation (the hippocampus-to-cortex transfer that makes memories permanent), synaptic renormalisation (the downscaling of the day's synaptic gains), endocrine restoration (the night's main growth-hormone pulse), and glymphatic clearance (the flushing of metabolic waste from brain tissue).

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The EEG (electroencephalogram, the scalp recording of the brain's summed electrical rhythms) is dominated by delta waves, high-amplitude slow activity below about 4 Hz, and by the slow oscillation of roughly 0.5 to 1 Hz, an alternation between a depolarised active up-state and a hyperpolarised silent down-state that sweeps across the cortex.

Scoring conventions require slow waves to occupy at least 20 percent of the epoch.

Eye movements are absent, muscle tone (by the electromyogram, the recording of muscle electrical activity) is low, heart rate and blood pressure are at their nightly lowest, breathing is slow and regular, and core temperature is near its minimum.

N3 occupies roughly 13 to 23 percent of the night in healthy young adults, concentrated in the first two cycles, with moderate to high confidence, and it declines markedly with age.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

N3 is thrown when sleep pressure is highest, in the first third of the night, and it is generated by the intrinsic slow oscillation of the cortex synchronised across the thalamocortical system.

The alternation between up-state and down-state arises from cortical network dynamics, the up-state being a period of recurrent excitation and the down-state a period of network-wide silence, coordinated with thalamic and hippocampal rhythms.

It is held by the lowest aminergic and cholinergic tone of the whole sleep-wake cycle, and it is discharged as the night proceeds and sleep pressure falls, so that later cycles carry progressively less N3.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

N3 broadcasts the deepest and most complete offline assignment of the night, the one that orchestrates the consolidation dialogue.

• Layer 9 (regions): it assigns the hippocampus to sharp-wave-ripple replay, the thalamus to spindle bursts nested on the slow oscillation, and the neocortex to the global up-down oscillation that times the whole exchange.

• Layer 8 (microcircuit motifs): it engages the cortical slow-oscillation motif and the triple-coupling motif that nests hippocampal ripples inside thalamic spindles inside cortical up-states, the physical substrate of systems consolidation.

• Layer 7 (junctions): it sets cortical synapses to net renormalisation, the down-selection and downscaling of the day's potentiated connections, while permitting targeted strengthening of replayed traces.

• Layer 6 (cells): it drives cortical pyramidal cells into synchronous up-down alternation and thalamic relay cells into the burst mode that produces nested spindles.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Acetylcholine falls to its lowest of the sleep-wake cycle, which is permissive for hippocampus-to-cortex transfer, and noradrenaline, serotonin, and histamine are all at their nightly lowest.

Growth-hormone-releasing hormone rises and orchestrates the pituitary growth-hormone pulse, so N3's broadcast is both neuromodulatory (a near-silent aminergic and cholinergic field) and endocrine (the growth-hormone surge), on a timescale of minutes.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

N3 runs the flagship computation of the whole volume: systems consolidation by interleaved replay, the transfer of memories from a fast hippocampal store to a slow neocortical store in a way that installs new memories without overwriting old ones.

The problem it solves is catastrophic forgetting, the failure mode in which teaching a network something new erases what it already knew; the brain's solution is to replay old and new memories interleaved during slow-wave sleep, so the neocortex is trained on a balanced mixture rather than on the new item alone.

Alongside this, N3 runs synaptic renormalisation (downscaling the day's synaptic weights to restore capacity and signal-to-noise, the synaptic-homeostasis account), the main growth-hormone pulse (driving tissue repair and growth), and glymphatic clearance (the enlargement of the fluid spaces between cells that lets cerebrospinal fluid flush metabolic waste, including amyloid, from the brain).

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

N3 is front-loaded: it dominates the first one to two cycles and diminishes or vanishes in later cycles as sleep pressure discharges.

Its total is about 13 to 23 percent of the night in healthy young adults, and after sleep deprivation the system rebounds by producing more and deeper N3 first, showing that it is the priority repayment.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry from N2 is scored when high-amplitude slow waves reach the 20 percent threshold of the epoch.

Exit is usually a lightening back into N2 before the cycle's REM period, and a forced awakening from N3 produces the most severe sleep inertia (Chapter 10), evidence of how far the state is from the waking configuration.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

N3 hosts the largest growth-hormone pulse of the twenty-four-hour day, released in the first slow-wave episode; cortisol is at its nadir early in the night and begins its pre-dawn rise later; and this deep-sleep endocrine restoration is a major reason sleep is physically as well as cognitively necessary.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Parasympathetic dominance is at its peak: heart rate, blood pressure, and sympathetic outflow are at their lowest and most stable of the whole cycle, a genuine cardiovascular rest period.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain metabolism and cerebral blood flow fall to their lowest of the sleep-wake cycle during N3, and it is in this low-metabolism, low-activity state that glymphatic clearance runs at full flow, so the deepest energy trough coincides with the heaviest cleaning.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Slow-wave sleep is abundant in children and adolescents, whose deep sleep supports development and the synaptic remodelling of the maturing brain, and it declines steeply across adulthood into old age.

The age-related loss of N3, especially of frontal slow-wave activity, tracks declines in overnight memory consolidation and is a focus of research into cognitive ageing.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Slow-wave sleep and its slow oscillation are conserved across mammals and have analogues in birds, and the rebound of deep sleep after deprivation is a robust cross-species signature that this state repays a genuine debt.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Slow-wave activity is highly individual and heritable and scales with prior waking duration, and it varies with age, exercise, and temperature, so the depth of a person's deep sleep is both a trait and a state marker.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Reduced or fragmented N3 is linked to impaired memory, and reduced glymphatic clearance in poor deep sleep is implicated in the accumulation of amyloid and in Alzheimer-type neurodegeneration.

N3 is the stage from which the non-REM parasomnias arise, including sleepwalking, sleep terrors, and confusional arousals, all of which are incomplete awakenings out of deep sleep.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Accumulated sleep pressure; hippocampal memory index; spindle and ripple timing; low aminergic and cholinergic tone.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Cortex-wide slow oscillation; consolidated neocortical memories; renormalised synaptic weights; growth-hormone pulse; glymphatic waste clearance.

• STATE.

◦ Thalamocortical system in synchronous up-down oscillation; hippocampus-to-cortex transfer channel open; metabolism at nightly minimum.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Slow oscillation about 0.5 to 1 Hz; delta below 4 Hz; slow waves at least 20 percent of the epoch; about 13 to 23 percent of the night in young adults.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Slow-oscillation period about 1 to 2 seconds; up-state and down-state each hundreds of milliseconds; growth-hormone pulse over minutes; deep-sleep episode tens of minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Impaired glymphatic clearance and amyloid accumulation with chronic N3 loss; sleepwalking, sleep terrors, and confusional arousals as incomplete awakenings from N3.

ENGLISH READABLE CODE:

The purpose of this procedure is to transfer memories from a fast hippocampal store into a slow cortical store during slow-wave sleep without erasing what the cortical store already knew, by replaying old and new memories interleaved.

Create a store called cortex that already holds a set of old memories, each as a pattern and its correct response.

Create a store called hippocampus that holds the new memories learned today.

Begin a loop that runs for a fixed number of replay rounds, one round per stretch of slow-wave sleep.

    Build a training batch by taking every new memory from the hippocampus and mixing it together with a sample of old memories drawn from the cortex.

    Shuffle the batch so that old and new items are interleaved rather than grouped.

    For each item in the batch, adjust the cortical store a small amount so that it better produces the item's correct response.

End the loop.

Measure how well the cortex now reproduces both the old memories and the new memories.

If the cortex reproduces both sets well, report success, because new learning was installed without catastrophic forgetting.

The procedure is complete.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because the whole claim of this chapter, that interleaving old and new during replay defeats catastrophic forgetting, can be shown as a controlled experiment: train the same store two ways and compare what it forgets.

# FLAGSHIP: systems consolidation in stage N3 by interleaved hippocampal replay.
# A fast store (hippocampus) teaches a slow store (cortex) overnight. Replaying
# NEW items alone erases OLD memories (catastrophic forgetting); interleaving OLD
# with NEW installs the new without erasing the old.
# (McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995, complementary learning systems;
#  Diekelmann & Born 2010, active systems consolidation.)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 20                                   # pattern width
def make(n):                             # n random pattern -> target pairs
    X = rng.standard_normal((n, N))
    W = rng.standard_normal((N,))
    return X, np.sign(X @ W)

Xold, yold = make(40)                    # OLD cortical memories
Xnew, ynew = make(10)                    # NEW hippocampal memories from today
w = np.linalg.lstsq(Xold, yold, rcond=None)[0]   # cortex has learned OLD

def acc(X, y):                           # fraction reproduced correctly
    return float(np.mean(np.sign(X @ w) == y))

def train(w, X, y, epochs=300, lr=0.02):
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for i in rng.permutation(len(X)):
            w += lr * (y[i] - np.sign(X[i] @ w)) * X[i]   # perceptron nudge
    return w

print(f"before sleep:      OLD={acc(Xold,yold):.2f}  NEW={acc(Xnew,ynew):.2f}")

# Way 1: replay NEW alone (no interleaving) -> catastrophic forgetting.
w_bad = train(w.copy(), Xnew, ynew)
def acc2(w,X,y): return float(np.mean(np.sign(X@w)==y))
print(f"replay NEW only:   OLD={acc2(w_bad,Xold,yold):.2f}  NEW={acc2(w_bad,Xnew,ynew):.2f}")

# Way 2: N3 interleaved replay -> NEW learned, OLD preserved.
Xmix = np.vstack([Xnew, Xold]); ymix = np.concatenate([ynew, yold])
w_good = train(w.copy(), Xmix, ymix)
print(f"interleaved (N3):  OLD={acc2(w_good,Xold,yold):.2f}  NEW={acc2(w_good,Xnew,ynew):.2f}")

WHAT MAKES IT UNMISTAKABLE:

The whole cortex breathing in unison at once per second, the sleeper nearly impossible to wake, growth hormone pouring out, and the memory of the day being written into the cortex for keeps while the brain washes itself clean.


CHAPTER 8. RAPID-EYE-MOVEMENT (REM) SLEEP (THE PARADOXICAL THEATRE) (COG)

Watch a sleeper deep in the last hours before dawn and you will see the eyes darting beneath the lids as if following a scene, while the body lies utterly slack, unable to move.

Inside, the cortex is running as hot as waking, spinning vivid narratives out of nothing, and yet the chemicals that in daylight shout warnings and stamp memories in are switched almost entirely off.

This is the paradox: a brain awake inside a body paralysed, a theatre in which the day's emotional experiences are replayed and quietly stripped of their sting.

REM stands for rapid eye movement, the darting eyes that name the state.

Paradoxical names the contradiction at its core, an activated cortex in a motionless body, a term coined precisely because the EEG looks awake while the sleeper is deeply asleep.

Theatre names its product: REM is the main stage of vivid dreaming, and the place where emotional memories are re-weighted.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Rapid-eye-movement sleep is the sleep state in which the cortex is activated to a near-waking degree, the eyes move rapidly, and the skeletal muscles are actively paralysed.

The organism in REM is internally busy and outwardly still: it generates vivid dreams, and it uses the state's unusual chemistry to recalibrate emotional memories and to integrate new information into existing knowledge structures.

REM has two sub-modes: phasic REM, marked by bursts of eye movements, muscle twitches, and internal waves, and tonic REM, the quieter background between the bursts.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The EEG (electroencephalogram, the scalp recording of the brain's summed electrical rhythms) is low-voltage and mixed-frequency, resembling waking, often with a sawtooth-wave pattern and, in deep structures, a prominent theta rhythm (4 to 8 Hz) in the hippocampus.

The eyes show the characteristic rapid movements, and muscle tone (by the electromyogram, the recording of muscle electrical activity) is nearly abolished, a state called REM atonia, broken only by brief twitches.

Heart rate and breathing become irregular and faster, especially in phasic REM, and thermoregulation is suspended, so the body drifts toward the ambient temperature.

REM occupies roughly 20 to 25 percent of the night in healthy adults, concentrated in the later cycles toward morning, with high confidence.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

REM is thrown by the REM switch (catalogued in Part Three), a second flip-flop in the pons between REM-on and REM-off neuronal populations, gated by the balance of cholinergic and aminergic tone.

REM-on cholinergic and glutamatergic populations activate the cortex, generate the internal waves (called ponto-geniculo-occipital or PGO waves, after the brainstem, thalamic, and cortical sites they traverse), and drive the eye movements and theta rhythm, while a descending circuit through the ventral part of the oral pontine reticular nucleus actively silences the spinal motor neurons to produce atonia.

The state is held by the near-total silence of the aminergic systems and ended when aminergic tone recovers, tipping the switch back.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

REM broadcasts an assignment unlike any other, an activated cortex driven by internal signals with the motor output cut.

• Layer 9 (regions): it assigns the hippocampus and amygdala (the emotional-salience hub) to theta-coupled interaction, the visual and association cortex to internally generated activation, and the motor output pathway to active inhibition.

• Layer 8 (microcircuit motifs): it engages the PGO-wave generator motif and the hippocampal-amygdala theta-coupling motif that drives emotional-memory plasticity.

• Layer 7 (junctions): it sets a plasticity regime tuned by high acetylcholine and near-zero noradrenaline, favouring the re-weighting and integration of existing traces over the faithful storage of new external input.

• Layer 6 (cells): it drives cortical cells into waking-like tonic firing while forcing spinal and cranial motor neurons into deep hyperpolarised silence.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The defining broadcast of REM is a chemical dissociation: acetylcholine runs high, near or above waking levels, while noradrenaline and serotonin fall to their lowest of the entire twenty-four-hour cycle, essentially silent.

This high-acetylcholine, near-zero-noradrenaline climate is the physical announcement of REM, and it is precisely what makes the state's signature emotional-memory computation possible, sustained across each REM period of minutes to tens of minutes.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

REM runs emotional-memory re-weighting and schema integration, and this is the heart of the chapter.

The leading account holds that REM replays the day's emotionally charged memories while noradrenaline is switched off, so that the memory content is reactivated and re-stored, but the associated arousal signal is stripped away, gradually softening the emotional charge of the memory while preserving its information, sometimes summarised as sleeping to forget the emotion while remembering the fact.

Theta coherence between the hippocampus and amygdala during REM drives PGO waves whose density predicts increases in plasticity-related gene expression, providing the molecular basis for this re-weighting, and the same activated, low-noradrenaline, high-acetylcholine state favours integrating new memories into existing schemas and recombining them, the substrate of dream narrative and of insight.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

REM is back-loaded across the night: the first REM period of the night is short, often under ten minutes, and successive REM periods lengthen, so that the longest, up to forty minutes, come in the final cycles before waking.

Its total is about 20 to 25 percent of the night, and REM deprivation produces a rebound in which lost REM is preferentially recovered, evidence of a homeostatic need.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is from N2 late in a cycle, marked by cortical activation, atonia, and the onset of rapid eye movements.

Exit is often into a brief awakening or into lighter non-REM sleep, and because the cortex is already activated, awakening directly from REM tends to produce less sleep inertia than awakening from N3.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

REM is concentrated in the later night when cortisol is rising toward its pre-dawn peak, and the growth-hormone pulse of early deep sleep is over by the REM-dominated hours; REM has no single signature hormone but sits within this late-night endocrine climate.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The autonomic nervous system is unstable and variable in REM, especially in phasic REM: heart rate and breathing surge and dip, blood pressure fluctuates, and this autonomic storminess, combined with suspended thermoregulation, makes REM a period of relative cardiovascular vulnerability.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain metabolism in REM is as high as, or higher than, in waking, so REM is an energetically expensive state despite the body's stillness, consistent with its intense internal computation.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

REM is greatly enriched in early life: newborns spend roughly half their sleep in a REM-like state, and its abundance during the period of maximal brain development points to a role in neural maturation and circuit wiring.

The proportion of REM falls through childhood to the adult level and declines more modestly in old age.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

REM sleep is found across mammals and, in a distinct form, in birds, and its deep conservation together with its post-deprivation rebound argue that it serves an essential and ancient function rather than being an accident of sleep.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

The amount and intensity of REM vary between species and individuals and are sensitive to antidepressant drugs, many of which strongly suppress REM by raising aminergic tone, a pharmacological confirmation of the state's chemistry.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

In REM sleep behaviour disorder the atonia fails, so sleepers physically act out their dreams, and this disorder is an important early marker of Parkinson-type neurodegeneration.

Disturbed REM and nightmare replay are central to post-traumatic stress disorder, consistent with a failure of the normal overnight de-escalation of emotional memory, and sleep-onset REM periods are a diagnostic feature of narcolepsy.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ The day's emotionally charged memory traces; hippocampal-amygdala theta drive; cholinergic REM-on activation; near-zero aminergic tone.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Vivid dream content; re-weighted emotional memories with softened arousal charge; integrated and recombined schemas; PGO waves and rapid eye movements.

• STATE.

◦ Cortex activated; motor output actively inhibited; noradrenaline off, acetylcholine high; two sub-modes, phasic and tonic.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Hippocampal theta 4 to 8 Hz; REM about 20 to 25 percent of the night; first REM period under 10 minutes lengthening to about 40; acetylcholine high, noradrenaline and serotonin near zero.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ REM period minutes to tens of minutes; phasic bursts sub-second to seconds; the re-weighting effect accrues over multiple nights.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Loss of atonia in REM sleep behaviour disorder as a marker of Parkinson-type neurodegeneration; failed emotional de-escalation and nightmare replay in post-traumatic stress disorder; sleep-onset REM in narcolepsy.

The purpose of this procedure is to re-weight emotional memories during rapid-eye-movement sleep by replaying them while the noradrenaline signal is switched off, so that the memory's content is kept but its emotional charge is reduced.

Create a list called memories, where each memory has a content value and an emotional charge value.

CODE SAMPLE:

Python is chosen because the claim is a simple contrast, that replay with noradrenaline switched off lowers emotional charge while leaving content intact, and a short loop makes the before-and-after obvious.

# Emotional memory re-weighting in REM sleep under low noradrenaline.
# REM replays emotional memories while noradrenaline is silenced, so the arousal
# charge decays across nights while the informational content is preserved
# (van der Helm & Walker 2009, "sleep to forget, sleep to remember";
#  Goldstein & Walker 2014, the emotional brain and sleep).
memories = [
    {"content": "dog bit me", "charge": 0.95},
    {"content": "exam question", "charge": 0.70},
    {"content": "argument", "charge": 0.85},
]
NIGHTS = 5
DECAY = 0.35          # fraction of charge stripped per night when noradrenaline is off

for night in range(1, NIGHTS + 1):
    noradrenaline = 0.0                     # REM silences the aminergic broadcast
    for m in memories:
        # content is reactivated but never rewritten; only the charge relaxes
        if noradrenaline == 0.0:
            m["charge"] *= (1 - DECAY)      # arousal tag decays toward calm baseline
    charges = ", ".join(f'{m["content"]}={m["charge"]:.2f}' for m in memories)
    print(f"after night {night}: {charges}")

print("contents preserved:", [m["content"] for m in memories])

CHAPTER 9. THE ULTRADIAN SLEEP CYCLE (THE NINETY-MINUTE WHEEL) (NON-COG)

No single night's sleep stage tells the whole story, because the states do not occur once and stay; they turn, in order, like a wheel.

Roughly every ninety minutes the sleeper rides from light sleep down into the deep works, back up, and out into the paradoxical theatre, then round again, four to six times before dawn.

And the wheel is weighted: it carries mostly deep sleep in its first turns and mostly dreaming in its last, so that the night has a shape, and the shape is part of the design.

Ultradian names its period: an ultradian rhythm is one that cycles more than once per day, here about every ninety minutes.

Sleep Cycle names the object, one full circuit through the stages.

The Ninety-Minute Wheel names its character: it is a turning schedule, not a state, and ninety minutes is its approximate turn.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The ultradian sleep cycle is the whole-night programme that sequences the four sleep stages into repeating cycles of roughly ninety minutes, four to six times per night.

It is not itself a state but an ordering, and it is catalogued here because the order is functional: the stages are arranged across the night on purpose, with deep sleep front-loaded and rapid-eye-movement (REM) back-loaded.

The organism uses this ordering to run its offline jobs in the right sequence, discharging the heaviest sleep-pressure repayment early, when deep sleep is available, and concentrating emotional and integrative processing later, in the REM-rich hours before waking.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The signature is a repeating trajectory rather than a fixed configuration: within each cycle the sleeper descends from N1 through N2 into N3, returns toward N2, and enters REM, then begins again.

Each cycle lasts about 90 minutes, with a normal range of roughly 70 to 120 minutes, and a full night contains four to six cycles.

The composition of successive cycles shifts systematically: N3 is greatest in the first one to two cycles and diminishes thereafter, while REM periods lengthen from under ten minutes in the first cycle to as much as forty minutes in the last.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The cycle is produced by the alternation of the sleep-wake and REM flip-flops (Part Three) under the shifting pressures of the two-process governor.

Within each cycle, the REM switch in the pons oscillates between REM-on and REM-off states, timing the return of REM roughly every ninety minutes, while the falling homeostatic sleep pressure (Process S) across the night reduces the depth and amount of N3 in successive cycles, biasing later cycles toward N2 and REM.

The interplay of the intra-night REM oscillator and the across-night discharge of sleep pressure is what gives the wheel both its turn and its weighting.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

As a scheduler rather than a state, the cycle's assignment is meta: it sequences the assignments of Chapters 5 through 8.

• It assigns which stage's downward broadcast is active at each point in the night, handing control among the N1, N2, N3, and REM configurations in order.

• It weights the sequence so that the N3 (deep-sleep) assignment dominates early cycles and the REM assignment dominates late cycles.

• It thereby schedules the layer-by-layer mode assignments of the individual stages into a purposeful nightly order rather than leaving them to occur at random.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The cycle does not have its own neuromodulator; it is expressed through the orderly rise and fall of the same channels the individual stages use, most visibly the roughly ninety-minute oscillation of the acetylcholine-to-noradrenaline balance that drives the alternation into and out of REM.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that this state or machinery serves a governing, protective, or metabolic role rather than a cognitive-computational role, and is tagged (NON-COG); the cognition runs in the individual stages, while the cycle only schedules their order.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The unit is the roughly ninety-minute cycle, and the programme is the whole sleep period of four to six such cycles across seven to nine hours.

The scheduling law is the front-loading of N3 and the back-loading of REM, which is why a shortened night preferentially loses REM-rich late sleep and why sleeping in cuts mostly into REM.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

The programme begins at sleep onset with the first descent through N1 and ends at final awakening, usually out of a late N2 or REM period.

Between cycles there are often brief arousals or lightenings, normal punctuation of the wheel rather than faults.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

The cycle rides on the night's endocrine arc: the growth-hormone pulse is tied to the early N3-heavy cycles, and the pre-dawn cortisol rise coincides with the later REM-heavy cycles, so the ordering of the stages and the ordering of the hormones are aligned.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Autonomic tone swings with the cycle, deep parasympathetic calm during the early N3-rich cycles giving way to the autonomic variability of the later REM-rich cycles, so the wheel is visible in the heart-rate record across the night.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Brain metabolism rises and falls with the cycle, lowest in the N3 troughs of early cycles and highest in the REM peaks of later cycles, so whole-night energy use follows the wheel's weighting.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The cycle is shorter and less clearly organised in infancy, when sleep often begins in REM and cycles are around fifty to sixty minutes, and it matures to the adult ninety-minute, non-REM-first pattern over childhood.

In older age cycles persist but carry less N3, flattening the early-night deep-sleep weighting.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Cyclic alternation of non-REM and REM sleep is a conserved mammalian feature, with cycle length scaling with body and brain size across species, and the ordered sequencing of consolidation stages appears to be an evolved feature rather than a byproduct.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Cycle length varies between individuals and species, and while ninety minutes is the human average, healthy cycles range widely, so advice to time waking to exact ninety-minute multiples overstates the precision of the wheel.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Disrupted cycling, such as the loss of the normal non-REM-to-REM progression or the intrusion of sleep-onset REM, appears in narcolepsy and in depression, where shortened REM latency (REM arriving too early in the night) is a recognised marker.

◦ Position of the sleep-wake and REM flip-flops; accumulated and discharging sleep pressure; circadian phase; time since sleep onset.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ The ordered sequence of stage assignments across the night, front-loaded for N3 and back-loaded for REM.

• STATE.

◦ Current cycle number; current stage within the cycle; remaining sleep pressure.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Cycle length about 90 minutes, range 70 to 120; four to six cycles per night; N3 fraction falling and REM fraction rising across successive cycles.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ One cycle about 90 minutes; the whole programme seven to nine hours; the REM oscillator period about 90 minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Disordered cycling and sleep-onset REM in narcolepsy; shortened REM latency in depression; loss of early-night N3 weighting in ageing.

CHAPTER 10. SLEEP INERTIA (THE COLD-START PENALTY) (NON-COG)

The alarm sounds and your eyes open, but you are not yet back, not really; for some minutes the world is thick and slow, the simplest decision is beyond you, and you would swear you could sink straight back under.

This grogginess is not weakness or laziness; it is a measurable transitional state, the cortex still running a sleep configuration while the world demands a waking one.

It is proof that the states of this layer do not switch instantly, that a brain, like a large engine, has a cold-start penalty.

Sleep Inertia names the phenomenon: inertia is the tendency of a system to resist a change in its state of motion, and here the sleeping configuration resists the sudden demand to be awake.

The Cold-Start Penalty names its engineering character: like a machine that runs poorly until it warms up, the just-woken brain performs poorly until it reaches operating configuration.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Sleep inertia is the transitional state of impaired alertness and cognition that immediately follows awakening, before the brain has fully reached its waking configuration.

The organism in sleep inertia is neither asleep nor fully awake: it is running a mixed configuration in which some systems have switched to waking while others, notably the executive prefrontal cortex, lag behind.

During it the organism performs poorly at demanding tasks, feels groggy and disoriented, and has a strong drive to return to sleep.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The signature is a temporary persistence of sleep-like features into waking: cerebral blood flow and metabolism in the anterior, executive regions of the brain remain below waking levels and take up to twenty to thirty minutes to recover, and slow-wave-like activity can briefly persist in the EEG (electroencephalogram, the scalp recording of the brain's summed electrical rhythms) after the eyes open.

Behaviourally, reaction time, decision-making, and memory are impaired, and in the first fifteen to thirty minutes the impairment can exceed that of mild alcohol intoxication.

The effect typically lasts about 15 to 30 minutes, with subtle deficits sometimes persisting up to two hours, and it is most severe when awakening occurs out of deep N3 slow-wave sleep.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

Sleep inertia is thrown by an abrupt flip of the sleep-wake switch to waking before the underlying neuromodulatory and metabolic state has caught up, so it is the visible cost of a fast switch acting on slow-recovering substrates.

Its severity is set by the prior sleep depth: awakening from high-delta-power N3 produces the worst inertia because the cortex must climb furthest, whereas awakening from the already-activated rapid-eye-movement (REM) or from light N2 produces less.

It is held by the lag in restoring aminergic arousal tone and anterior cerebral blood flow, and it resolves as those catch up to the demands of waking.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

Sleep inertia broadcasts a mixed, out-of-register assignment, part waking and part residual sleep.

• Layer 9 (regions): it leaves the prefrontal executive cortex in a still-partly-offline mode while primary sensory and motor regions have returned to waking, producing the executive-specific deficit.

• Layer 8 (microcircuit motifs): it allows residual slow-oscillation motifs to persist briefly in cortex after behavioural waking.

• Layer 7 (junctions): it holds sensory and associative synapses at a not-yet-restored waking gain during the warm-up.

• Layer 6 (cells): it leaves some thalamocortical cells in a residual burst-prone mode before they fully return to steady tonic relay.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The broadcast during sleep inertia is the incomplete recovery of the waking neuromodulators: aminergic tone (noradrenaline, serotonin, histamine) and orexin drive are rising but have not yet reached waking set-points, and this shortfall is the chemical reason the cortex runs a degraded configuration for the first tens of minutes.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that this state or machinery serves a governing, protective, or metabolic role rather than a cognitive-computational role, and is tagged (NON-COG); sleep inertia is a transitional penalty during which cognition is degraded rather than performed.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Sleep inertia is not scheduled by the organism; it is triggered by awakening and lasts about 15 to 30 minutes typically, with subtle effects up to two hours.

Its duration and severity are greater after awakening from deep N3, after sleep deprivation, and at the circadian low, and lesser after awakening from light sleep near the natural end of a cycle.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is the moment of awakening, whether spontaneous or forced.

Exit is the gradual return of anterior cerebral blood flow and aminergic tone to waking levels over roughly twenty to thirty minutes, hastened by light, activity, and caffeine and worsened by awakening from deep sleep.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Sleep inertia coincides with the cortisol awakening response, the sharp rise in cortisol in the first half hour after waking, which is part of the normal endocrine restart of waking rather than a cause of the grogginess.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The autonomic system shifts from parasympathetic sleep dominance toward sympathetic waking activation across the inertia period, with heart rate and blood pressure climbing back to waking levels as alertness is restored.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Cerebral metabolism and blood flow, especially in the prefrontal cortex, are still below waking levels at the moment of awakening and take tens of minutes to climb, and this metabolic lag is the direct substrate of the cognitive penalty.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Sleep inertia can be pronounced in adolescents and after the deep sleep of the early night, and its severity varies with age, sleep debt, and the depth of the sleep from which one wakes.

Severe, prolonged, or confusional awakenings are more common in children, in whom deep sleep is abundant.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

A warm-up cost on exiting a deep protective state is a plausible tradeoff for the benefits of deep sleep, and the persistence of sleep inertia across people and its link to deep-sleep depth suggest it is an intrinsic cost of the sleep architecture rather than a defect.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Individuals differ markedly in how badly and how long they experience sleep inertia, and it is worst when sleep is cut short and one is woken from deep sleep, which is the rationale, of limited precision, behind trying to time alarms to the light end of a cycle.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Severe, prolonged sleep inertia is a feature of idiopathic hypersomnia (sometimes called sleep drunkenness) and of disorders of arousal, and inertia-driven errors are a genuine safety hazard for shift workers and on-call staff woken to act immediately, such as medical and emergency personnel.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Depth of sleep at awakening (delta power); prior sleep debt; circadian phase; abruptness of the waking stimulus.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Degraded reaction time, decision-making, and memory; grogginess and disorientation; strong drive to resume sleep.

• STATE.

◦ Prefrontal cortex still partly offline; anterior cerebral blood flow below waking; aminergic tone rising but incomplete.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Typical duration 15 to 30 minutes; subtle deficits up to about 2 hours; worst after awakening from N3.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Anterior cerebral blood flow recovery about 20 to 30 minutes; peak impairment in the first 15 to 30 minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Severe prolonged inertia in idiopathic hypersomnia and disorders of arousal; inertia-driven errors in shift workers and on-call responders.

PART THREE. THE STATE MACHINERY

The states of Parts One and Two do not switch themselves.

Underneath the waking family and the nightly tour sits a small set of circuits and control laws whose whole job is to decide which state is running, to hold it steady against noise, and to schedule the next one - and this part catalogues that machinery.

Where a state is a configuration of the whole organism, a piece of machinery is a governor over configurations: a switch, a stabiliser, a rising counter, a clock, or a broadcast bus.

The recurring engineering idea, installed again and again at organism scale, is the flip-flop - the mutual-inhibition switch first catalogued as a motif in the Layer 8 catalogue (Chapter 36) - here wired between whole nuclei so that transitions are fast, complete, and bistable rather than slow and smeared.

None of these constructs computes, in the corpus sense; every one of them is tagged non-cognitive, because they are the operating system that decides when cognition runs, not the cognition itself.


CHAPTER 11. THE SLEEP-WAKE FLIP-FLOP (Reciprocal preoptic-arousal switch) (The Big Toggle) (NON-COG)

Somewhere below the front of the hypothalamus, a few thousand cells and the arousal nuclei they fight with hold the single most consequential switch in the body: awake, or asleep.

They do not fade between the two.

Like a wall light switch, the circuit is built to snap - to spend almost all of its time in one of two stable positions and almost none of it halfway.

Big names the reach: this one toggle reconfigures the entire nervous and endocrine system at once.

Toggle names the mechanism: two populations that inhibit each other, so that whichever is winning suppresses its rival and thereby wins harder.

Flip-flop names the family: the Layer 8 mutual-inhibition motif (Chapter 36), installed here between whole nuclei.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The sleep-wake flip-flop is the switching circuit that assigns the organism to either the waking states or the sleep states, built from mutual inhibition between the sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO, a small cell group at the front-underside of the hypothalamus) and the ascending arousal nuclei catalogued in the Layer 9 catalogue at section 2.4.4.

In operation the circuit does one thing: it keeps the organism decisively in one global state and moves it cleanly to the other when the governing drives demand it.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The signature of a healthy flip-flop is not a level but a shape: transitions between waking and sleep completed in seconds to a few minutes, and very little time spent in between.

Measured on the electroencephalogram (EEG, the scalp recording of brain electrical rhythms), the crossover from the fast low-voltage desynchronised waking trace to the slow high-voltage sleep trace is abrupt rather than gradual.

The VLPO fires roughly two to three times faster in sleep than in waking, while the arousal nuclei show the reciprocal pattern - fast in waking, near-silent in deep sleep - so that at any instant one side is loud and the other quiet.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The throwing is done by the mutual inhibition itself: the VLPO releases the inhibitory transmitters gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA, the brain's main inhibitory messenger) and the peptide galanin onto the arousal nuclei, and those nuclei release noradrenaline, serotonin, histamine, and acetylcholine that inhibit the VLPO in return.

Because each side suppresses the other, any small advantage runs away to a complete win - the defining behaviour of the Layer 8 flip-flop motif (Chapter 36) at whole-nucleus scale.

The holding is done by that same positive feedback, which is why the two states are stable steady points; the finger that biases the switch toward waking and prevents unwanted flips is the orexin system of Chapter 13.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it assigns the arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4 - the locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe, tuberomammillary nucleus, and basal forebrain - to either their tonic-firing wake mode or their silenced sleep mode, and gates the thalamus between relay and rhythm.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it sets the thalamocortical motif into either desynchronised transmission or synchronised oscillation, the switch that lets slow waves and spindles appear at all.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it sets synapses into a waking, neuromodulator-rich regime or a sleep, plasticity-renormalising regime.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it flips thalamic relay neurons between tonic (faithful relay) and burst firing, the single cellular change that most cleanly marks the crossing.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The state decision is announced by the arousal transmitters themselves - noradrenaline, serotonin, histamine, acetylcholine - rising together at wake onset and falling at sleep onset, on a timescale of seconds to minutes.

These are the same channels catalogued as one organ in Chapter 16; the flip-flop is their master switch.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that the flip-flop schedules cognition rather than running it; what runs is decided by which side of the switch is up, not by the switch.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The switch itself has no duration; it holds whichever state it last selected until the balance of drives crosses threshold, which in humans means roughly sixteen hours held in waking and eight in sleep across a day.

Its scheduling is set from outside, by Process S (Chapter 14) and Process C (Chapter 15).

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry to sleep occurs when accumulated sleep pressure plus the falling circadian arousal signal tip the VLPO into the lead; exit to waking occurs when discharged pressure plus the rising circadian signal tip the arousal side back.

Because of the runaway feedback, both crossings are fast and complete - the design goal of the circuit.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

The switch gates rather than secretes: throwing to sleep permits the nocturnal growth-hormone pulse and the melatonin rise, while throwing to waking permits the morning cortisol peak.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Waking biases the balance toward sympathetic (activating) tone; sleep biases it toward parasympathetic (calming) tone, with heart rate and blood pressure stepping down as the switch crosses.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The switch is metabolically cheap - a few thousand cells - but it governs the largest daily swing in whole-brain energy use, since the states it selects differ by roughly a fifth in cortical metabolic rate.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The consolidated two-state pattern matures across infancy as the circuit strengthens, giving the newborn's fragmented many-nap day and the adult's single long night; in old age the switch grows leakier, yielding lighter, more broken sleep and daytime dozing.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Mutual-inhibition state switches are ancient and appear across the animal kingdom wherever an organism must commit to one of two incompatible whole-body modes; the preoptic-arousal version is conserved across mammals.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Species differ mainly in how the switch is scheduled - one long human night versus the polyphasic naps of a rodent - rather than in the switch design; among humans, the sharpness of the crossing varies and blurs with sleep deprivation.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Damage to the VLPO side degrades sleep and fragments the night, as seen in the profound insomnia that can follow injury to the preoptic region and in the sleep loss of ageing; a switch with a weak stabiliser flips inappropriately, the theme of narcolepsy in Chapter 13.

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Sleep pressure signal from Process S (adenosine, Chapter 14); circadian arousal signal from Process C (Chapter 15); stabilising drive from orexin (Chapter 13).

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ A single binary state selection, wake or sleep, broadcast through the arousal transmitters of Chapter 16.

• STATE.

◦ Relative firing of the VLPO versus the arousal nuclei, effectively one variable near either extreme.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Mutual-inhibition gains; threshold offsets set by Process S and Process C; stabilising bias from orexin.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Transition seconds to a few minutes; state hold hours.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Insomnia when the sleep side cannot win or hold; pathological sleepiness and state intrusions when the wake side cannot hold, most starkly in narcolepsy.

In engineering terms this is the sleep_wake_toggle_state, a two-position governor on the global configuration bus that subscribes to the two-process scheduler and publishes a single binary mode selection to every subsystem.

The nearest engineering analogue is a hardware power-state controller that moves a machine between on and suspend, or a bistable latch holding one of two logic levels.

The biological difference is that the latch is built from cells that inhibit each other and is deliberately biased and de-biased over hours by slow chemical drives, rather than being switched instantly by a clean logic signal.

CHAPTER 12. THE REM SWITCH (Pontine REM-on/REM-off flip-flop) (The Dream Latch) (NON-COG)

Inside sleep there is a second switch, smaller and faster than the first, and it does something stranger: it turns the sleeping brain almost awake while leaving the body paralysed.

Rapid eye movement sleep (rapid-eye-movement (REM), the paradoxical stage of vivid dreaming) does not creep in; like the sleep-wake toggle above it, it is thrown by a flip-flop, so that a sleeper is either in REM or not in REM with little in between.

Dream names what the latch admits: the theatre of Part Two's REM state.

Latch names the mechanism: two pontine populations that hold each other off.

REM marks the stage it gates.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The REM switch is the flip-flop in the pons (the bulge of the brainstem) that gates entry into and exit from rapid eye movement sleep (REM), built from mutual inhibition between REM-on cells of the sublaterodorsal nucleus and REM-off cells of the ventrolateral periaqueductal grey and lateral pontine tegmentum.

In operation it makes REM a discrete episode with sharp on and off edges rather than a gradual deepening.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The signature is a package that arrives and leaves together: an awake-like fast low-voltage electroencephalogram (EEG), bursts of rapid eye movement, and near-complete loss of skeletal muscle tone (atonia) on the electromyogram (EMG, the recording of muscle electrical activity).

In a healthy adult REM occupies roughly twenty to twenty-five percent of the night, arriving in episodes that lengthen toward morning, the first often only a few minutes and the last twenty to forty.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The two pontine populations are mutually inhibitory GABAergic (gamma-aminobutyric acid-releasing) groups, so the circuit is a second instance of the Layer 8 flip-flop motif (Chapter 36), nested inside sleep.

It is biased by the aminergic and cholinergic tone catalogued in the arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4: falling noradrenaline and serotonin release the REM-on side, while acetylcholine promotes it, which is why REM appears when the aminergic broadcasts fall silent.

Holding is again by runaway mutual inhibition; the atonia is enforced downstream by REM-on glutamatergic cells driving inhibitory neurons in the medulla and spinal cord.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it silences the aminergic arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4 while sustaining cholinergic and cortical activation, and switches on the medullary atonia circuit.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it sets the thalamocortical motif back toward the desynchronised, wake-like transmission mode within sleep.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it opens an acetylcholine-rich, aminergic-poor synaptic regime distinctive to REM.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it drives spinal and cranial motor neurons into active inhibition, producing the paralysis, while cortical and hippocampal cells run near their waking regime.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The announcement is chemical inversion: noradrenaline and serotonin near zero, acetylcholine high, over the minutes-long span of an episode.

These are the same Chapter 16 channels, here set to a signature no waking or non-REM state ever shows.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that the switch gates REM rather than performing REM's computations, which belong to the REM state entry of Part Two; the latch only decides when they may run.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The switch fires roughly every ninety minutes across the ultradian cycle, biasing later cycles toward longer REM as the night's aminergic tone and sleep pressure fall.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry occurs when non-REM sleep has lowered aminergic tone enough to release the REM-on side; exit occurs when the REM-off side regains the upper hand or the sleeper wakes, both crossings sharp by the flip-flop's design.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

REM carries no signature hormone pulse of its own; it sits within the sleep-permitted endocrine window set by Chapter 11.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

REM is autonomically stormy: heart rate and breathing become irregular and thermoregulation is suspended, so the body neither shivers nor sweats to defend temperature during an episode.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Whole-brain metabolic rate in REM rises to near or above waking levels despite the paralysis, making it, energetically, the opposite of restful.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

REM dominates the newborn's sleep, taking around half of it, consistent with a role in wiring the developing brain, then falls to the adult fifth and declines gently into old age.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

A distinct REM-like state with atonia is found across mammals and birds, implying an ancient pontine switch; its universality argues that discrete REM serves a conserved function.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Species vary widely in total REM and episode length, but the pontine flip-flop architecture is conserved; within humans, REM proportion is fairly stable but rebounds strongly after deprivation.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

When the atonia arm of the switch fails, dreams are acted out - REM sleep behaviour disorder, an early marker of later neurodegeneration; when the switch intrudes into waking, its components appear out of place as the cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic hallucinations of narcolepsy (Chapter 13).

◦ A binary REM/not-REM selection, plus a motor-atonia command to the brainstem and spinal cord.

◦ Mutual-inhibition gains; aminergic and cholinergic bias set by the ultradian cycle.

◦ Episode minutes; inter-episode roughly ninety minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ REM sleep behaviour disorder when atonia fails; cataplexy and sleep paralysis when REM elements intrude on waking in narcolepsy.

CHAPTER 13. THE OREXIN STABILISER (Lateral hypothalamic orexin/hypocretin drive) (The Finger On The Switch) (NON-COG)

A flip-flop is fast but jittery; left alone, a switch built from mutual inhibition can chatter, flipping at the wrong moments.

The lateral hypothalamus solves this with a small population of peptide neurons that lean on the wake side of the switch and hold it there through the day - and when those cells die, the switch chatters, which is the disease called narcolepsy.

Finger names the role: not the switch, but the steady pressure that keeps it from flipping by accident.

Switch names what it stabilises: the sleep-wake flip-flop of Chapter 11 and, through it, the rapid-eye-movement (REM) latch of Chapter 12.

Stabiliser marks the function - stability, not switching.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The orexin stabiliser is the population of lateral-hypothalamic neurons releasing the wake-promoting peptides orexin-A and orexin-B (also called hypocretin-1 and hypocretin-2) that supply a tonic excitatory drive to the arousal nuclei, biasing the sleep-wake flip-flop toward stable, consolidated waking.

In operation it prevents inappropriate transitions - into sleep during the day, and into REM elements during waking - so that the organism holds each state cleanly instead of drifting.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The measurable signature is consolidation: long unbroken bouts of waking and of sleep rather than frequent short ones.

Orexin neurons fire during active waking, slow in quiet waking, and fall nearly silent in sleep, and the peptide can be measured in cerebrospinal fluid, where levels below about 110 picograms per millilitre are diagnostic of orexin-deficient narcolepsy.

The human orexin population is small, on the order of fifty to eighty thousand neurons.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

Orexin does not throw the switch; it holds it, by exciting the arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4 (locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe, tuberomammillary nucleus, basal forebrain) and, indirectly, by opposing the sleep-promoting ventrolateral preoptic (VLPO).

Its own tonic firing is sustained metabolically - lactate supplied by neighbouring astrocytes supports the steady discharge - and is itself gated by the circadian clock, so the stabiliser is strongest during the biological day, adding a bias to the flip-flop that widens the wake state's basin of stability.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it raises the tonic firing of the arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4, deepening their commitment to the wake mode.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it stiffens the sleep-wake flip-flop motif (Chapter 36), widening the wake attractor so noise cannot flip it.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it sustains the neuromodulator-rich synaptic regime of waking.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it holds arousal-nucleus neurons in steady tonic firing rather than letting them lapse toward sleep patterns.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Orexin is itself a broadcast peptide, one of the six systems considered as a single organ in Chapter 16, released steadily across the biological day and withdrawn in sleep.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that the stabiliser holds states steady rather than running computation within them.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The stabiliser runs across the whole waking day, ramping up under circadian control to counter rising sleep pressure and withdrawing at night to permit sleep - it is the finger that presses harder as the day wears on.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Orexin drive rises after morning waking and is sustained through the circadian wake-maintenance zone in the evening, when it counters peak sleep pressure, then falls at sleep onset.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

As a hypothalamic peptide system, orexin couples arousal to metabolic and stress state, integrating hunger and motivation into the wake drive, so that a fasting or threatened animal stays awake.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Orexin raises sympathetic tone alongside arousal, nudging heart rate, blood pressure, and metabolic rate upward during active waking.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The population is tiny but its steady firing is metabolically demanding, depending on astrocyte-supplied lactate; this dependence links the stabiliser to whole-body energy balance.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Orexin signalling matures across childhood as sleep consolidates into a single night, and orexin tone declines modestly with age, contributing to the more fragmented sleep and wake of later life.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The orexin system is conserved across vertebrates and ties feeding to arousal, a coupling that predates mammals; its role as a state stabiliser appears to be an elaboration on that ancient feeding-arousal link.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

All studied mammals with orexin loss show state instability resembling narcolepsy, and the dog and mouse models were how the human disease was solved; among humans, orexin tone and its resilience vary and shape how well waking is consolidated.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Selective loss of orexin neurons, in humans usually autoimmune and often triggered in genetically susceptible people, causes narcolepsy type 1 - the disease of unstable state boundaries, with fragmented sleep and wake, sleep attacks, and the intrusion of REM elements as cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and hallucinations.

CHAPTER 14. PROCESS S, THE HOMEOSTATIC SLEEP PRESSURE (Adenosine sleep-debt counter) (The Rising Bill) (NON-COG)

The switch of Chapter 11 has to be told when to flip, and half of that instruction is a simple running total: the longer you are awake, the more you owe.

This is Process S, a pressure that climbs hour by hour of waking and is paid down only by sleep - the body's sleep-debt counter, written chemically in the brain.

Rising names its behaviour: it only goes up while you are awake.

Bill names what it is: a debt that accumulates and must be settled.

Process S is the corpus's name, from the two-process model of sleep regulation.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Process S is the homeostatic component of sleep regulation: a sleep-promoting pressure that rises across waking and falls across sleep, one half of the two-process governor first formalised by Alexander Borbely in 1982.

Its chief chemical marker is adenosine, a by-product of the brain's energy use that accumulates during waking and promotes sleep; in operation Process S continuously reports how long the organism has been awake and how much recovery it is owed.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The measurable signature of Process S is slow-wave activity - the power of the slow (roughly 0.5 to 4 hertz) delta rhythm on the electroencephalogram (EEG) in deep sleep - which is high at the start of the night, after long waking, and declines as the night discharges the debt.

Process S rises and falls roughly exponentially: it climbs steeply early in waking and saturates, and after sleep loss the first recovery night shows markedly elevated slow-wave activity, the clearest quantitative fingerprint of the discharged debt.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

Process S is not a circuit but an accumulating signal, built from adenosine and other markers of metabolic and synaptic load rising in the waking brain; adenosine acts on its receptors to promote sleep, and caffeine works precisely by blocking those receptors and masking the debt.

It biases, but does not itself operate, the sleep-wake flip-flop of Chapter 11: as Process S climbs it shifts the switch's threshold toward the sleep side, and slow-wave sleep is what discharges it, closing the loop.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): high Process S lowers the arousal nuclei's ability to sustain the wake mode and deepens the drive to slow-wave sleep, where hippocampus-to-cortex replay is scheduled.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it raises the amplitude and prevalence of the cortical slow-oscillation motif once sleep is entered.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it tracks the accumulated synaptic weight that slow-wave sleep renormalises, in line with the synaptic-homeostasis view of sleep function.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it deepens the up-down alternation of cortical neurons that constitutes slow-wave activity.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Process S is announced by adenosine acting locally in basal forebrain and cortex, a slow chemical signal integrated over the whole waking period rather than a fast broadcast.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that Process S is a scheduling signal, not a state in which computation runs; what it schedules is the slow-wave sleep of Part Two.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Process S is the scheduler's accumulator: it integrates over the sixteen-or-so waking hours and discharges over the eight-or-so sleeping hours, setting how soon and how deeply sleep is needed.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

There is no transition to enter; Process S simply rises whenever the organism is awake and falls whenever it sleeps, most steeply in the deep sleep of the early night.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Process S is coupled to the growth-hormone pulse of early deep sleep, which rides on the same high slow-wave activity that marks the discharging debt.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

As Process S discharges in deep sleep, parasympathetic dominance is deepest and heart rate and blood pressure reach their nightly lows.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Process S is, in effect, a read-out of the metabolic cost of waking; the deep sleep that discharges it coincides with peak glymphatic clearance, when the brain flushes waste including the very metabolites that accumulated.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Slow-wave activity, the signature of Process S, is highest in childhood and adolescence and declines steeply with age, so older adults show a flatter debt curve and lighter deep sleep.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

A homeostatic sleep-debt process is found across the animal kingdom, even in the fruit fly and the roundworm, marking it as one of the most ancient features of sleep and evidence that sleep pays a genuine biological cost of waking.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

The exponential build-and-discharge shape is conserved across mammals, but the rate of build-up varies between individuals - fast builders need sleep sooner - and is partly heritable, tracked in humans through slow-wave-activity dynamics.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Chronic sleep restriction lets Process S run a standing debt with cumulative cognitive cost; when the discharge machinery of deep sleep fails, as in some insomnias and in ageing, the debt is poorly paid and daytime function suffers.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Time spent awake; brain metabolic and synaptic load, reported chemically by adenosine.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ A rising sleep-pressure scalar that biases the sleep-wake flip-flop (Chapter 11) toward sleep.

• STATE.

◦ Current sleep-debt level, read out as slow-wave activity in the next sleep.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Exponential build-up and decay time constants; adenosine-receptor sensitivity.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Build-up over hours of waking; discharge over hours of sleep, steepest early.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Standing sleep debt under chronic restriction; poor discharge in insomnia and ageing.

In engineering terms this is the sleep_pressure_accumulator, half of the two_process_scheduler, a monotonic counter that integrates uptime and publishes a pressure value that, with Process C (Chapter 15), sets the sleep-wake flip-flop's threshold.

The nearest engineering analogue is a wear-and-load counter or a battery-charge gauge that triggers a maintenance cycle once it crosses a threshold.

The biological difference is that the counter is a real accumulating metabolite whose discharge is not instantaneous reset but an active, hours-long maintenance state - slow-wave sleep - during which the brain is cleaned and its synapses rescaled.

CHAPTER 15. PROCESS C, THE CIRCADIAN DRIVE (Suprachiasmatic circadian arousal waveform) (The Day Wave) (NON-COG)

Sleep pressure alone would put you to bed a little later each hour of waking, monotonically; instead you feel most alert in the evening, just when your debt is highest.

That paradox is the second half of the governor: Process C, a twenty-four-hour wave of alerting drive produced by the brain's master clock, timed to the sun and set to oppose the rising debt.

Day names its period: one turn per twenty-four hours.

Wave names its shape: a smooth rise and fall, not a switch.

Process C is the corpus's name, the circadian half of the two-process model.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Process C is the circadian component of sleep regulation: a roughly twenty-four-hour waveform of arousal drive generated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the master clock in the hypothalamus, above the crossing of the optic nerves), opposing and shaping Process S as the other half of the two-process governor.

In operation it tells the organism what time it is, independent of how long it has been awake, and times the alerting signal so that waking stays consolidated across the day and sleep across the night.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The measurable signature is a smooth daily rhythm running through nearly every physiological variable: core body temperature falling to its trough in the late night and rising through the day, melatonin (the darkness hormone) rising in the evening and clearing by morning, and cortisol peaking near waking.

The clock's alerting drive is strongest in the evening wake-maintenance zone, the couple of hours before habitual bedtime when it is hardest to fall asleep, precisely because Process C is then countering a high Process S - a real, measurable opposition of the two forces.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The generator is the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a self-sustaining molecular clock of interlocking gene feedback loops that keeps near-twenty-four-hour time and is reset each day to the sun by light reaching it from the retina.

It does not throw the sleep-wake flip-flop directly but broadcasts a phase signal - through melatonin, through autonomic and temperature rhythms, and through the orexin system of Chapter 13 - that biases the switch's threshold on a slow, predictable daily schedule.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it sets the daily phase of the arousal nuclei of section 2.4.4 and gates the orexin stabiliser (Chapter 13), so wake drive peaks in the evening.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it entrains peripheral and central clock motifs, keeping tissue rhythms in phase.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it imposes daily rhythms on synaptic and plasticity thresholds.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it drives the clock-gene oscillation in cells throughout the body, the molecular basis of every tissue's local sense of time.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Process C is announced by melatonin (evening rise, morning fall), by the cortisol rhythm, and by the body-temperature and autonomic rhythms - slow signals with a twenty-four-hour period rather than the fast broadcasts of the arousal transmitters.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that Process C is a timing signal, not a state in which computation runs; it schedules when other states may run.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Process C is the scheduler's clock, cycling once per twenty-four hours and phase-locked to the light-dark cycle; it is the master schedule within which Process S and the flip-flops are booked.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

There is no entry or exit; Process C runs continuously, its phase advanced or delayed by light exposure, with morning light advancing the clock and evening light delaying it.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Process C is largely defined by endocrine rhythm: melatonin marks biological night, and the pre-waking cortisol rise prepares the body for the day, so the clock's output is in large part a hormonal schedule.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The clock drives daily rhythms in heart rate, blood pressure, and body temperature, with a nocturnal parasympathetic dip and a morning sympathetic rise that partly explains the dawn peak in cardiovascular events.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Process C schedules the body's metabolic day and night, aligning feeding, temperature, and hormone cycles so that maintenance work is timed to the right phase - misalignment, as in shift work, carries a real metabolic cost.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The circadian system matures over the first months of life as the newborn's rhythms consolidate to the day-night cycle, shifts later in adolescence (the teenage night-owl phase), and in old age tends to advance and flatten, giving earlier, lighter sleep.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Near-twenty-four-hour clocks are nearly universal in life, from cyanobacteria to mammals, an ancient adaptation to the rotating planet; the suprachiasmatic nucleus is the mammalian version of this deep inheritance.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

All mammals carry a suprachiasmatic clock, differing mainly in whether the animal is day-active or night-active; among humans, chronotype - the lark-to-owl axis - is a partly heritable difference in the clock's phase relative to the sun.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Misalignment between Process C and the imposed schedule underlies jet lag and shift-work disorder, and clock disorders such as delayed and advanced sleep-phase syndromes; the free-running rhythms of many totally blind people show what Process C does when it loses its light reset.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ A twenty-four-hour arousal-drive waveform and phase signal that shapes the sleep-wake flip-flop (Chapter 11) opposite to Process S.

• STATE.

◦ Clock phase; amplitude of the alerting waveform.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Intrinsic period near twenty-four hours; light-response (phase-shift) sensitivity; amplitude.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Twenty-four-hour cycle; re-entrainment over several days after a phase shift.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Jet lag, shift-work disorder, delayed or advanced sleep-phase disorders, free-running rhythm in blindness.

The nearest engineering analogue is a real-time clock or a cron daemon that triggers scheduled jobs by wall-clock time rather than by load.

The biological difference is that the clock is a molecular oscillator running in every cell, kept in step by a light-driven master in the hypothalamus, and its output opposes the load-based scheduler on purpose, so that alertness can be high exactly when the sleep debt is greatest.

CHAPTER 16. THE ASCENDING BROADCAST SYSTEM (The five neuromodulators plus orexin as one organ) (The State Announcement Service) (NON-COG)

A state decision is worthless if it stays in the switch; every cell in every catalogue has to be told.

The telling is done by a handful of small nuclei whose axons fan out over the entire brain, releasing chemicals that reconfigure whatever they touch - and considered together, these are one Layer 11 organ, the physical wiring of the corpus's recurring phrase, thrown from above.

Announcement names the job: to publish the state to the whole system.

Service names the design: a shared bus every subscriber listens to.

Ascending marks the direction: up from the brainstem and hypothalamus to all of cortex.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The ascending broadcast system is the set of diffusely projecting neuromodulatory nuclei - the noradrenaline of the locus coeruleus, the serotonin of the raphe, the acetylcholine of the brainstem and basal forebrain, the histamine of the tuberomammillary nucleus, the dopamine of the ventral tegmental area, plus the orexin of the lateral hypothalamus - considered as a single Layer 11 organ, the arousal nuclei catalogued at section 2.4.4 of the Layer 9 catalogue.

Its function is to announce the current global state to every cell in every catalogue, so that once the flip-flops decide, the decision is physically delivered everywhere at once.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

The signature is a chemical fingerprint per state: high noradrenaline, serotonin, and acetylcholine in waking; all aminergic tone low and acetylcholine falling in deep sleep; the paradoxical inversion of rapid-eye-movement (REM) (short for rapid eye movement, the dreaming stage), with aminergic tone near zero and acetylcholine high.

Each state thus has a distinctive neuromodulator profile that can be read off the mix, and it is the mix, not any single chemical, that names the state.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

This system is the broadcast, not the switch; it is driven by the flip-flops of Chapters 11 and 12 and stabilised by the orexin of Chapter 13, and it holds a state by sustaining the appropriate transmitter levels across the whole forebrain.

Its anatomy is built for reach: small numbers of neurons with enormously branched axons contacting most of the brain, so that a modest nucleus can retune the entire cortex within seconds.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it delivers the state to every catalogued region, setting each into its state-appropriate mode - for example, hippocampus toward ripple-replay in slow-wave sleep.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it sets thalamocortical and cortical motifs between desynchronised transmission and synchronised oscillation.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it opens or closes plasticity-permissive synaptic regimes, since acetylcholine and noradrenaline gate learning.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it flips thalamic relay cells between tonic and burst mode and sets cortical excitability - the same cellular assignments the flip-flops command, here physically carried.

The channels are the six transmitter systems themselves; this chapter is, precisely, the catalogue of the broadcast channels considered as one organ, operating on a seconds-to-minutes timescale as tone rises and falls with state.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Several broadcast nuclei sit at the interface with the endocrine system, and orexin in particular couples the broadcast to metabolic and stress hormones, so the announcement carries endocrine consequences as well as neural ones.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The same nuclei, especially the noradrenergic locus coeruleus, drive autonomic tone, so the broadcast simultaneously reconfigures pupils, heart, and gut in step with the brain.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The broadcast organ is a small fraction of the brain's neurons but sets the metabolic level of the whole cortex by choosing its state, making it a high-leverage, low-cost controller.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The neuromodulatory nuclei mature early and shape the development of the very circuits they later modulate; their gradual loss in ageing and in neurodegeneration - the locus coeruleus, for example, degenerates early in Alzheimer and Parkinson disease - weakens the broadcast and blurs the states.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Diffuse neuromodulatory broadcast is an ancient plan, present in the simplest nervous systems, where a few cells set global behavioural state; the mammalian system is a conserved elaboration of that basic broadcast architecture.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

The six-channel plan is conserved across mammals; individual variation in these systems underlies temperament and vulnerability to mood and arousal disorders, and is the target of many psychiatric drugs.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Because the broadcast sets global state, its disruption produces global failures: delirium, the acute confusional state of a mis-set broadcast often driven by illness or drugs; the arousal and mood disturbances of depression; and the state-boundary chaos that follows loss of one channel, as with orexin in narcolepsy.

PART FOUR. THE AROUSAL, STRESS, AND DEFENCE STATES

Everything catalogued so far assumed the world would wait its turn.

The five states of this part exist because it will not: a snapped twig, a lunging predator, a week of siege, an invading microbe, or an empty larder can each demand that the whole organism drop its schedule and reconfigure at once.

They are layered over waking rather than alternatives to it - emergency overlays that commandeer the broadcast channels of Part Three and re-tune every catalogue below within a heartbeat, a minute, or a season.

All five carry the (NON-COG) tag, and that is the part's lesson: the machinery that decides whether cognition may continue, and at what aperture, is not itself cognition.

For an engineering realisation, this part specifies the interrupt controller, the emergency power plan, the degraded-operations mode, the quarantine protocol, and the battery-versus-mains duty cycle.


CHAPTER 17. PHASIC AROUSAL (The Startle Spike) (NON-COG)

A door slams behind you, and before you have any idea what happened your pupils have widened, your heart has skipped, and every cortical area is momentarily listening at full gain.

No thought did that.

A cluster of some fifty thousand neurons in the brainstem fired one synchronised volley, and the whole machine turned toward the unknown in less time than a single heartbeat.

This is the shortest global state in the catalogue, and the most frequently entered - hundreds of times a day, usually without notice.

Startle names the trigger class: the sudden, the salient, the unexplained.

Spike names the shape: a sub-second burst riding on the waking baseline, gone almost before it arrives.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Phasic arousal is a transient, whole-system alerting burst, driven chiefly by a synchronised discharge of the locus coeruleus (the "blue spot" nucleus of the pons that is the brain's main source of noradrenaline, the alerting chemical messenger), layered on top of whatever waking state was running.

In it the organism does exactly one thing: it interrupts ongoing processing, orients toward the eliciting event, and resets the cortex to receive whatever comes next.

It is the biological interrupt request - the mechanism by which an unscheduled event pre-empts the scheduled one.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Locus coeruleus neurons fire a phasic burst of 2 to 3 spikes at short latency, within roughly 15 to 70 milliseconds of a salient stimulus, on top of a tonic waking rate of about 1 to 3 hertz.

The electroencephalogram (EEG, the recording of the brain's electrical waves from the scalp) desynchronises within about 40 milliseconds of locus coeruleus activation, shifting toward low-voltage fast activity, and event-related responses such as the P300 wave follow at about 300 milliseconds.

The pupil dilates by roughly 0.2 to 0.5 millimetres, beginning about 200 to 500 milliseconds after the event and peaking near 1 to 1.5 seconds - the standard peripheral readout of the burst, though an imperfect one, since pupil size tracks locus coeruleus firing only loosely moment to moment.

Heart rate shows a brief deceleration then acceleration of a few beats per minute, skin conductance rises within 1 to 3 seconds, and the startle blink itself, when the stimulus is intense, runs through a three-synapse brainstem reflex arc in 20 to 50 milliseconds.

The whole envelope is over within about 1 to 5 seconds, all values moderate confidence and stimulus-dependent.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The thrower is the locus coeruleus of the Layer 9 catalogue, driven at short latency by collaterals from the brainstem and midbrain salience pathways, including the superior and inferior colliculi and the nucleus paragigantocellularis.

Its burst mode versus tonic mode is the corpus's textbook example of a Layer 9 regional mode pair, and the adaptive-gain account of Aston-Jones and Cohen treats the phasic burst as the system's temporal filter for decision-relevant events.

There is no holding machinery worth the name: the burst is self-terminating, quenched within a second by the locus coeruleus's own noradrenaline acting on its inhibitory alpha-2 autoreceptors (self-sensing receptors that brake the cell that released the messenger) - a designed refractory period that prevents the interrupt line from chattering.

The basal forebrain acetylcholine system and the salience-detecting circuits of the amygdala co-fire when the event carries threat value, deepening the burst.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9, regional modes: locus coeruleus to phasic-burst mode; superior colliculus to orienting mode, driving gaze and head toward the event; amygdala to evaluation mode; cortex-wide shift toward stimulus-driven salience-network dominance for the duration of the burst.

• At Layer 8, motif modes: cortical microcircuits to high-gain, reset-and-listen mode - ongoing oscillations are phase-reset, winner-take-all motifs are momentarily flattened so no prior winner blocks the new input.

• At Layer 7, junctional regimes: noradrenaline transiently sharpens signal-to-noise at sensory relay synapses and opens a brief plasticity-permissive window, tagging the moment as worth remembering.

• At Layer 6, cellular modes: thalamic relay cells are pushed firmly from burst mode into tonic relay mode, ensuring faithful transmission of whatever arrives next; cortical interneuron networks are briefly disinhibited.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

One channel dominates: noradrenaline, released quasi-synchronously along the locus coeruleus's axons to essentially the entire forebrain, spinal cord, and cerebellum, acting within tens to hundreds of milliseconds and cleared within seconds.

Acetylcholine from the basal forebrain co-broadcasts on a similar timescale when attention is captured.

No hormone participates: the event is over before any gland could answer, which is precisely why the architecture keeps a purely neural fast channel.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Almost nothing, deliberately: the state's function is to stop what was running.

The jobs scheduled here are the network reset, the orienting movement, the snapshot evaluation of the stimulus (threat, opportunity, or noise), and the fork decision - return to the prior state, or escalate to the acute stress state of Chapter 18.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Sub-second to a few seconds per episode, unscheduled by definition, with a refractory period of a few hundred milliseconds enforced by the autoreceptor brake.

A person experiences from dozens to hundreds of phasic arousal events per waking day, more in noisy or novel environments.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is the fastest state transition in the catalogue, complete within about 100 milliseconds of the eliciting event, and requires no permission from any slower governor.

Exit is either decay back into the parent waking state within seconds, or escalation: if the evaluation returns "real threat," the sympathetic nervous system ignites and Chapter 18 begins.

Repeated identical triggers habituate - the same slammed door loses its power within a few presentations, an entry filter tuned by the salience circuits.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

This section does not apply to this state for the reason that the state begins and ends faster than any endocrine loop can act; it is the catalogue's one purely neural global state.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

A brief sympathetic volley - pupil dilation, a few-beat heart rate change, a skin conductance wave - with a transient vagal (parasympathetic, rest-and-digest branch) contribution producing the initial heart rate deceleration of orienting.

The autonomic ripple outlasts the neural burst by a few seconds, which is why a fright can be felt in the chest after the mind has already dismissed it.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Metabolic cost is negligible per episode - a transient rise in cortical energy use lasting seconds - but the state exists to protect the energy ledger, spending milliwatt-seconds on false alarms so that true alarms are never missed.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The startle apparatus is functional at birth, as the neonatal Moro reflex advertises, and habituation efficiency matures through childhood.

In ageing, tonic locus coeruleus activity tends to rise while phasic responsiveness flattens, degrading the interrupt's signal-to-noise; locus coeruleus integrity is now tracked as an early marker in Alzheimer's disease research.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

An orienting-and-alerting burst to sudden stimuli is documented across vertebrates, and analogous giant-fibre escape interrupts exist in fish, insects, and molluscs - among the oldest whole-organism reflexes known.

The mammalian refinement is the single-source noradrenaline broadcast, letting one tiny nucleus re-tune an enormous forebrain at once.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Startle magnitude and habituation rate vary severalfold between healthy individuals and are moderately heritable.

Trait anxiety raises the burst's amplitude and slows its habituation; sensation-seeking temperaments show the reverse.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

In post-traumatic stress disorder, exaggerated startle is a core diagnostic symptom - the interrupt fires too large and habituates too little.

Hyperekplexia, a rare glycine-receptor disorder, produces pathological startle with stiffening and falls; in anxiety disorders the escalation threshold from Chapter 17 into Chapter 18 is set too low.

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Salience-flagged sensory events from collicular and brainstem pathways, latency 15 to 70 milliseconds; threat evaluations from the amygdala; escalation vetoes from the prefrontal cortex.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ A forebrain-wide noradrenaline pulse; a network-reset command observable as EEG phase-resetting; an orienting motor command; an escalation flag toward the acute stress state.

• STATE.

◦ Locus coeruleus mode (tonic versus phasic); burst amplitude 1 to 3 spikes; refractory timer; per-stimulus habituation counters.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Tonic baseline 1 to 3 hertz; burst latency 15 to 70 milliseconds; pupil response 0.2 to 0.5 millimetres; habituation over roughly 3 to 10 repetitions.

◦ Neural burst under 100 milliseconds; EEG reset about 40 milliseconds; pupil peak 1 to 1.5 seconds; full envelope 1 to 5 seconds; autoreceptor refractory period a few hundred milliseconds.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Exaggerated startle and failed habituation in post-traumatic stress disorder; pathological startle in hyperekplexia; blunted phasic responses with raised tonic noise in ageing and early Alzheimer-spectrum locus coeruleus degeneration.

CHAPTER 18. THE ACUTE STRESS STATE (The Sympatho-Adrenal Surge) (NON-COG)

The evaluation came back "real threat," and now the body is being rebuilt in place.

Within seconds the heart is pounding, the airways are wide, the liver is pouring out sugar, and blood has been recalled from the gut and skin to the muscles that may have to save your life.

This is fight-or-flight proper, the state Walter Cannon named a century ago - not a feeling but a coherent whole-body configuration, thrown by a mass discharge of one half of the autonomic nervous system.

Sympatho-Adrenal names the two-part engine: the sympathetic nerves firing as one, and the adrenal medulla they command.

Surge names the dynamics: a flood, fast-rising, costly, and not built to last.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The acute stress state is the emergency mobilisation configuration, entered when a threat is judged real, in which the organism is optimised for one job: intense, immediate physical action - fighting, fleeing, or freezing in readiness.

The sympathetic nervous system (the accelerator branch of the autonomic, or involuntary, nervous system) discharges en masse, the adrenal medulla (the core of the adrenal gland atop each kidney) injects adrenaline into the blood, and a slower hormonal wave of cortisol follows to fund the effort.

Digestion, growth, repair, reproduction, and reflective cognition are all deferred, because none of them helps in the next sixty seconds.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Heart rate rises within 2 to 5 seconds and can reach 150 to 180 beats per minute; blood pressure climbs 20 to 60 millimetres of mercury; breathing quickens and deepens; pupils dilate maximally.

Plasma adrenaline rises within seconds and can reach 5 to 10 times baseline within 1 to 2 minutes, and noradrenaline overflows from sympathetic nerve endings on the same timescale.

Blood glucose rises as the liver breaks down glycogen (its stored sugar); blood flow is shunted from skin and gut toward skeletal muscle by up to severalfold; sweating begins; blood clotting factors rise.

Cortisol, the slower stress hormone, begins rising at 3 to 5 minutes and peaks at 15 to 30 minutes after stress onset, typically 2 to 4 times baseline for a strong laboratory stressor, moderate confidence.

The EEG (electroencephalogram, the scalp recording of brain waves) shows a fully desynchronised, high-vigilance waking pattern with elevated beta-band power.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The central command is the hypothalamus, chiefly its paraventricular nucleus, acting on brainstem sympathetic premotor centres such as the rostral ventrolateral medulla - the Layer 9 catalogue's autonomic master nodes.

The amygdala throws the switch when threat is confirmed, the locus coeruleus of Chapter 17 sustains a high-tonic noradrenaline broadcast, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis - hypothalamus to pituitary gland to adrenal cortex - launches the cortisol wave via corticotropin-releasing hormone and adrenocorticotropic hormone.

The state is held by ongoing threat input and by positive feedback between amygdala and noradrenaline, and it is actively ended by the medial prefrontal cortex signalling safety, by the vagal brake re-engaging, and by cortisol's own negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9, regional modes: locus coeruleus to high-tonic mode; amygdala to threat-processing dominance; hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus to secretory drive mode; prefrontal cortex partially taken offline, shifting control from deliberate to habitual circuits; hippocampus to encoding mode for the emotional event.

• At Layer 8, motif modes: salience and vigilance motifs locked on; central pattern generators for locomotion primed at readiness; deliberative recurrent loops suppressed in favour of fast feedforward reactions.

• At Layer 7, junctional regimes: noradrenaline and cortisol jointly open a strong plasticity-permissive regime in amygdala and hippocampus - the reason threat memories are written so deeply; neuromuscular junctions run at maximal fidelity.

• At Layer 6, cellular modes: sympathetic postganglionic neurons to mass-discharge mode; adrenal chromaffin cells to secretory burst mode; cardiac pacemaker cells accelerated by beta-adrenergic drive; hepatocytes (liver cells) switched to glycogen-breakdown mode; gut smooth muscle to quiescent mode.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Three nested channels, by speed: sympathetic nerve noradrenaline, wired and acting within 1 to 2 seconds; circulating adrenaline and noradrenaline from the adrenal medulla, blood-borne and acting within seconds to minutes with a plasma half-life of about 2 to 3 minutes; and cortisol, acting from minutes to hours, reaching every nucleated cell, and changing gene expression as well as fast signalling.

Central noradrenaline and corticotropin-releasing hormone broadcast the state inside the brain on the seconds timescale.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Emergency physical performance and its support jobs: maximal cardiovascular and respiratory output, fuel mobilisation, pain suppression through endogenous opioids, sharpened low-level perception, and rapid habit-based action selection.

One cognitive-adjacent job runs here as a side effect worth naming: privileged writing of the threat memory, which is why people remember accidents in unwanted detail.

Deferred: digestion, immunity's slow programmes, growth, reproduction, and reflective thought.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Seconds to roughly an hour; the sympathetic core decays within minutes of threat offset, while the cortisol tail takes 60 to 90 minutes to return to baseline.

Unscheduled and pre-emptive: it outranks every state in the catalogue except the deepest pathological ones, and will tear the organism out of any sleep stage.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is escalation from phasic arousal (Chapter 17) upon threat confirmation, complete within a few seconds.

Exit runs through a recovery ramp - vagal reactivation slowing the heart within seconds to minutes, catecholamine clearance in minutes, cortisol feedback over the following hour - often with a post-threat trembling and fatigue as the debt is paid.

If the stressor does not end, the state does not exit but transforms: the sustained stress state of Chapter 19.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Adrenaline and noradrenaline high from the adrenal medulla; cortisol rising via the HPA axis; glucagon up and insulin down, tipping metabolism to fuel release; antidiuretic hormone up, conserving water; growth, reproductive, and digestive hormone axes acutely suppressed.

Prolactin and endorphins rise with intense stressors.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The defining feature: near-maximal sympathetic outflow with vagal withdrawal - the accelerator floored and the brake released simultaneously.

Heart rate variability (the beat-to-beat variation that indexes the vagal brake) collapses, a standard laboratory readout of the state.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The most expensive state per minute in the catalogue: whole-body energy expenditure can rise severalfold, funded by liver glycogen and mobilised fat.

All maintenance is suspended - digestion, tissue repair, immune surveillance's slow work - which is affordable for minutes and ruinous for months, the ledger entry that Chapter 19 inherits.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Present and vigorous from birth; early-life stress exposure calibrates the amygdala and HPA set-points for decades, a well-documented developmental programming effect.

In ageing, the surge still fires but recovery slows, and the cardiovascular cost of each episode rises - acute emotional stress can precipitate takotsubo cardiomyopathy, the "broken-heart" stunning of the cardiac muscle, chiefly in older adults.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The fight-or-flight configuration is conserved across vertebrates, with catecholamine stress signalling older still - adrenaline-family molecules serve stress roles even in invertebrates.

Cannon described the pattern in 1915; Selye generalised it in the 1930s; the design has not changed since fish.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Individuals differ reliably in surge amplitude and recovery speed - cardiovascular reactivity is moderately heritable and predicts hypertension risk.

Trained responders (athletes, emergency personnel) show the same surge with faster recovery, the tell of a well-tuned governor rather than a weaker engine.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Panic disorder is this state firing without a threat - a false-positive interrupt escalation, complete with the full autonomic signature.

Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, stress-triggered arrhythmias, and phaeochromocytoma (an adrenaline-secreting adrenal tumour that throws the state chemically) mark its failure space, and post-traumatic stress disorder preserves its memory-writing side effect pathologically.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Threat confirmations from the amygdala; escalation flags from arousal_interrupt_state; safety signals from the medial prefrontal cortex; baroreceptor and metabolic feedback from the body.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Mass sympathetic outflow; adrenal medullary secretion commands; HPA axis launch; suppression orders to digestive, growth, and reproductive programmes.

• STATE.

◦ Sympathetic tone; vagal tone; plasma adrenaline, noradrenaline, and cortisol; hepatic glycogen remaining; threat-active flag.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Heart rate up to 150 to 180 beats per minute; adrenaline 5 to 10 times baseline; cortisol peak 15 to 30 minutes at 2 to 4 times baseline; blood pressure rise 20 to 60 millimetres of mercury.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Sympathetic onset 1 to 5 seconds; catecholamine half-life 2 to 3 minutes; cortisol rise 3 to 5 minutes, peak 15 to 30 minutes, recovery 60 to 90 minutes.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Panic disorder (false triggering); takotsubo cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia (surge damage); phaeochromocytoma (chemical hijack); chronic non-exit into the allostatic-load pathway of Chapter 19.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

The governor is acute_stress_governor, subscribing to threat_confirmed and safety_signal topics and publishing sympathetic_broadcast, adrenal_surge, and hpa_launch on the configuration bus while asserting a suspend flag over maintenance services.

The nearest engineering analogue is an uninterruptible-power-supply failover combined with a real-time priority override that pre-empts all background jobs; the biological difference is that the failover physically rebuilds the plant - redirecting coolant, opening fuel reserves, thickening the working fluid - and bills the cost to a repair budget that must be repaid later.

CHAPTER 19. THE SUSTAINED STRESS STATE (The Cortisol Plateau) (NON-COG)

The emergency did not end.

The threat is a mortgage, a war, a hospital ward, an exam season - nothing to fight and nowhere to run - and so the surge of Chapter 18 cannot discharge and cannot exit.

What remains when the adrenaline fades is a quieter, heavier configuration: the stress axis holding its output high, day after day, funding a siege instead of a sprint.

It is genuinely adaptive for days to weeks, and genuinely corrosive beyond them; this chapter records both faces honestly.

Cortisol names the currency: the adrenal cortex's glucocorticoid hormone, the long-loan financier of the stressed body.

Plateau names the shape: not a spike but a raised, flattened baseline that has forgotten how to come down.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The sustained stress state is the configuration in which the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis - the chain from hypothalamus, via corticotropin-releasing hormone, to the pituitary gland, via adrenocorticotropic hormone, to the adrenal cortex - holds glucocorticoid (cortisol) output elevated across hours to weeks.

In it the organism runs a long campaign: sustained fuel mobilisation, sustained vigilance, tightened emotional learning, and systematic borrowing against growth, repair, reproduction, and immunity.

The corpus adopts McEwen's frame: the state is allostasis - stability through change - and its unrelieved continuation is allostatic load, the accumulated wear of a protection system that cannot stand down.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Cortisol's normal daily rhythm - a morning peak of roughly 10 to 20 micrograms per decilitre in plasma and a late-evening trough below 5 - is elevated and, characteristically, flattened: the evening trough rises, the morning awakening response blunts, and total daily output climbs, low-to-moderate confidence on exact magnitudes since patterns vary by stressor and person.

Resting heart rate runs a few beats high with reduced heart rate variability; blood pressure trends up; blood glucose and abdominal fat deposition rise; sleep architecture shows lighter, more fragmented nights with reduced slow-wave sleep.

Inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein drift upward over weeks even as some immune functions are suppressed - the paradoxical immune signature of chronic glucocorticoid exposure.

Feedback sensitivity itself changes: the dexamethasone suppression test, which probes the axis's brake, shows blunted suppression in a substantial fraction of chronically stressed and depressed individuals.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The thrower is the same HPA cascade as Chapter 18; the holder is the failure of its brakes.

Cortisol normally shuts down its own production through glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus, hypothalamus, and pituitary; under sustained load these feedback sites desensitise - hippocampal receptors downregulate and hippocampal dendrites retract - so the brake weakens exactly where it is most needed, a genuine vicious-circle motif of the Layer 8 kind operating at organism scale.

The amygdala, in contrast, is potentiated by cortisol, driving the hypothalamus harder: one node's gain rises while the braking node's falls, and the plateau locks in.

Exit machinery is the same medial prefrontal safety signalling, plus the slow restoration of receptor populations once demand ends.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9, regional modes: hippocampus to a plasticity-restricted, atrophy-prone mode with suppressed adult neurogenesis; amygdala to hypertrophic threat-vigilance mode; prefrontal cortex to reduced-control mode with medial dendritic retraction; hypothalamic drive nuclei biased toward energy intake and away from reproduction.

• At Layer 8, motif modes: the HPA feedback loop itself re-tuned from negative-feedback regulation toward runaway; vigilance and rumination loops given standing priority; slow-oscillation sleep motifs curtailed.

• At Layer 7, junctional regimes: glucocorticoids shift hippocampal and prefrontal synapses toward a plasticity-suppressed regime while amygdala synapses stay plasticity-permissive - learning is not stopped but re-aimed at threat.

• At Layer 6, cellular modes: hepatocytes to gluconeogenesis mode (making new glucose from protein); muscle and lymphoid cells to catabolic, protein-yielding mode; adipocytes (fat cells), especially visceral, to storage mode; osteoblasts (bone-building cells) throttled; hippocampal pyramidal cells to dendritic-retraction mode.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Cortisol is the channel - blood-borne, reaching every nucleated cell, acting through receptors that are transcription factors, so the broadcast is read as changed gene expression over hours to days as well as faster signalling.

Two receptor classes give it two volumes: high-affinity mineralocorticoid receptors, mostly occupied at baseline, and low-affinity glucocorticoid receptors, recruited only at stress levels - the plateau is defined by chronic glucocorticoid-receptor occupation.

Sympathetic noradrenaline runs a raised tonic accompaniment, and corticotropin-releasing hormone broadcasts anxiety centrally.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

The campaign jobs: sustained glucose provision, sustained blood pressure support, sustained vigilance, and consolidation of threat-relevant learning.

What is progressively unscheduled matters more: slow-wave sleep's repair and clearance shifts, long-term immunity, bone and muscle building, reproductive cycling, and the reflective prefrontal supervision of behaviour.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Hours to weeks adaptively; months to years pathologically.

It is not scheduled but sustained - re-thrown daily by the persisting stressor, and superimposed on the circadian cortisol programme of Part Five, which it visibly deforms.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is the non-resolution of Chapter 18, or the accumulation of repeated daily surges without recovery intervals.

Exit, when the stressor lifts, takes days to weeks: rhythm amplitude recovers first, feedback sensitivity later, and hippocampal dendritic architecture over weeks to months - largely reversible in the animal literature, a fact worth stating with relief.

Failed exit shades into major depression, in which the plateau persists with no external stressor left to blame.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Cortisol chronically elevated with flattened rhythm; corticotropin-releasing hormone high centrally; growth hormone and thyroid axes suppressed; the reproductive axis suppressed at every level, from hypothalamic pulse generator to gonad; insulin resistance developing in the periphery.

This is the fullest endocrine reconfiguration of any state in Part Four, which is why it is the part's chief exporter of disease.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

A moderate but unrelenting sympathetic bias with reduced vagal tone - not the floored accelerator of Chapter 18 but a hand that never fully leaves it, measurable as chronically reduced heart rate variability and raised resting blood pressure.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The defining trade: energy is continuously diverted from maintenance to readiness.

Protein is stripped from muscle and bone for glucose; visceral fat accumulates as a rapid-access depot; repair, growth, and immune memory are chronically underfunded - allostatic load is precisely this deferred-maintenance debt, and it is payable in cardiovascular, metabolic, and psychiatric coin.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Early-life and prenatal exposure to this state programmes the axis for life, raising adult reactivity and disease risk - the developmental-origins literature's central finding.

In ageing, feedback weakens further, and the glucocorticoid-cascade hypothesis links cumulative exposure to hippocampal vulnerability and dementia risk, moderate confidence.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The glucocorticoid stress axis is conserved across vertebrates, where it manages famine, migration, rank conflict, and season - all genuine multi-week campaigns.

The mismatch argument is standard and fair: human psychosocial stressors hold the axis on for durations it never evolved to sustain, with no metabolic discharge through action.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Individuals differ widely in plateau amplitude and recovery, shaped by genetics (glucocorticoid-receptor and FKBP5 gene variants), early life, rank, and perceived control - Sapolsky's baboon field studies made subordinate rank's cortisol signature famous.

Perceived control and social support measurably lower the plateau for the same objective stressor.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Cushing's syndrome - pathological cortisol excess from tumour or treatment - is the state's chemical caricature, complete with visceral obesity, hypertension, diabetes, bone loss, depression, and hippocampal shrinkage.

Major depression frequently shows HPA overdrive; chronic stress load contributes to hypertension, type 2 diabetes, immune dysregulation, and accelerated brain ageing - the allostatic-load fault ledger in full.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Persisting threat and demand appraisals from amygdala and prefrontal circuits; cortisol feedback at hippocampus, hypothalamus, and pituitary; circadian phase from the master clock; energy status signals.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Chronic glucocorticoid broadcast to every tissue; suppression orders to growth, reproductive, and immune programmes; sleep architecture modifications; appetite and fat-deposition biases.

• STATE.

◦ Daily cortisol output and rhythm amplitude; feedback (brake) sensitivity; hippocampal and amygdala structural set-points; accumulated allostatic-load index.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Morning plasma cortisol 10 to 20 micrograms per decilitre, evening trough below 5, both shifted upward and flattened; dexamethasone suppression blunted in a substantial minority; heart rate variability chronically reduced.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Genomic hormone actions hours to days; receptor desensitisation days to weeks; dendritic remodelling weeks; recovery after stressor removal weeks to months.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Cushing's syndrome; major depression with HPA overdrive; hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and immune dysregulation as allostatic-load endpoints; hippocampal atrophy with memory impairment.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

The governor is sustained_stress_governor, subscribing to demand_appraisal and cortisol_feedback topics and publishing a standing glucocorticoid_broadcast plus maintenance_deferral orders on the configuration bus, with an allostatic_load counter exposed to the watchdog service.

The nearest engineering analogue is a system held in a degraded-operations mode that cannibalises its own maintenance windows to sustain throughput; the biological difference is that the brake sensor (the hippocampus) is itself corroded by the very signal it is supposed to limit, so the fault silently disables its own alarm.

CHAPTER 20. THE SICKNESS BEHAVIOUR STATE (The Infection Protocol) (NON-COG)

You know this state from the inside: the day the fever comes, and the most interesting book, the best company, and your favourite meal all turn to ash.

You want darkness, stillness, warmth, and solitude, and you want them with a motivational force that overrides ambition, appetite, and affection alike.

For a century medicine treated this as damage - the toxin's toll.

It is not; it is a programme, commanded by the immune system's messenger molecules, and the organism runs it because withdrawal, fever, and rest are how a body wins an infection.

Infection names the trigger; Protocol names the discovery: not a breakdown but an ordered, reversible, centrally coordinated procedure.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The sickness behaviour state is the whole-organism configuration commanded by pro-inflammatory cytokines - the immune system's signalling proteins, chiefly interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumour necrosis factor - when innate immunity detects infection.

In it the organism does one job: it fights the pathogen, and it reorganises everything else - temperature, appetite, sleep, movement, mood, and social behaviour - in service of that fight.

The corpus follows Dantzer and colleagues: sickness behaviour is a central motivational state, as genuine a global state as fear or hunger, and the clearest proof in the whole catalogue that immune signals sit at the Layer 11 state controls.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Core temperature is regulated upward by 1 to 3 degrees Celsius - fever, a raised set-point actively defended by shivering and warmth-seeking, not a control failure.

Locomotor activity, food intake, and social interaction all fall sharply; sleep changes are specific, with non-rapid-eye-movement (non-REM, the deep, slow-wave family of sleep) increased and rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep suppressed.

Circulating interleukin-6 can rise from under 5 picograms per millilitre to tens or hundreds; C-reactive protein follows over 24 to 48 hours; the HPA axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal, the cortisol axis of Chapter 19) is activated, and iron and zinc are deliberately withdrawn from the blood to starve bacteria.

In experimental humans given endotoxin (a bacterial wall fragment), the full signature - fatigue, lowered mood, social withdrawal, fever - appears within 1 to 3 hours and resolves within a day, high confidence.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The throw arrives by two routes from immune periphery to brain.

The fast neural route: cytokines excite vagus nerve afferents at the infection site, and cutting the vagus below the diaphragm abolishes much of the behavioural response in rodents - the founding experiment of the field.

The slow humoral route: blood-borne cytokines act at the circumventricular organs and choroid plexus (brain regions with a leaky blood-brain barrier) and induce prostaglandin E2 synthesis in the brain's blood vessel walls, which acts on the hypothalamic preoptic area to raise the temperature set-point.

The state is held by the brain's own microglia (its resident immune cells) re-expressing the same cytokines centrally for the infection's duration, and it is ended by anti-inflammatory resolution signalling - interleukin-10, glucocorticoid feedback, and the vagal anti-inflammatory reflex - once pathogen load falls.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9, regional modes: hypothalamic preoptic area to raised-set-point thermoregulation mode; hypothalamic feeding nuclei to anorexia mode; sleep-promoting circuits to enhanced non-REM drive; reward circuitry, including the ventral tegmental dopamine system, to anhedonic low-gain mode; HPA axis to activated mode.

• At Layer 8, motif modes: sleep-wake flip-flop biased toward sleep; shivering pattern generators enabled; foraging and social approach motifs disabled.

• At Layer 7, junctional regimes: cytokines shift hippocampal synapses to a plasticity-suppressed regime - interleukin-1 at sickness levels impairs long-term potentiation, the standing explanation for the fogginess of fever.

• At Layer 6, cellular modes: microglia to activated cytokine-expressing mode; hepatocytes to acute-phase-protein production mode; muscle to protein-release mode, funding antibody synthesis; brown fat and shivering muscle to heat-production mode.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The cytokines themselves - interleukin-1, interleukin-6, tumour necrosis factor - broadcasting in blood and, re-expressed by microglia, within the brain, over hours to days; prostaglandin E2 as the local hypothalamic fever messenger; the vagus nerve as the fast wired channel; cortisol as the accompanying endocrine broadcast.

This is the framework's neuroimmune channel operating as a first-class state announcement service, parallel to the aminergic bus of Part Three.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

The immune campaign and its logistics: fever (raising temperature impairs many pathogens and speeds immune kinetics), acute-phase protein synthesis by the liver, nutrient withdrawal from the blood, antibody production funded by muscle protein, and enhanced non-REM sleep.

Behavioural jobs run too, and they are the point: energy conservation through lethargy, avoidance of predation while weak, reduced foraging (which also withholds iron from pathogens), and social withdrawal that protects kin from contagion.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Hours to about a week or two for ordinary infections, tracking pathogen load; unscheduled, triggered by immune detection.

Its priority is high but negotiable: motivational studies show a sick animal will still suppress sickness behaviour temporarily for an emergency - the protocol yields to Chapter 18, then resumes.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry over 1 to 3 hours from immune detection - notably the slowest entry ramp in Part Four, set by cytokine synthesis rather than nerve conduction.

Exit follows pathogen clearance and resolution signalling over hours to days, with appetite and sociability returning before full energy.

The pathological non-exit is well documented: when inflammation persists, sickness behaviour can transition into genuine depression, the inflammation-depression bridge that has reshaped psychiatry.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

HPA axis activated, with cortisol serving as the protocol's own brake against runaway inflammation; growth and reproductive axes suppressed; leptin dynamics altered; insulin resistance transiently induced, sparing glucose for immune cells, which consume it prodigiously.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Mixed and purposeful: sympathetic drive to brown fat and cutaneous vessels serves fever generation and heat retention, while the vagus runs both the afferent alarm and the efferent cholinergic anti-inflammatory reflex, in which vagal signalling restrains macrophage cytokine release - a dedicated neuroimmune feedback loop.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Fever is expensive: metabolic rate rises roughly 10 to 13 percent per degree Celsius, while intake falls - the state deliberately runs a deficit, spending stored energy on immunity now to shorten the campaign.

Non-immune maintenance and growth are paused; the enhanced non-REM sleep is the one maintenance job promoted rather than deferred.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Functional from infancy, though neonatal fever regulation is immature; early-life immune activation programmes later neuroimmune reactivity in animal models.

In older adults the protocol misfires characteristically: fever is often blunted while the behavioural and cognitive components exaggerate - infection in the elderly commonly presents as confusion, the fragility of this state's ageing machinery.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Fever and sickness withdrawal are ancient beyond vertebrates: fish and reptiles seek warmth when infected (behavioural fever), and insects show infection-induced anorexia and withdrawal.

Hart's 1988 analysis established the adaptive reading - the costs are the point, an investment strategy conserved for hundreds of millions of years.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Cytokine responsiveness varies severalfold between individuals with genetic polymorphisms in interleukin genes; sex differences are consistent, with females mounting stronger inflammatory responses on average.

Prior chronic stress (Chapter 19) sensitises the neuroimmune machinery, coupling the two chapters' fault spaces.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Inflammation-associated depression is the flagship fault: interferon therapy for hepatitis induced major depression in a substantial fraction of patients, the natural experiment that proved the causal arrow.

Chronic fatigue conditions, the prolonged sickness-like states after some infections, delirium during systemic infection, and sepsis-associated encephalopathy mark the protocol's failure space; cachexia is its wasting extreme.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Vagal alarm minutes; cytokine induction 1 to 3 hours; acute-phase proteins 24 to 48 hours; typical episode 3 to 14 days; pathological persistence months.

CHAPTER 21. THE FED AND FASTED METABOLIC STATES (NON-COG)

Underneath every state in this catalogue, a slower alternation has been running since before you had a nervous system to notice it.

A few times a day the body switches between storing energy and spending its stores, and the switch is not a detail of digestion: it re-tunes hypothalamic drives, vagal traffic, hormone broadcasts, and even the circuits of memory.

Hunger that sharpens the mind before the hunt, and drowsiness that follows the feast, are not folklore; they are the felt surface of a genuine pair of whole-organism configurations.

Fed and Fasted name the two poles plainly; Metabolic States names their register: this is the duty cycle of the fuel system, the battery-versus-mains alternation underneath all cognition.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The fed (absorptive) state is the configuration led by insulin - the pancreatic hormone of plenty - in which the organism digests, absorbs, and stores: glucose burned and banked as glycogen, fat deposited, protein built.

The fasted (post-absorptive) state is the configuration led by glucagon (insulin's opposing pancreatic hormone) and ghrelin (the stomach's hunger hormone), in which stores are drawn down, the liver releases and then manufactures glucose, fat is mobilised, and the organism is motivated to forage.

Between meals the healthy human crosses this boundary several times a day; extend the fast beyond a day or two and a third configuration, ketosis, appears, in which the liver converts fat into ketone bodies that can fuel the brain.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Fed: blood glucose rises from a fasting 4 to 5.5 millimoles per litre toward 7 to 8 after a meal, peaking at 30 to 60 minutes; insulin rises 5 to 10 times baseline; ghrelin falls; leptin (the fat store's report hormone) reads out adiposity; vagal afferent traffic from the gut surges; a mild postprandial drowsiness follows large meals.

Fasted: glucose held near 4 to 5 millimoles per litre by glucagon; ghrelin rises in anticipatory peaks before habitual mealtimes; free fatty acids climb; growth hormone pulses enlarge.

Prolonged fast: ketone bodies rise from under 0.1 millimoles per litre toward 1 to 5 by day 3 to 7, by which point they can supply more than half the brain's fuel, high confidence for the metabolism, moderate for exact timing.

The gut's electrical signature flips too: fasting installs the migrating motor complex, a housekeeping contraction wave sweeping the gut every 90 to 120 minutes, abolished the moment food arrives.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The throw is distributed, a genuine oddity in this catalogue: pancreatic beta and alpha cells sense glucose directly and secrete insulin or glucagon with no neural instruction required, though vagal and sympathetic input tunes them.

The brain-side machinery is hypothalamic: the arcuate nucleus holds two opposed populations - AgRP/NPY neurons (agouti-related peptide and neuropeptide Y, the hunger drivers) and POMC neurons (pro-opiomelanocortin, the satiety side) - reading ghrelin, leptin, insulin, and glucose, and holding either the fasted-drive or fed-quiescent configuration; their mutual opposition is a Layer 8 flip-flop motif in hormonal clothing.

The brainstem's nucleus of the solitary tract aggregates vagal meal reports; meal-pattern scheduling is entrained by the circadian clock, which is why ghrelin peaks anticipate habitual mealtimes.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9, regional modes: arcuate nucleus to AgRP-dominant foraging drive (fasted) or POMC-dominant quiescence (fed); reward circuitry re-gained, with food cues commanding dopamine responses when fasted that vanish when fed; hippocampus and orbitofrontal cortex showing enhanced responses to food stimuli under ghrelin.

• At Layer 8, motif modes: the arcuate flip-flop set to the corresponding pole; the gut's migrating motor complex pattern generator enabled (fasted) or disabled (fed); foraging and food-seeking motifs prioritised when fasted.

• At Layer 7, junctional regimes: ghrelin shifts hippocampal synapses toward a plasticity-permissive regime, promoting long-term potentiation and dendritic spine formation - hungry animals learn food locations better; insulin supports hippocampal synaptic plasticity and local fuel use in the fed state.

• At Layer 6, cellular modes: hepatocytes to glycogen-synthesis mode (fed) versus glycogenolysis-then-gluconeogenesis mode (fasted); adipocytes to lipogenesis versus lipolysis mode; muscle fibres to glucose uptake versus fatty-acid-burning mode; AgRP neurons to high-firing mode when fasted, silenced within minutes of food detection.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

A layered hormonal bus: insulin and glucagon on the minutes timescale, announcing the current fuel direction; ghrelin rising over tens of minutes to hours as the hunger annunciator; leptin over hours to days as the long-term reserves report; ketone bodies as both fuel and signal in deep fasting.

The vagus carries the fast wired channel in both directions - meal reports up, secretory and motility commands down - and glucose itself is read directly by sensing neurons in hypothalamus and brainstem.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Fed: digestion, absorption, storage, protein synthesis, and the anabolic side of tissue maintenance; postprandial cognition dips mildly for demanding tasks after large meals.

Fasted: store mobilisation, the gut's housekeeping wave, sharpened food-cue salience, and enhanced spatial and food-related learning under ghrelin - the configuration evolution tuned for effective foraging.

Prolonged fasting adds cellular housekeeping: autophagy, the cell's recycling of damaged components, is promoted as insulin signalling falls.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The fed state runs roughly 3 to 5 hours per meal; ordinary daily life alternates the pair several times, and the overnight fast of 8 to 12 hours is the one deep fasted interval most humans still keep.

Scheduling is hybrid: need-driven in principle, but strongly circadian-entrained in practice, with the clock pre-positioning hormones before expected meals.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry to fed begins before swallowing: the cephalic phase - the sight and smell of food triggering vagally driven insulin and secretions - is the state pre-loading its own transition.

The fed-to-fasted crossing is gradual over 3 to 5 hours as absorption completes; the fasted-to-ketotic transition takes 1 to 3 days; refeeding reverses ketosis within hours, and after true starvation must be managed carefully - the refeeding syndrome is a dangerous state transition done too fast.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

The chapter's defining axis: insulin high with glucagon low (fed), reversed (fasted); ghrelin and leptin as the drive and reserves signals; incretin hormones (gut hormones such as glucagon-like peptide 1 that amplify insulin release when food arrives by mouth) active in the fed transition; growth hormone elevated and thyroid output trimmed in extended fasting - the endocrine pancreas, gut, and adipose tissue acting together as a distributed endocrine organ.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Fed is the vagal showcase: rest-and-digest parasympathetic dominance driving secretion, motility, and gut blood flow - the operational opposite of Chapter 18, which is why fear stops digestion.

Fasted runs a mild sympathetic bias that grows with fast depth, supporting fat mobilisation and glucose defence; hypoglycaemia, if reached, triggers a full sympatho-adrenal counter-regulatory response - Chapter 18's machinery borrowed for an internal emergency.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

This state pair is the energy ledger itself: fed banks, fasted spends, and the brain - roughly 20 percent of resting energy use on about 2 percent of body mass - is the protected creditor, its glucose supply defended by every hormone in the chapter.

Maintenance is split by pole: anabolic building runs fed, autophagic recycling runs fasted - a division of the maintenance schedule that intermittent-fasting research is still mapping.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The neonate alternates fed and fasted every few hours with small reserves and immature counter-regulation; adolescence tunes leptin signalling into the reproductive axis, which is why body energy status gates puberty.

In ageing, glucose tolerance declines and postprandial excursions enlarge; midlife metabolic health, including postprandial control, tracks later cognitive outcomes and hippocampal volume, moderate confidence.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The fed-fasted alternation predates nervous systems - single cells already switch between storage and mobilisation - and insulin-family signalling is conserved from worms to humans.

The mammalian addition is the brain-led anticipation: hunger circuits that sharpen cognition for the hunt, and cephalic responses that prepare the gut before the first bite.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Metabolic flexibility - the crispness of the switch between fuel sources - varies widely and degrades in obesity and type 2 diabetes, where the organism idles in a blurred, insulin-resistant middle state.

Meal-timing cultures, from grazing to one-meal patterns, set very different daily duty cycles on the same machinery; individual glycaemic responses to identical meals differ reproducibly.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus are the fed state's broken switch - absent or ignored insulin - leaving the body chemically fasted amid plenty; hypoglycaemia is the fasted state's guard-rail failure, with confusion, seizure, and coma as the brain's fuel fails.

Obesity involves leptin resistance, a reserves report the brain no longer believes; anorexia nervosa and cachexia hold the fasted configuration pathologically; the refeeding syndrome marks the transition's danger zone.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Blood glucose 4 to 8 millimoles per litre read by pancreatic and hypothalamic sensors; vagal meal reports; ghrelin, leptin, and insulin levels; circadian mealtime predictions.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Insulin-glucagon balance to all metabolic tissues; hunger and satiety drives; gut motility programmes including the migrating motor complex; plasticity biases to hippocampal circuits.

• STATE.

◦ Current pole (fed, fasted, ketotic); hepatic glycogen roughly 100 grams full; ketone level; arcuate flip-flop position; hours since last meal.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Fasting glucose 4 to 5.5 millimoles per litre, postprandial peak 7 to 8 at 30 to 60 minutes; insulin excursion 5 to 10 times baseline; ketones 1 to 5 millimoles per litre by day 3 to 7 of fasting.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Insulin action minutes; fed state 3 to 5 hours per meal; migrating motor complex cycle 90 to 120 minutes; glycogen depletion about 24 hours; ketotic transition 1 to 3 days.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Type 1 and type 2 diabetes; hypoglycaemic encephalopathy; obesity with leptin resistance; anorexia nervosa and cachexia; refeeding syndrome.

PART FIVE. THE CIRCADIAN AND INFRADIAN PROGRAMMES

The states of Parts One through Four are things the organism is in; the entries of Part Five are schedules it runs.

A programme is a state sequence with a period: the twenty-four-hour day, the roughly twenty-eight-day ovarian month, the roughly ninety-minute waking cycle.

Each books the other states into slots, so that the same brain runs alert waking at one clock phase and slides toward sleep at another, not by accident but on a timetable written into clock genes and hormone pulses.

These three programmes are catalogued as (NON-COG) because their role is scheduling and bodily regulation, the deciding of which cognition runs when, rather than cognition itself.


CHAPTER 22. THE CIRCADIAN DAY PROGRAMME AND NIGHT PROGRAMME (Twenty-Four-Hour Whole-Body Schedule) (The Master Timetable) (NON-COG)

Every cell you own keeps a clock, and once a day a small knot of neurons above the eyes' crossing point calls them all into step, so that liver, heart, muscle, and cortex agree on what hour it is.

The day programme is the body configured to be out in the world; the night programme is the body configured to repair itself in the dark.

The switch between them is not a light going off but a slow, hormone-carried handover that begins hours before you feel it.

Master names its rank: this is the schedule inside which every other state is booked.

Timetable names what it is: a fixed order of hormone phases, temperature, and alertness across twenty-four hours.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The circadian programme is the paired twenty-four-hour configuration of the whole nervous and endocrine system, where circadian means "about a day" (from the Latin circa, about, and dies, day).

In the day programme the organism forages, computes, moves, and eats; in the night programme it sleeps, consolidates, clears waste, secretes growth and repair hormones, and lowers its thermostat.

The programme does not itself perform cognition; it decides at which clock phase the cognitive states of Parts One and Two are allowed to run.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Core body temperature swings about 0.5 to 1.0 degrees Celsius, peaking in the early evening near 37.2 and reaching its lowest point (its nadir) roughly two hours before habitual waking, near 36.4.

Plasma melatonin, the darkness hormone, is near zero by day and crests in the middle of the sleep episode, roughly two hours before the temperature nadir; cortisol, the morning readiness hormone, crests four to six hours after the melatonin peak, around waking.

Subjective alertness and cognitive performance track close to the temperature curve, with a well-documented mid-afternoon dip and a "wake maintenance zone" of high alertness in the early evening; clock-gene messenger levels (the genes BMAL1, CLOCK, PER, and CRY) oscillate with a roughly twenty-four-hour period in nearly every tissue sampled.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The pacemaker is the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a paired cluster of about twenty thousand neurons in the hypothalamus, catalogued in the Layer 9 corpus, which runs a self-sustaining transcription-translation feedback loop of the clock genes with a period close to but not exactly twenty-four hours.

It is reset daily to the solar day by light captured by melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells and delivered down the retinohypothalamic tract; it holds phase by driving the pineal gland's melatonin rhythm and by projecting to the autonomic and neuroendocrine hypothalamus.

This is the Layer 8 pacemaker motif installed at whole-organism scale, with the suprachiasmatic nucleus as conductor and peripheral tissue clocks as the orchestra.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it sets the ventrolateral preoptic sleep nucleus and the ascending arousal nuclei to their day or night bias, gating the sleep-wake flip-flop of Part Three.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it phases the two-process governor by supplying Process C, the circadian arousal waveform that opposes accumulating sleep pressure.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it sets a plasticity-permissive window at night, when synaptic renormalisation and consolidation are scheduled, and a plasticity-and-encoding bias by day.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it shifts thalamic relay cells toward tonic (relay) firing by day and toward burst-capable readiness by night, and it phases metabolic and clock-gene state in peripheral cells everywhere.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

Melatonin is the principal night-announcing hormone, rising over one to two hours after "dim-light melatonin onset" in the evening; cortisol, released in a pulsatile rhythm peaking near waking, is the day-announcing hormone.

Core temperature itself acts as a slow broadcast, since many cellular clocks are temperature-compensated yet still entrained by the body's own thermal rhythm; autonomic tone shifts these signals over hours, not seconds.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

By day, online cognition and foraging; by night, the scheduled maintenance jobs of the sleep states, including systems consolidation, synaptic renormalisation, glymphatic clearance, and growth-hormone-driven tissue repair.

The programme's job is to keep these two workloads from colliding, running each at the clock phase where its supporting hormones and temperature are correct.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

The period is close to 24.2 hours in humans when free-running without time cues, corrected daily to 24.0 by light; the day and night programmes each occupy roughly half the cycle, shifted by season and latitude.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry into the night programme begins with dim-light melatonin onset in the early evening and completes over a few hours as temperature falls; entry into the day programme is led by the pre-waking cortisol rise.

Rapid phase shifts, as in jet travel or shift work, desynchronise the peripheral clocks from the suprachiasmatic nucleus and from each other, the state of jet lag.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Melatonin high at night, cortisol high near waking, growth hormone pulsed in early deep sleep, thyroid-stimulating hormone peaking before sleep onset, and appetite hormones (leptin and ghrelin) phased across the cycle.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) tone dominates the night programme, lowering heart rate and blood pressure into a nocturnal "dip"; sympathetic tone rises across the morning transition.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The programme concentrates energetically costly repair and clearance in the night phase, when the organism is not spending on locomotion and sensing, an economical division of the day into earning and housekeeping shifts.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The rhythm is weak and unconsolidated in newborns, matures over the first months of life, is phase-delayed in adolescence, and flattens and advances in old age, when melatonin amplitude falls.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Circadian clocks are ancient, present from cyanobacteria to mammals, an adaptation to the single most predictable feature of the planet, its rotation; the transcription-translation loop is a deeply conserved solution.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Individual chronotype ("morningness" versus "eveningness") varies with clock-gene alleles, so that intrinsic period and preferred phase differ by an hour or more between people.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders (delayed and advanced sleep phase, non-24-hour rhythm in some blind people, shift-work disorder), and the elevated metabolic, cardiovascular, and mood risks associated with chronic circadian misalignment.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Retinal light (melanopsin) 0 to thousands of lux; feeding, activity, and temperature cues as weaker entrainers.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Melatonin rhythm, cortisol phase, core temperature waveform, and the circadian arousal drive (Process C).

• STATE.

◦ Internal clock phase (0 to 24 hours), amplitude, and the degree of central-peripheral synchrony.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Free-running period near 24.2 hours; temperature swing 0.5 to 1.0 degrees Celsius; melatonin-to-temperature-nadir lag about 2 hours.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Daily light correction; phase shifts of about 1 hour per day maximum with bright light; full re-entrainment after a large shift over several days.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Circadian misalignment in shift work and jet lag; delayed and advanced sleep-phase disorders; the flattened, advanced rhythm of ageing and dementia.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

In engineering terms this is the master_schedule_governor, or two_process_scheduler when paired with sleep pressure: it subscribes to a light-sensor input and publishes a phase signal on the global configuration bus that every other scheduler reads.

The nearest engineering analogue is the system real-time clock plus cron, the always-running timekeeper that fires scheduled jobs at set hours; the biological difference is that this clock is distributed into every cell and is itself entrained by the environment rather than set once by an operator.

WHAT MAKES IT UNMISTAKABLE:

A single twenty-thousand-neuron clock above the optic chiasm that, by melatonin at night and cortisol at dawn, holds every tissue in the body to the same twenty-four-hour timetable, so that temperature, hormones, and alertness rise and fall together on schedule.


CHAPTER 23. THE MENSTRUAL (OVARIAN) CYCLE PROGRAMME (Roughly Monthly Whole-System Programme) (The Monthly Tide) (NON-COG)

Layered on top of the daily clock, in half the adult population for much of adult life, runs a slower wheel with a period near a month, driven not from the brain's clock but from a two-way conversation between brain and ovary.

It reconfigures temperature, sleep architecture, autonomic tone, and mood across its phases, a genuine whole-system programme rather than a purely reproductive event.

Monthly names its period; tide names its slow, hormone-carried rise and ebb across weeks.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The menstrual or ovarian cycle programme is the roughly twenty-eight-day (range about 21 to 35 days) reconfiguration of the neuroendocrine system across the follicular and luteal phases, named for the ovarian structures that dominate each.

Its overt purpose is reproduction, but its state-space relevance is that its two hormonal climates measurably retune thermoregulation, sleep, autonomic balance, and affect; it is the corpus's clearest infradian (longer-than-a-day) programme.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

In the early follicular phase both oestradiol and progesterone are low; oestradiol rises to a pre-ovulatory peak near mid-cycle; after ovulation the mid-luteal phase carries high progesterone with a secondary oestradiol peak.

Core body temperature rises 0.3 to 0.7 degrees Celsius in the luteal phase under progesterone; sleep electroencephalogram (EEG) spindle activity increases prominently in the luteal phase, with fast-spindle density correlating positively with progesterone, while objective sleep continuity stays largely unchanged across an asymptomatic cycle.

Reported cognitive-affective modulation is real but modest and variable, with some evidence of better mental-rotation performance in the early follicular phase and elevated daytime sleepiness in the high-progesterone luteal phase.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The programme is run by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulses from the hypothalamus drive pituitary luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone, which drive ovarian oestradiol and progesterone, which feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary, switching from negative to positive feedback to trigger the mid-cycle luteinising-hormone surge.

The kisspeptin neurons of the hypothalamus, catalogued in the Layer 9 corpus, are the switchboard that reads oestradiol and times the surge; the ovarian corpus luteum holds the luteal hormonal climate for about two weeks unless pregnancy intervenes.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): oestradiol and progesterone tune the hippocampus, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex, shifting resting-state connectivity across phases.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): luteal progesterone, via its neurosteroid metabolite allopregnanolone, up-regulates the sleep-spindle-generating thalamreticular motif, raising spindle density.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): the sex steroids are plasticity modulators, oestradiol generally raising dendritic-spine density and excitatory tone, progesterone's metabolite raising inhibitory (gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-A receptor) tone.

• At Layer 6 (cells): allopregnanolone acts as a positive modulator at the GABA-A receptor on neurons body-wide, biasing them toward inhibition, the same target family as anaesthetics and anxiolytics.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The announcing signals are the ovarian steroids oestradiol and progesterone in the bloodstream, changing over days, with the luteal neurosteroid allopregnanolone as a key central messenger.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

No distinct cognition runs "in" the programme; rather, the ongoing cognition of the waking and sleep states is modulated across phases, with luteal sleep carrying denser spindles and follicular waking associated in some studies with particular performance profiles.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Period roughly 28 days (21 to 35), with a relatively fixed luteal phase near 14 days and a variable follicular phase; nested inside the daily circadian programme.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

The follicular-to-luteal transition is triggered by ovulation following the luteinising-hormone surge; the luteal-to-follicular transition (menstruation) follows the fall of progesterone as the corpus luteum regresses.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Follicular: rising oestradiol, low progesterone; peri-ovulatory: oestradiol peak and luteinising-hormone surge; luteal: high progesterone and secondary oestradiol, both falling before menstruation.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The luteal phase carries a modest shift toward sympathetic dominance and reduced heart-rate variability relative to the follicular phase, alongside the progesterone-driven rise in core temperature.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Basal metabolic rate rises modestly in the luteal phase, consistent with the higher temperature set-point; the programme is metabolically inexpensive relative to the states it modulates.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The programme begins at menarche in adolescence, runs through the reproductive years (interrupted by pregnancy and lactation), and ends at menopause, whose hormonal withdrawal itself reconfigures sleep and thermoregulation.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis is a conserved vertebrate reproductive controller; overt menstruation is restricted to humans and a few other species, but the underlying ovarian cycle is broadly mammalian.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Cycle length, symptom burden, and cognitive-affective sensitivity vary widely between individuals; exogenous hormonal contraceptives override the endogenous programme with a synthetic hormonal climate.

CHAPTER 24. THE BASIC REST-ACTIVITY CYCLE (Roughly Ninety-Minute Waking Ultradian Programme) (The Daytime Echo) (NON-COG)

The night runs in ninety-minute rounds; the question this chapter poses is whether the day does too.

Nathaniel Kleitman, who mapped the sleep cycle, proposed in 1963 that the same ninety-minute wheel keeps turning while we are awake, tilting us every hour and a half from outward, focused activity toward inward, recovery-tilted rest.

The evidence is real but contested, and this entry is admitted with a low-confidence note, honestly flagged.

Daytime names where it is claimed to run; echo names its status as the sleep cycle's uncertain reflection.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

The basic rest-activity cycle (BRAC) is the proposed roughly ninety-minute ultradian (shorter-than-a-day) waxing and waning of arousal and performance across the waking day, the waking counterpart of the nocturnal non-REM/rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep cycle.

If real as an independent rhythm, the organism alternates every ninety minutes or so between a higher-arousal, outward, task-oriented tilt and a lower-arousal, inward, recovery tilt; it performs no dedicated computation, only modulating the states already running.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Reported correlates include roughly ninety-minute oscillations in alertness, reaction time, fantasy and daydreaming, spontaneous rest breaks, gastric motility, and the nasal cycle (alternating airflow dominance between nostrils), plus fluctuations in autonomic measures such as heart-rate variability.

The amplitudes are small, the periods scatter widely between 70 and 120 minutes, and the signal is easily masked by meals, activity, and the stronger circadian curve, which is why the rhythm's independent existence during waking remains disputed.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

No single pacemaker for a waking BRAC has been confirmed; the leading candidate is the same brainstem oscillatory machinery that times the non-REM/REM alternation in sleep, the reciprocal aminergic-cholinergic circuits of the pons catalogued in the Layer 9 corpus, hypothesised to keep running at low amplitude by day.

Because no dedicated governor has been isolated, the entry is held with explicit low confidence, and some investigators argue that no basic rest-activity cycle exists outside sleep at all.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it is proposed to impose a slow gain oscillation on the ascending arousal nuclei, nudging cortical arousal up and down.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): if present, it is an ultradian oscillator motif running at low amplitude through waking.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): any effect is a mild, cyclic modulation of signal-to-noise, not a scheduled plasticity window.

• At Layer 6 (cells): a small periodic drift in the tonic firing rate of arousal-sensitive neurons, near the threshold of detectability.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

No distinct hormonal announcement is established; proposed correlates are low-amplitude cyclic shifts in noradrenergic and cholinergic tone and in autonomic balance, over tens of minutes.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Nothing is scheduled exclusively in it; the claim is only that ongoing waking cognition rises and falls in efficiency on a roughly ninety-minute beat, which, if real, would argue for pacing sustained work in ultradian blocks with recovery breaks.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Proposed period roughly 90 minutes (observed 70 to 120), running through the waking portion of the circadian day programme; nested inside the circadian cycle and, at night, becoming the well-established sleep cycle.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

No discrete transitions; the rhythm, if present, is continuous and sinusoidal rather than switched, which is part of why it is hard to demonstrate.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

No robust endocrine signature is established for the waking cycle; some ultradian pulsatility in cortisol and other hormones exists but is not clearly the same rhythm.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

The nasal cycle and cyclic heart-rate-variability fluctuations are the most cited autonomic correlates, consistent with a slow oscillation in sympathetic-parasympathetic balance.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

If real, the cycle's recovery phase would offer brief, cheap restorative pauses within the working day; no distinct metabolic cost is attributed to it.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

The ultradian period lengthens from infancy (where feed-sleep cycles are short) into adulthood, mirroring the maturation of the sleep cycle; waking-day evidence across the lifespan is sparse.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

Ultradian rhythms are widespread in mammals and are more prominent in small animals and infants; the human waking BRAC, if it exists, would be a low-amplitude remnant of a stronger ancestral rhythm.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Reported periods and amplitudes vary greatly between individuals and studies, one reason the phenomenon resists firm confirmation.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

No disorder is specifically defined by BRAC disruption; disturbed ultradian structure is described within sleep disorders rather than as a waking pathology.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Proposed brainstem oscillator drive; masking inputs from meals, activity, and the circadian arousal curve.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ A low-amplitude, roughly ninety-minute arousal and performance oscillation (existence disputed).

• STATE.

◦ Phase within the ultradian cycle (if present); amplitude near the noise floor.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Period roughly 90 minutes (70 to 120); amplitude small and inconsistent.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Tens of minutes per half-cycle; no fast transitions.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Not defined; the principal "failure" here is of the hypothesis itself, whose independent existence in waking is not firmly established.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

In engineering terms this would be the waking_ultradian_pacer, a low-priority background oscillator subscribing to brainstem timing and publishing a weak arousal-bias signal on the global configuration bus, flagged experimental.

The nearest engineering analogue is a low-amplitude background duty-cycle or periodic micro-idle; the biological difference, and the honest caveat, is that unlike the well-instrumented sleep cycle this daytime process may not be a real, separable component at all.

PART SIX. THE ENGINEERED AND BOUNDARY STATES

The healthy system does not schedule the two entries that follow; they are catalogued because a map of the state space is incomplete without its edges.

One is imposed on purpose by drugs to abolish consciousness for surgery; the other is imposed by injury when the state machinery itself is broken.

Each is the most informative possible probe of the normal states, showing what has to be present for consciousness to run and what its absence looks like.

Both are (NON-COG), and both are handled with the honest framing the outline gives them: an engineered off-switch and a fault space, not natural states of the register.


CHAPTER 25. GENERAL ANAESTHESIA (Pharmacologically Imposed Reversible Unconsciousness) (The Engineered Off-Switch) (NON-COG)

Every day, in operating theatres worldwide, a controlled dose of drug reaches into the brain and switches consciousness off, holds it off for hours, and then lets it come back with the person intact.

This is not a state evolution built; it is one medicine engineered, and precisely because it is engineered it has become the single most revealing probe of what the natural states of consciousness are made of.

Engineered names its origin; off-switch names its function, a deliberate, reversible abolition of the conscious configuration.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

General anaesthesia is a drug-induced, reversible state family combining unconsciousness, amnesia (no memory formation), analgesia (no pain), and immobility, produced by agents such as propofol, the volatile ethers (for example sevoflurane), and others.

In it the organism does nothing cognitively; it is held unconscious and unresponsive while surgery proceeds, its vital functions supported or monitored by the anaesthetist.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Propofol and the ethers, acting largely at the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-A receptor (the brain's main inhibitory receptor), first produce paradoxical excitation, then a hallmark strong frontal alpha rhythm (about 8 to 12 cycles per second) with slow-delta activity, a pattern called "anteriorisation" that reflects altered thalamocortical communication.

At deeper levels the electroencephalogram (EEG) shows burst suppression, an alternation of high-amplitude bursts with near-flat suppression periods; deeper still it can reach an isoelectric (flat) trace, and throughout, measures of cortical information integration and long-range communication collapse.

Autonomic and respiratory drive are depressed and often supported mechanically; temperature regulation is impaired, requiring active warming.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The state is thrown by the drug, not by the brain's own switches: GABA-A agonists hyperpolarise cortical and thalamic neurons, and the resulting thalamocortical disconnection removes the substrate on which the arousal nuclei of the Layer 9 corpus would otherwise act.

Propofol specifically disrupts the posterior alpha network tied to sensory thalamic nuclei while imposing a coherent frontal alpha tied to cognitive thalamic nuclei, dismantling both sensory relay and cortical integration; the depth is held by continuous infusion or inspired concentration and titrated to EEG and clinical signs.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): it silences effective thalamocortical relay and decouples cortical regions, abolishing the integrated broadcast that waking requires.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): it forces a pathological thalamocortical oscillation (frontal alpha, then burst suppression) unlike any natural state's rhythm.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): it drives inhibitory (GABA-A) synapses into sustained dominance, closing the door on normal signalling.

• At Layer 6 (cells): it hyperpolarises neurons via GABA-A chloride channels and, at depth, imposes the all-or-none bursting of burst suppression on the whole cortex.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The "broadcast" here is pharmacological, the blood and brain concentration of the anaesthetic itself, rising and falling over minutes; it substitutes a drug for the neuromodulatory announcements of natural states.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

Nothing cognitive runs; this is precisely the point of the state, and its scientific value is negative, showing by subtraction what consciousness needs by removing it cleanly and reversibly.

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Minutes to many hours, scheduled entirely by the clinician to match the surgery, not by any internal governor.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Induction takes seconds to a couple of minutes; emergence follows drug clearance over minutes to tens of minutes and is not simply the reverse of induction, sometimes passing through distinct reactivation stages, occasionally with post-operative delirium.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Anaesthesia blunts but does not abolish the surgical stress hormone response; cortisol and catecholamines can still rise with surgical stimulation unless adequately suppressed.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Sympathetic and parasympathetic reflexes are depressed; blood pressure, heart rate, and breathing are commonly supported, since the drug removes the brain's own autonomic governance.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

The GABAergic anaesthetics lower the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen; burst suppression represents a deeply metabolically downregulated cortex, which is one reason it is used therapeutically to protect the brain.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Dose requirements and risks vary with age; concern exists about anaesthetic effects on the developing brain in the very young and about post-operative cognitive dysfunction and delirium in the elderly.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

None in the ordinary sense; general anaesthesia is a nineteenth-century medical invention, not an evolved state, though it exploits an ancient, conserved receptor (GABA-A).

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Anaesthetic sensitivity varies with genetics, age, body composition, and drug interactions; the EEG signature also differs between agents, for example ketamine produces a very different, high-frequency pattern.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

Intraoperative awareness (rare failure of the off-switch), anaphylaxis, malignant hyperthermia, delayed emergence, and post-operative delirium and cognitive dysfunction.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ Anaesthetic drug concentration (for example propofol effect-site micrograms per millilitre); surgical stimulation.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Unconsciousness, amnesia, immobility; EEG signature (frontal alpha, slow-delta, burst suppression at depth).

• STATE.

◦ Anaesthetic depth (light to burst suppression to isoelectric), monitored by processed EEG indices.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Frontal alpha 8 to 12 cycles per second; burst suppression ratio 0 to 1; drug concentration as the control knob.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Induction seconds to about 2 minutes; emergence minutes to tens of minutes as the drug clears.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ Intraoperative awareness; over-deep anaesthesia and prolonged burst suppression associated with post-operative delirium; cardiorespiratory depression.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

In engineering terms this is the engineered_forced_halt, an externally commanded power-down that does not go through the normal sleep-wake scheduler but clamps the hardware directly, publishing a "halted" flag on the global configuration bus.

The nearest engineering analogue is pulling the power or forcing a low-power halt state from outside the operating system rather than via its own shutdown routine; the biological difference is that this halt is finely reversible and titratable, and the machine reboots itself, intact, when the clamp is removed.

CHAPTER 26. THE DISORDERS OF CONSCIOUSNESS SPECTRUM (Lesioned Global State Machinery) (The Broken State Machine) (NON-COG)

The states of this whole layer assume an intact machine: switches that flip, arousal nuclei that fire, a thalamocortical fabric that carries the broadcast.

This last entry catalogues what happens when the machine itself is broken by injury, so that arousal and awareness, normally yoked, come apart, and a body can be awake without anyone home.

It is the fault space of Layer 11, and it is honestly flagged as a spectrum of pathology, not a natural state.

Broken names its origin in lesion; state machine names what is damaged, the very switching apparatus the rest of the layer describes.

WHAT IT IS AND WHAT THE ORGANISM DOES IN IT:

Disorders of consciousness (DOC) are the states following severe brain injury in which arousal (wakefulness) and awareness (the contents of consciousness) are dissociated: coma, the vegetative state or unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS), and the minimally conscious state (MCS).

In coma the organism does nothing and cannot be aroused; in the vegetative state it cycles through eye-open "wakefulness" without any evidence of awareness; in the minimally conscious state it shows fluctuating, reproducible but inconsistent signs of awareness without reliable communication.

THE WHOLE-SYSTEM SIGNATURE:

Coma: eyes closed, no sleep-wake cycling, no arousal to stimulation, often a slow or featureless electroencephalogram (EEG).

Vegetative state / unresponsive wakefulness: preserved brainstem-driven sleep-wake cycling with eye opening but no reproducible purposeful behaviour; EEG shows arousal but degraded cortical integration.

Minimally conscious state: intermittent command-following, visual pursuit, or contingent emotional responses; EEG and imaging show partially restored long-range cortical communication.

A related but distinct condition, locked-in syndrome, is fully conscious with almost complete motor paralysis and must not be confused with these.

THE THROWING AND HOLDING MACHINERY:

The states are "thrown" by lesions, not by governors: coma follows damage to the brainstem ascending arousal system (the Layer 9 arousal nuclei) or to large volumes of both cortical hemispheres; recovery of eye opening in the vegetative state reflects a surviving brainstem arousal engine driving a cortex too damaged to generate awareness.

Progress toward the minimally conscious state and beyond depends on re-establishing thalamocortical and cortico-cortical connectivity, with the central thalamus and its cortical loops implicated as a key gate.

THE DOWNWARD ASSIGNMENT (WHAT IT SETS AT EACH LAYER):

• At Layer 9 (regions): the arousal nuclei may fire (driving wakefulness) while thalamus and cortex fail to broadcast (no awareness), the defining dissociation.

• At Layer 8 (motifs): the integrative thalamocortical broadcast motif is disrupted, so arousal rhythms can persist without conscious integration.

• At Layer 7 (junctions): widespread structural and functional disconnection reduces effective long-range signalling.

• At Layer 6 (cells): neuronal loss, diffuse axonal injury, and metabolic depression leave surviving cells unable to sustain coordinated activity.

THE BROADCAST CHANNELS:

The neuromodulatory announcement systems may be partly intact (permitting arousal) yet unable to reach or coordinate a damaged cortex; there is no orderly hormonal signature, only the residue of the injury.

WHAT RUNS IN THIS STATE:

By definition little or no cognition runs; the clinical and scientific task is to detect whether any covert cognition survives, since some behaviourally unresponsive patients show command-following brain activity on functional imaging ("cognitive-motor dissociation").

DURATION AND SCHEDULING:

Not scheduled; coma is typically transient (days to weeks), evolving toward recovery, the vegetative or minimally conscious state, or death; chronic disorders of consciousness can persist for years.

ENTRY AND EXIT TRANSITIONS:

Entry is the injury (trauma, cardiac arrest with oxygen deprivation, haemorrhage); exit, when it happens, is a graded re-emergence, coma to vegetative to minimally conscious to confusional recovery, tracked by standardised scales.

THE ENDOCRINE CONFIGURATION:

Severe brain injury commonly disrupts the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, producing endocrine failures (for example diabetes insipidus or hypopituitarism) that must be managed.

THE AUTONOMIC CONFIGURATION:

Autonomic instability is common, including paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (surges of heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and sweating) after severe injury.

ENERGY AND MAINTENANCE:

Cerebral metabolism is markedly reduced, most severely in the vegetative state, and partial metabolic recovery in associative cortex and thalamus tracks the transition toward the minimally conscious state.

DEVELOPMENT AND LIFESPAN:

Causes and outcomes differ by age; children can show better recovery potential, while outcome overall depends heavily on cause (traumatic injury generally carries a better prognosis than oxygen deprivation) and time.

EVOLUTIONARY PROVENANCE:

None; these are pathological states, informative precisely because they reveal, by lesion, the components the healthy layer relies upon.

COMPARATIVE AND INDIVIDUAL VARIATION:

Outcomes vary enormously between individuals; misdiagnosis is a major problem, with up to about forty percent of vegetative-state diagnoses overturned when standardised behavioural scales, imaging, or electrophysiology are properly used.

DISEASES AND DISORDERS:

The spectrum itself is the disorder: coma, vegetative state / unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, minimally conscious state, and the frequently misdiagnosed cognitive-motor dissociation and locked-in syndrome.

SYSTEMS INTERFACE:

• SUBSCRIBES (INPUTS).

◦ The lesion (location and extent); residual arousal-nucleus and thalamocortical function.

• PUBLISHES (OUTPUTS).

◦ Dissociated arousal and awareness; a clinical level scored on scales such as the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised.

• STATE.

◦ Position on the spectrum (coma, vegetative/UWS, minimally conscious), with trajectory over time.

• PARAMETERS.

◦ Degree of preserved sleep-wake cycling; degree of restored long-range cortical connectivity; residual cerebral metabolism.

• TIME CONSTANTS.

◦ Coma over days to weeks; recovery or chronicity over months to years.

• FAILURE MODE.

◦ The state itself is the failure of Layer 11's machinery; the clinical failure mode is misdiagnosis, missing covert awareness in an apparently unresponsive patient.

THE ENGINEERING NOTE:

In engineering terms this is the fault_state_registry, the catalogue of what happens when the scheduler, the arousal watchdog, or the broadcast bus is physically damaged, publishing a degraded or dissociated status that the rest of the system cannot correct on its own.

The nearest engineering analogue is a machine whose power supply runs (fans spin, lights blink) but whose main bus is severed so no coordinated computation occurs, or a watchdog timer firing over a dead kernel; the biological difference is that partial, unpredictable self-repair is possible, and detecting hidden function in a seemingly halted machine is itself an active science.

THE COGNITIVE SCOREBOARD

Of the twenty-six chapters, six carry the (COG) tag - and the six form a sentence.

Alert task-engaged waking (Chapter 1), relaxed wakefulness (Chapter 2), and focused absorption (Chapter 3) are cognition's online shift.

Stage N2 (Chapter 6), stage N3 (Chapter 7), and rapid-eye-movement sleep (Chapter 8) are its offline shift - the states in which consolidation, which is cognition, runs while the organism appears switched off.

Everything else - the vestibule and inertia states, the six machinery chapters, the five emergency and metabolic overlays, the three scheduled programmes, and the two boundary chapters - is the government: the componentry that decides which cognition runs when, announces the decision to every catalogue below, and guards the boundaries of the state space.

Three closing observations.

First, the downward completion: with this volume, every "thrown from above" in the Layers 6 through 9 modes volumes now has a thrower with a chapter, a mechanism, and an interface - the corpus's mode hierarchy is closed at the top.

Second, the two-shift principle: biology splits cognition into an online phase that acquires and an offline phase that consolidates, connected by a governed schedule - and it pays real costs (sleep inertia, vulnerability, a third of the lifespan) to keep the split, which is the strongest possible evidence that the split is load-bearing.

Third, the engineering lesson: this volume specifies the operating system - a two-process scheduler (Chapters 14 and 15), bistable state switches with a stabiliser (11 through 13), a global configuration bus (16), power and emergency states (17 through 21), cron programmes (22 through 24), and a maintenance cycle (6 through 8) in which the system's own learning is finished.

A synthetic mind built from the corpus's parts but run always-on would be a brain denied sleep - and the clinical literature on that condition is not encouraging.

The neurons think; the other cells make thinking possible; the motifs are how thinking is done; the systems are what thinking is done with; and the states of this volume are WHEN - the calendar and the clock of the mind.
