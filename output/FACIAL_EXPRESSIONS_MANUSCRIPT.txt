PART I: FOUNDATIONS OF FACIAL EXPRESSION SCIENCE

Chapter 1: What Is a Facial Expression

This opening chapter lays the groundwork for the entire book by explaining what scientists mean when they talk about a facial expression, what expressions are for, and the different levels at which they can be studied.

If you have ever wondered why a smile or a frown seems to speak without words, this chapter explains the basic vocabulary you will need for everything that follows.

1.1 Defining the Terms

1.1.1 Facial expression: a coordinated movement of facial muscles that observers can read

A facial expression is a pattern of movement produced by the muscles of the face, and it is coordinated in the sense that several muscles usually act together rather than one at a time.

For example, a typical smile involves muscles that pull the corners of the mouth upward while other muscles may crinkle the skin around the eyes.

What makes these movements interesting to scientists is that other people can read them, meaning that observers reliably notice them and draw conclusions from them about the person's feelings or intentions.

The movements change the shape of the skin, the eyes, the brows, and the mouth, and those visible changes are the signal that observers pick up.

So a facial expression has two sides: a production side, which is the muscle movement itself, and a perception side, which is what an observer makes of it.

1.1.2 Expression versus display: readout of feeling versus social signal

The word expression suggests that something inside, such as a feeling, is being pressed outward onto the face, like steam escaping from a kettle.

Scientists who prefer this idea talk about the face as a readout, meaning a direct display of the emotional state happening inside the person, similar to a gauge on a dashboard.

Other scientists, most famously the psychologist Alan Fridlund, prefer the word display, which comes from the study of animal behavior and means a signal aimed at influencing other individuals.

On the display view, a smile is less like a gauge showing inner happiness and more like a message that says something such as I am friendly, or let us cooperate.

The difference matters, because a readout should appear whenever the feeling occurs, while a display should appear mainly when there is an audience to receive it.

This book will use the everyday word expression throughout, but the tension between readout and signal will return again and again in later chapters.

1.1.3 Spontaneous versus posed (deliberate) expressions

A spontaneous expression is one that happens by itself, without the person deciding to make it, such as the grimace that appears when you stub your toe.

A posed expression, also called a deliberate expression, is one that a person makes on purpose, such as smiling for a photograph.

Research suggests that these two kinds of expression are driven by partly different brain systems: deliberate expressions rely mainly on the outer layer of the brain called the cortex, while spontaneous emotional expressions rely more on deeper, older brain structures.

Because of this, spontaneous and posed versions of the same expression can differ in their exact muscle patterns and in their timing, a theme explored in detail later in this book.

The difference also matters for research, because a study built entirely on posed photographs may not tell us how faces behave in real emotional life.

1.1.4 Macroexpressions, microexpressions, and subtle expressions

A macroexpression is an ordinary, full-face expression that lasts roughly half a second to a few seconds and is easy for others to see.

A microexpression is a claimed category of very brief expression, lasting only a fraction of a second, that is said to occur when a person tries to conceal a feeling and a flash of it escapes anyway.

Microexpressions are popular in television and in security training, but, as a later chapter will explain, careful research finds them to be rare and only weakly connected to lying.

A subtle expression is different again: it is an expression of normal duration but of very low intensity, involving only a slight movement, such as a faint tightening of the lips.

Keeping these three terms separate helps avoid a common confusion, because a brief expression, a weak expression, and a concealed expression are not the same thing.

1.2 The Functions of Facial Expressions

1.2.1 Communicating internal states to others

The most familiar function of facial expressions is communication, meaning the transfer of information from one person to another.

When a friend's face falls, you learn something about their disappointment without a single word being spoken, and this can happen in a fraction of a second.

This kind of rapid, wordless information sharing is valuable for a highly social species like ours, because knowing how others feel helps us predict what they will do next.

Even scientists who disagree about whether expressions are true readouts of emotion agree that faces carry socially useful information.

The debate, which later chapters will explore, is about how tightly that information is tied to actual inner feeling.

1.2.2 Regulating social interaction: turn-taking, appeasement, and dominance

Facial expressions also work as traffic signals for conversation and social life, keeping interactions running smoothly.

Small facial movements, such as raised eyebrows or a listener's smile, help manage turn-taking, which is the back-and-forth rhythm by which people take turns speaking.

Other expressions serve appeasement, meaning they calm a tense situation, as when an embarrassed smile after a mistake signals that no challenge is intended.

Still others signal dominance, meaning social power or the intent to stand one's ground, as in a hard stare with lowered brows.

Seen this way, faces are tools for coordinating group life, not just windows onto private feeling.

1.2.3 Preparing the sense organs: widened eyes in fear, narrowed eyes in disgust

Some scientists argue that the shapes of expressions began not as messages at all but as physical adjustments that help the sense organs do their jobs.

Research led by Joshua Susskind and Adam Anderson found that making a fear face, with widened eyes and flared nostrils, enlarges the visual field and increases air intake, which would help a frightened person detect danger and prepare to flee.

A disgust face does roughly the opposite: the narrowed eyes and wrinkled nose reduce visual exposure and restrict the intake of air, which would help shield the body from something foul or contaminated.

Fear and disgust expressions are, in this sense, physical opposites, one opening the senses wide and the other closing them down.

On this account, the communicative value of expressions may have been added later in evolution, on top of an original sensory function.

1.2.4 Influencing others: the face as a social tool

A final function goes one step beyond communication: expressions can be used, deliberately or not, to change other people's behavior.

A child's exaggerated pout can win sympathy, a boss's stern look can end a discussion, and a warm smile can persuade a stranger to help.

Alan Fridlund's behavioral ecology view, discussed in Chapter 2, treats this influencing function as the main point of facial behavior, arguing that displays are tools for social negotiation rather than leaks of inner feeling.

Behavioral ecology is the branch of biology that studies how behavior helps animals survive and reproduce in their social and physical environments.

Whether influence is the whole story or only part of it, there is no doubt that people routinely use their faces to steer the people around them.

1.3 The Face as a Signal System

1.3.1 The face's special status in human attention and perception

Human beings are face specialists: from the first days of life, infants prefer to look at face-like patterns over other patterns of similar complexity.

Adults detect faces faster than almost any other kind of object, and the brain contains regions that respond much more strongly to faces than to houses, tools, or scenery.

This special status makes sense, because for a social species the face is the single richest source of information about identity, attention, intention, and feeling.

Because faces grab attention so powerfully, even small facial movements can be noticed and interpreted by observers.

Later chapters on the perceiving brain will describe this face-processing machinery in detail.

1.3.2 Static features versus dynamic movement

A face carries two different kinds of information: static features and dynamic movement.

Static features are the parts that do not change from moment to moment, such as the shape of the nose, the spacing of the eyes, and the structure of the jaw, and these mainly signal identity, age, and other stable characteristics.

Dynamic movement is the changing part, the rising brows and stretching lips, and this is where expression lives.

Research shows that observers recognize emotions more easily from moving faces than from frozen photographs, because motion adds information about how an expression unfolds over time.

This distinction matters for science, because a field built largely on still photographs may underestimate how much the timing and flow of movement contribute to real-life face reading.

1.3.3 The face in context: body posture, voice, and situation

In everyday life, a face is never seen alone: it comes attached to a body, accompanied by a voice, and embedded in a situation.

Experiments show that the very same facial photograph can be read quite differently depending on the posture of the body it is placed on or the scene surrounding it.

For example, a wide-eyed, open-mouthed face may read as terrified on a cowering body but as triumphant on the body of an athlete who has just won.

The voice adds further cues, since tone, loudness, and pace carry emotional information of their own.

Scientists increasingly agree that face reading in the real world is context reading, a point that becomes central in the debates covered later in this book.

1.4 Levels of Analysis

1.4.1 Muscular and anatomical level

The most concrete level of analysis is the anatomy, meaning the physical structure of the face itself.

At this level, scientists ask which muscles exist in the face, how they attach to skin and bone, and which visible changes each muscle produces when it contracts.

The human face contains a set of muscles, often called the mimetic muscles, that attach to the skin and move it, rather than moving bones as most body muscles do.

Careful description at this level makes it possible to say exactly what a face is doing, independent of any guess about what the person is feeling.

This anatomical foundation underlies the measurement systems described in Chapter 3 and the full anatomy tour in Part Two of this book.

1.4.2 Neural production and perception circuits

The second level concerns the brain: the neural circuits, meaning connected networks of nerve cells, that produce expressions and that perceive them in others.

On the production side, separate brain pathways drive deliberate facial movements and spontaneous emotional ones, which is why some patients with brain damage can smile on command but not with genuine amusement, or the reverse.

On the perception side, a network of brain regions specializes in detecting faces, recognizing who they belong to, and interpreting their movements.

Studying this level requires the neuroscience methods introduced at the end of Chapter 3, such as brain imaging and the study of patients with brain injuries.

Together, the production and perception circuits form a matched pair, since a signal is only useful if others have the machinery to receive it.

1.4.3 Behavioral and social level

The third level looks at whole people interacting: when do expressions actually occur, in front of whom, and with what effects.

At this level, scientists observe faces during conversations, games, arguments, and everyday encounters, asking how expressions coordinate social life.

A key finding here is the audience effect, which is the tendency for people to show more facial expression when others are present, or believed to be present, than when truly alone.

This level also covers display rules, which are the social norms telling people when to show, hide, exaggerate, or mask their expressions.

The behavioral level reminds us that a face never acts in a social vacuum.

1.4.4 Cultural and evolutionary level

The broadest level asks two deep questions: how did facial expressions arise over evolutionary time, and how do they vary across human cultures today.

The evolutionary question, first posed seriously by Charles Darwin, looks for continuity between human expressions and the facial displays of other animals, especially monkeys and apes.

The cultural question asks which parts of expression are shared by all human societies and which parts are local customs, learned like a language.

These questions are connected, because features shared across all cultures are candidates for evolved biology, while features that vary are candidates for cultural learning.

Much of the fiercest debate in this field, covered in Part Five, takes place at this level.

Chapter 2: History of the Field

The science of facial expression did not appear overnight: it grew from centuries of observation, some of it brilliant and some of it badly mistaken.

This chapter traces that story from ancient face reading, through Darwin's revolutionary book, to the modern researchers whose disagreements still shape the field today.

2.1 Early Observers

2.1.1 Ancient physiognomy and its errors

Physiognomy is the old practice of judging a person's character, such as honesty or intelligence, from the fixed features of their face, like the shape of the nose or forehead.

Versions of physiognomy appear in ancient Greek writings and remained popular in Europe for centuries, reaching a peak of fashion in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

The practice was mistaken at its core, because the stable structure of a face is not a reliable guide to character, and physiognomic claims repeatedly failed when tested.

Worse, physiognomy fed harmful prejudices, since it was used to declare whole groups of people criminal or inferior by their looks.

Its lasting lesson for science is a warning: people readily believe they can read deep truths from faces, and that intuition must be tested, not trusted.

Modern expression science differs from physiognomy in a crucial way, because it studies facial movement, not fixed facial structure.

2.1.2 Charles Bell and the anatomy of expression

Sir Charles Bell was a Scottish anatomist and surgeon who, in the early nineteenth century, wrote influential works on the anatomy and philosophy of facial expression.

Anatomy is the study of the physical structure of the body, and Bell brought a surgeon's precise knowledge of facial muscles and nerves to the question of how expressions are made.

Bell described how specific muscles produce specific expressive movements, and his work on the nerves of the face was a landmark of medical science, remembered today in the condition called Bell's palsy, a sudden weakness of the facial muscles.

However, Bell interpreted expression through religion: he argued that humans were given unique facial muscles by the Creator specifically to express uniquely human feelings.

That claim of human uniqueness set up a target, because Charles Darwin later argued the opposite, that human expressions are continuous with those of other animals.

2.1.3 Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne: electrical stimulation of facial muscles

Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne was a nineteenth century French physician who found a remarkable way to study facial muscles one at a time: he applied mild electrical currents to the surface of the face, causing individual muscles to contract.

He photographed the results, and his 1862 book, known in English as The Mechanism of Human Facial Expression, paired striking photographs with careful analysis of which muscle produces which visible change.

Because photography was new, Duchenne's work was among the first to freeze expressions for detailed study, and his images are still reproduced today.

His most famous discovery concerned smiling: he observed that a smile of genuine enjoyment involves not only the mouth muscles but also the muscle circling the eye, which raises the cheeks and crinkles the eye corners.

That eye-crinkling smile is now called the Duchenne smile in his honor, and his muscle-by-muscle approach directly inspired the modern coding systems described in Chapter 3.

2.2 Darwin's Foundation

2.2.1 The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872)

In 1872, Charles Darwin published The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, the book that founded the scientific study of facial expression.

Darwin, already famous for his theory of evolution, gathered evidence from an astonishing range of sources: observations of his own children, reports from missionaries and travelers around the world, studies of animals, and Duchenne's photographs.

He even ran an early version of a judgment study, showing Duchenne's photographs to guests and recording what emotions they saw in them.

He also sent questionnaires across the globe asking whether people in distant cultures expressed grief, anger, and joy with the same facial movements as Europeans, and the answers suggested broad similarity.

The book argued that emotional expressions are products of evolution, inherited from our animal ancestors, rather than gifts unique to humans.

Nearly every debate in the modern field can be traced back to a question Darwin raised in this book.

2.2.2 Darwin's three principles of expression

Darwin proposed three principles to explain why expressions take the particular forms they do.

The first, called the principle of serviceable associated habits, says that movements which were once practically useful in a given state of mind become habitual, so they appear whenever that state occurs, even when they no longer serve any purpose.

For example, an animal that once bared its teeth to bite may come to bare its teeth whenever it is angry, whether or not it intends to bite.

The second, the principle of antithesis, says that opposite states of mind produce opposite movements: if hostility involves a stiff, upright posture, then friendliness will involve the reverse, a relaxed and lowered one, simply because it is the opposite.

The third principle is the direct action of the excited nervous system on the body, meaning that strong emotion releases surplus nervous energy that spills into movements such as trembling, which serve no purpose at all.

Modern scientists have revised these principles, but the underlying question, why does each expression look the way it does, remains central to the field.

2.2.3 Continuity of expression between animals and humans

A core claim of Darwin's book is continuity, meaning that human expressions are not a fresh invention but modified versions of displays found in other animals.

Darwin pointed to similarities such as the snarl of a dog and the human sneer, or the play behavior of apes and human laughter.

This claim directly opposed Charles Bell's view that expression was a special human endowment, and it supported Darwin's larger argument that humans evolved from earlier animals.

Modern research on monkeys and apes has broadly supported continuity, finding, for example, that the relaxed open-mouth play face of apes resembles human laughter, and that the bared-teeth display of some primates may be related to the human smile.

The evolutionary story is now studied with rigorous comparative methods, described in Part Five, but the founding insight is Darwin's.

2.3 The Twentieth-Century Revival

2.3.1 Silvan Tomkins and affect theory

For decades after Darwin, psychology largely lost interest in facial expression, partly because behaviorism, the school of psychology that studied only outward behavior and distrusted talk of inner states, dominated the field.

The revival began with Silvan Tomkins, an American psychologist who, starting in the 1960s, proposed what he called affect theory.

Affect is a technical word psychologists use for feeling states, and Tomkins argued that humans are born with a small number of primary affects, each produced by an innate program in the brain.

Crucially, Tomkins placed the face at the center of emotion, claiming that each innate affect program drives its own distinctive pattern of facial movement.

Tomkins personally encouraged two younger researchers, Paul Ekman and Carroll Izard, to test these ideas, and their work would define the next several decades of the field.

2.3.2 Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen: cross-cultural judgment studies

Paul Ekman, working with his long-term collaborator Wallace Friesen, set out to test whether facial expressions of emotion are universal, meaning recognized the same way by people everywhere.

By Ekman's own account, he initially expected to prove Tomkins wrong, believing that expressions were learned like language and would differ across cultures.

Ekman and Friesen showed photographs of posed expressions to observers in many countries across the Americas, Europe, and Asia, asking them to name the emotion shown.

Observers in every country agreed well above chance on faces showing happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise, a result Ekman took as evidence for universality.

A critic could object that all these countries shared movies, magazines, and television, so agreement might reflect shared media exposure rather than shared biology, and answering that objection required finding people isolated from world media.

2.3.3 Carroll Izard and differential emotions theory

Working independently at the same time, Carroll Izard conducted his own cross-cultural judgment studies with his own set of photographs and found similarly high agreement across many literate cultures.

Izard developed differential emotions theory, which holds that a set of distinct, discrete emotions, each with its own facial expression pattern, is present from early in life and serves basic adaptive functions.

The word differential signals the theory's key claim that emotions such as anger, fear, and joy are genuinely different systems, not just points on a single scale of feeling good or bad.

Izard applied his theory especially to infants and children, and he built one of the first systematic tools for coding infant facial expressions.

The convergence of Ekman's and Izard's independent findings persuaded many psychologists that Darwin had been right about universality.

2.3.4 The Papua New Guinea studies with the Fore people

To answer the media-exposure objection, Ekman and Friesen traveled in the late 1960s to the highlands of Papua New Guinea to study the Fore people, a group with minimal contact with the outside world and no access to films or magazines.

Because many Fore participants could not read and found lists of emotion words difficult, the researchers used a story-matching task: a translator told a brief story, such as one about a person whose child had died, and the participant chose which of three photographs matched the story.

Fore adults and children chose the predicted face at rates well above chance for most emotions, though fear and surprise were frequently confused with each other.

In a second study, Fore participants were asked to pose the expressions themselves, and American college students later viewing those films could largely identify the intended emotions.

Published in 1971 under the title Constants Across Cultures in the Face and Emotion, this work became the most famous evidence for universality, and, as later chapters will show, also the most closely re-examined.

2.4 The Modern Debate Takes Shape

2.4.1 James Russell's 1994 critique of universality evidence

In 1994, the psychologist James Russell published a detailed critique in the journal Psychological Bulletin, asking whether the universality evidence really proved what it was claimed to prove.

Russell argued that the standard methods contained hidden helps for participants: the forced-choice format, in which observers must pick one label from a short list, can inflate agreement, because even an unsure observer must choose something.

He also noted that the studies used carefully posed, exaggerated photographs rather than natural expressions, and that within-culture agreement was often noticeably higher than agreement in distant cultures.

Russell did not claim that faces are meaningless across cultures; rather, he argued for what he later called minimal universality, the idea that some information in faces is widely shared while specific emotion labels are shaped by culture and language.

His critique forced the field to sharpen its methods and marked the beginning of the modern debate that runs through this entire book.

2.4.2 Alan Fridlund's behavioral ecology view

Also in the early 1990s, Alan Fridlund published a different kind of challenge, known as the behavioral ecology view of facial displays.

Fridlund argued that faces are not readouts of inner emotion at all: instead, facial displays are social tools that evolved to influence the behavior of others.

On this view, a smile does not mean I feel happy inside but rather signals something like an intention to affiliate, and a scowl signals readiness to confront.

The view predicts audience effects, and research has confirmed that people do smile far more in social settings, or even when merely imagining an audience, than when truly alone.

Fridlund's position remains a minority view, but it has proved durable, and its emphasis on the social purpose of displays has been absorbed into much of mainstream thinking.

2.4.3 Lisa Feldman Barrett and the constructionist challenge

A third challenge comes from Lisa Feldman Barrett and the constructionist school of emotion theory.

Constructionism is the view that emotions are not built-in biological programs but are constructed in the moment by the brain, out of basic bodily feelings combined with concepts learned from one's culture and language.

On this view, there is no single anger circuit in the brain and no single anger face; instead, people express anger in many different ways depending on the situation, and cultures carve up feelings differently in the first place.

In 2019, Barrett led a team of five senior scientists from different specialties in a major review which concluded that people express and recognize emotions with far more variability than the classic view assumed, and that context matters more than the face alone.

That review explicitly warned against technologies and training programs that claim to read emotions from facial movements by themselves, a warning that becomes important in the final part of this book.

2.4.4 Carlos Crivelli and the Trobriand Islands findings

The psychologist Carlos Crivelli, working with James Russell and others, returned to the strategy of studying isolated societies, spending extended time with the Trobriand Islanders of Papua New Guinea.

In research published in 2016, Trobriander participants shown the classic gasping fear face, with raised brows, widened eyes, and open mouth, most often interpreted it not as fear but as anger and threat, as the face of someone intending to attack.

In free-labeling tasks, where participants describe faces in their own words instead of choosing from a list, Trobrianders rarely produced the emotion labels that the universality thesis predicts.

These findings echo Russell's methodological warnings, suggesting that the impressive agreement in classic studies depended partly on the constrained methods used.

Defenders of universality dispute how to interpret the Trobriand results, and this live controversy, along with the field's points of genuine agreement, is examined fully in Part Five.

Chapter 3: Methods of Studying Facial Expression

Before diving deeper into findings, it helps to understand how facial expression science is actually done, because many of the field's disputes turn out to be disputes about methods.

This chapter surveys the main tools: asking observers to judge faces, measuring facial movement directly, choosing the right face stimuli, and looking inside the brain.

3.1 Judgment Studies

3.1.1 Forced-choice labeling and its criticisms

A judgment study is an experiment in which people, called observers or judges, look at faces and report what they see, and it is the oldest and most common method in the field.

In the forced-choice version, the observer views a face and must pick one label from a fixed short list, such as happy, sad, angry, afraid, disgusted, or surprised.

Forced choice is easy to run and easy to score, which is why the classic universality studies relied on it.

Its central weakness is that it can manufacture agreement: an observer who is unsure, or who would naturally describe the face some other way, is still funneled into one of the offered labels.

Experiments comparing methods show that forced choice yields higher apparent recognition rates than more open methods, so results from forced-choice studies must be read with that inflation in mind.

3.1.2 Free labeling and open-ended methods

In free labeling, the observer looks at a face and answers in their own words, with no list of options provided.

This method removes the artificial help of a label list, so it gives a stricter and arguably more honest test of what observers spontaneously see in a face.

Free-labeling studies typically find lower, though often still above-chance, agreement compared with forced choice, and the gap between the two methods is itself informative.

The method has costs: answers are messy, since one observer may say furious while another says annoyed, and researchers must make judgment calls about which answers count as matching.

Fully open-ended methods go further still, letting observers describe the situation or the person's likely behavior rather than requiring an emotion word at all, which matters because some cultures may not naturally describe faces in emotion terms.

3.1.3 Matching faces to stories and situations

A third approach asks observers to match faces to stories or situations instead of to single words.

In the classic version used with the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, a short story was told, such as one about a person meeting friends, and the participant pointed to the face, among several, that fit the story.

This method suits participants who cannot read, and it avoids the problem of translating emotion words across very different languages.

However, it still constrains the answer, because the participant must choose among the faces provided, so some of the inflation problem of forced choice remains.

Comparing results across forced choice, free labeling, and story matching has become an important research strategy in itself, since conclusions that survive all three methods are on much firmer ground.

3.2 Measuring the Face Directly

3.2.1 The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) and action units

Judgment studies measure what observers think about faces, but scientists also need to measure what faces actually do, and the standard tool is the Facial Action Coding System (FACS).

Published by Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen in 1978, the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) breaks every visible facial movement into units called action units, each corresponding to the contraction of a particular facial muscle or muscle group.

For example, action unit one is the inner brow raiser, action unit four is the brow lowerer, and action unit twelve is the lip corner puller that forms the core of a smile.

Crucially, the system describes movement only, without naming any emotion, so a trained coder records exactly which action units occurred, at what intensity, and when, leaving interpretation as a separate step.

This separation of description from interpretation was a major scientific advance, because it lets researchers test, rather than assume, which movements go with which feelings.

The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) is covered in depth in Part Two of this book.

3.2.2 Facial electromyography (EMG): recording muscle electrical activity

Electromyography (EMG) is a technique that records the tiny electrical signals muscles produce when they contract, using small sensors placed on the skin.

Applied to the face, electromyography (EMG) can detect muscle activity so slight that it produces no visible movement at all, making it far more sensitive than visual coding.

Two sites are used most often: the zygomaticus major, the cheek muscle that pulls the mouth corners up in smiling, and the corrugator supercilii, the brow muscle that knits the eyebrows in frowning.

Activity in these two muscles tracks pleasant and unpleasant reactions respectively, so electromyography (EMG) is widely used to measure emotional responses to pictures, sounds, films, and advertisements.

The method's main limits are that sensors can be placed on only a few muscles at a time, and that wearing sensors on the face may itself make behavior less natural.

3.2.3 Automatic computer-vision measurement

Computer vision is the field of computing that teaches machines to interpret images, and modern software can now detect faces in video and estimate which action units are active, frame by frame.

Automated coding is transforming the field, because a computer can code hours of video in the time a human coder would need for minutes, making very large studies affordable.

Validation studies that compare software against human coders and against electromyography (EMG) show encouraging agreement for strong, clear movements, but the software remains less sensitive and less accurate for subtle activity.

Performance also degrades with poor lighting, head turns, glasses, and other real-world complications, and accuracy can vary across faces of different ages and skin tones.

Careful researchers therefore treat automated measurements as useful but imperfect, checking them against human coding, a caution that matters even more for the commercial applications discussed in Part Eight.

3.3 Stimulus Sets and Study Design

3.3.1 Standardized face databases: posed photograph sets and their limits

A stimulus is any item, such as a photograph, shown to a participant in an experiment, and expression research depends on shared sets of face stimuli.

Standardized databases, such as the Pictures of Facial Affect, the Karolinska Directed Emotional Faces set, and the NimStim set, contain photographs of people posing specified expressions under controlled conditions.

Shared databases are valuable because they let different laboratories run comparable studies and check one another's results.

Their central limitation is that the expressions are posed on request, often in deliberately strong, prototype form, so they may be cleaner and more exaggerated than the expressions of everyday life.

Older databases have also been criticized for containing mostly young adult faces from a narrow range of backgrounds, and newer sets have been built to include more diverse ages and ethnicities.

Findings obtained with posed prototypes do not always generalize to spontaneous faces, which is a recurring theme in the universality debate.

3.3.2 Dynamic video stimuli versus static images

Most classic research used static images, meaning still photographs, but real expressions are dynamic, meaning they move and unfold over time.

Studies comparing the two show that observers generally recognize emotions better from moving faces, because motion reveals how an expression starts, builds, and fades.

Researchers have created dynamic stimuli in several ways: filming actors, or using morphing software, which smoothly transforms one image into another, to animate a neutral face into an expressive one.

Timing information turns out to carry meaning of its own, for instance in distinguishing genuine from deliberate smiles, so leaving it out is not a harmless simplification.

The shift from photographs to video is one of the clearest methodological trends in the modern field.

3.3.3 Spontaneous expression elicitation in the laboratory

To study genuine expressions, researchers must elicit them, meaning bring them about, in the laboratory, since simply asking a person to look afraid produces a pose, not an emotion.

Common elicitation methods include showing emotionally powerful film clips, delivering surprises, presenting bad smells, asking participants to relive intense memories, and staging carefully controlled social situations.

Participants are often video-recorded, sometimes without knowing the recording is the point of the study, and their faces are later coded with the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) or by software.

Ethics committees, the boards that review research to protect participants, require that any deception be mild and be explained to participants afterward.

Spontaneous elicitation is harder and messier than using posed photographs, but it addresses the field's most important question: what do faces actually do during real emotion, as opposed to what posed prototypes assume they do.

3.4 Neuroscience Methods

3.4.1 Brain imaging of expression perception

Brain imaging refers to technologies that measure activity in the living brain while a person performs a task, such as viewing faces.

The most widely used method is functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which tracks changes in blood flow that follow neural activity, showing where in the brain face processing occurs.

Electroencephalography (EEG), which records the brain's electrical activity through sensors on the scalp, and magnetoencephalography (MEG), which records the accompanying magnetic fields, show when processing occurs, with precision of a few thousandths of a second.

Imaging studies have mapped a network of regions that respond strongly to faces and expressions, including the amygdala, an almond-shaped structure deep in the brain involved in detecting emotional significance, which can respond to fearful faces within roughly a sixth of a second.

Each method trades off strengths, since functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) shows where but not precisely when, while electroencephalography (EEG) shows when but not precisely where, so researchers often combine them.

3.4.2 Lesion and stimulation evidence

A lesion is an area of damage in the brain, caused by injury, stroke, surgery, or disease, and lesion studies ask what abilities are lost when a particular region is damaged.

This evidence is powerful because it moves beyond correlation: if damage to a region abolishes an ability, that region was in some way necessary for it.

A famous example is that patients with damage to the amygdala on both sides of the brain can have selective difficulty recognizing fear in faces, partly because they fail to look at the eye region, which carries the key information for fear.

Stimulation methods approach the question from the opposite direction, activating brain tissue directly, either with electrodes placed during surgery for epilepsy or with transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), a noninvasive technique that uses magnetic pulses from a coil held over the scalp to briefly alter activity in a targeted patch of cortex.

Together, lesion and stimulation evidence tests whether the regions highlighted by imaging actually cause the abilities in question, rather than merely accompanying them.

3.4.3 Eye tracking and gaze patterns while reading faces

An eye tracker is a device, usually camera-based, that records exactly where a person is looking from moment to moment.

Applied to face perception, eye tracking reveals the scan path, the sequence of looks an observer makes across a face, which typically concentrates on the eyes and mouth.

Research shows that observers direct their gaze toward the most informative regions for each expression, such as the eyes for fear and the mouth for happiness.

Eye tracking has also revealed group differences, for example that observers from different cultures may distribute their attention differently across the face, and that some autistic individuals show reduced looking at the eyes.

Because it is noninvasive and works with infants, patients, and even other animals, eye tracking has become a standard companion to nearly every other method in this chapter.

PART II: THE PRODUCING FACE: ANATOMY AND NEURAL CONTROL

Chapter 4: The Anatomy of the Face

This chapter looks at the physical machinery of expression - the muscles that move the face and the nerve that commands them.

It also asks what makes the human face unusual among animals as a signaling surface.

4.1 The Facial Muscles

4.1.1 The mimetic muscles: muscles that move skin rather than bone

Most muscles in the body pull on bones to move a limb or a joint.

The mimetic muscles - the muscles of facial expression - are different, because most of them attach at one end to bone or to other muscle and at the other end directly to the skin of the face.

When a mimetic muscle contracts, it pulls, folds, or wrinkles the skin itself, which is why a small contraction can visibly change the whole look of a face.

Anatomists group these muscles into regions around the eyes, the nose, and the mouth, and all of them together form a thin, interwoven sheet just under the facial skin.

There are roughly twenty paired mimetic muscles, though textbooks count them slightly differently because some muscles blend into one another.

4.1.2 The brow and eye region: frontalis, corrugator, orbicularis oculi

The frontalis is the broad, flat muscle of the forehead, and when it contracts it raises the eyebrows and creates the horizontal furrows of a surprised or worried forehead.

The corrugator supercilii is a small, slender muscle hidden near the inner end of each eyebrow, and it pulls the brows together and downward, producing the vertical frown lines seen in anger, concentration, and distress.

The orbicularis oculi is a ring of muscle that circles each eye.

Its inner, eyelid part closes the lids gently in blinking, while its outer, orbital part squeezes the whole eye region shut and raises the cheek, producing the crow's-feet wrinkles at the eye corners.

That outer part will matter greatly later in this book, because its contraction is the classic marker of a smile of genuine enjoyment.

4.1.3 The midface and mouth region: zygomatic major, levator labii, orbicularis oris

The zygomaticus major runs diagonally from the cheekbone down to the corner of the mouth, and it pulls the lip corners up and outward - it is the main smiling muscle.

The levator labii superioris, whose name simply means the lifter of the upper lip, raises the upper lip and deepens the fold beside the nose, a movement central to the disgust expression.

A neighboring muscle, the levator labii superioris alaeque nasi, also wrinkles the nose, adding the scrunched-nose look of strong disgust.

The orbicularis oris is a ring of muscle encircling the mouth, and it closes, presses, purses, and shapes the lips for speech, kissing, and expressions such as determined lip pressing.

Because these mouth-region muscles interlace with one another, the mouth is the most finely adjustable part of the face.

4.1.4 The lower face: depressor anguli oris, mentalis, platysma

The depressor anguli oris runs from the lower jaw up to the corner of the mouth, and it pulls the lip corners downward into the drooping mouth of sadness and grief.

The mentalis sits in the chin, and when it contracts it pushes the chin skin upward, dimpling it and pushing the lower lip out into a pout.

The platysma is a broad, thin sheet that spreads from the lower face down across the front of the neck, and it tightens the neck skin and pulls the lower face down, as in expressions of intense fear or effort.

Together these lower-face muscles handle much of the downward, tightening, and protruding side of the expressive repertoire.

4.2 The Facial Nerve

4.2.1 Cranial nerve seven and its five branches

Cranial nerves are nerves that emerge directly from the brain rather than from the spinal cord, and humans have twelve pairs of them.

The seventh cranial nerve, called the facial nerve, is the one that carries the movement commands to almost all of the mimetic muscles.

After leaving the brainstem - the stalk at the base of the brain - the facial nerve passes through the skull, exits near the ear, and fans out across the face through a salivary gland called the parotid gland.

Within the face it divides into five main branches: the temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, and cervical branches.

Roughly speaking, the temporal branch serves the forehead and brow, the zygomatic branch serves the eye region, the buccal branch serves the cheek and upper mouth, the marginal mandibular branch serves the lower lip and chin, and the cervical branch serves the platysma in the neck.

4.2.2 Upper-face versus lower-face innervation and its clinical meaning

Innervation means the nerve supply that controls a muscle.

Each side of the face is driven by the brain, but the two halves of the face are wired differently: the muscles of the upper face, such as the forehead, receive commands from both sides of the brain, while the muscles of the lower face receive commands mainly from the opposite side of the brain.

This wiring difference gives doctors a valuable clue.

If a stroke damages one side of the brain, the patient's lower face droops on the opposite side, but the forehead still moves, because the undamaged side of the brain can still reach it.

If instead the facial nerve itself is damaged - as in Bell's palsy, a usually temporary paralysis of the facial nerve - the whole half of the face, forehead included, stops moving on that side.

So a spared forehead points toward a problem in the brain, while a paralyzed forehead points toward a problem in the nerve.

4.3 What Makes the Human Face Special

4.3.1 Fine muscle control and bare facial skin

Human facial skin is largely bare, unlike the fur-covered faces of most mammals, so every crease, fold, and color change is visible to an onlooker.

The human face is also served by an unusually rich and fine-grained nerve supply, allowing small, precisely graded movements rather than only large, all-or-nothing ones.

Comparative anatomists note that the basic set of facial muscles is broadly shared across primates - the group of animals that includes monkeys, apes, and humans - so the human difference is more a matter of control, visibility, and use than of wholly new muscles.

The result is a signaling surface on which subtle differences, such as a slight tightening of the eyelids or a faint lip press, can carry meaning.

4.3.2 The white sclera of the eye and gaze signaling

The sclera is the tough outer coat of the eyeball, the part visible as the white of the eye.

In humans the exposed sclera is uniformly white, and the eye opening is horizontally elongated, so the dark iris stands out sharply and its position is easy to see.

The gaze-signaling hypothesis, extended by the cooperative eye hypothesis, proposes that these features evolved because they let others see exactly where we are looking, which supports cooperation, joint attention, and communication.

Experimental work has shown that both humans and chimpanzees judge gaze direction more easily from human-like eyes with white sclera than from darker chimpanzee-like eyes, especially at a distance or in dim light.

Some recent comparative studies have complicated the simple story by finding that other apes vary in scleral color more than once thought, so researchers now treat the uniform whiteness of the human sclera, rather than white sclera as such, as the distinctive feature.

4.3.3 Comparison with the faces of other primates

Other primates have expressive faces too, and this is a matter of degree rather than an absolute human monopoly.

Chimpanzees, our closest living relatives, share most of our facial muscles and produce recognizable expressions such as the play face, an open-mouthed expression shown during rough-and-tumble play, and the bared-teeth display, often a signal of appeasement or submission.

Monkeys such as macaques also have rich facial repertoires, including lip smacking, a rapid lip movement used in friendly interactions.

Systematic comparisons using anatomy-based coding systems, described in the next chapter, show substantial overlap in the movements available to humans and chimpanzees, along with differences in mobility of specific regions such as the brow.

What appears most distinctly human is the combination of bare skin, high-contrast eyes, fine graded control, and the use of expressions within language-rich, highly cooperative social life.

Chapter 5: The Facial Action Coding System

To study facial expressions scientifically, researchers needed a way to describe exactly what a face is doing without guessing at what the person feels.

This chapter introduces the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), the standard tool for doing precisely that.

5.1 The Logic of FACS

5.1.1 Describing movement, not emotion: anatomy-based coding

The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) was published in 1978 by the psychologists Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen, building on earlier anatomical work by the Swedish researcher Carl-Herman Hjortsjo.

Its central idea is discipline: the coder records only the visible muscle movements on a face, never the emotion the face might seem to show.

A FACS coder does not write down smile or anger; the coder writes down which specific movements occurred, how strongly, and when.

This separation matters because it keeps the description of behavior independent from any theory about what the behavior means.

Whether a particular movement pattern really signals a particular emotion then becomes a question that evidence can answer, rather than an assumption built into the measurement.

5.1.2 Action units as the alphabet of facial behavior

The basic unit of FACS is the action unit (AU), a minimal facial movement that observers can reliably tell apart, usually produced by one muscle or a small group of muscles.

Each action unit has a number and a plain descriptive name: for example, action unit 1 is the inner brow raiser, action unit 12 is the lip corner puller, and action unit 9 is the nose wrinkler.

The core system contains around thirty action units for facial movements, plus additional codes for head and eye positions and miscellaneous actions.

Action units work like an alphabet: just as letters combine into words, action units combine into full facial expressions, and a coder describes any expression as a list such as 1 plus 4 plus 15.

This compact vocabulary lets researchers in different laboratories describe faces in exactly the same terms.

5.1.3 Intensity scoring from A (trace) to E (maximum)

FACS also records how strong each movement is, because a faint movement and a full-force one can mean different things.

Intensity is scored on a five-step letter scale: A means a trace, a movement barely visible at all; B means slight; C means marked or pronounced; D means severe or extreme; and E means the maximum the face can produce.

A coder writes the letter after the action unit number, so 12C means a lip corner pull of clearly visible, moderate strength.

Scoring intensity takes training, because the coder must judge strength from changes in skin folds, wrinkles, and feature positions rather than from any direct measure of muscle force.

5.2 Key Action Units

5.2.1 Brow actions: inner brow raiser, outer brow raiser, brow lowerer

Action unit 1, the inner brow raiser, lifts only the inner ends of the eyebrows, giving the face the oblique, worried brow seen in sadness and fear.

Action unit 2, the outer brow raiser, lifts the outer ends of the eyebrows, and together with action unit 1 it produces the fully raised brows of surprise.

Action unit 4, the brow lowerer, is driven mainly by the corrugator muscle described in Chapter 4; it pulls the brows down and together, creating the vertical frown lines of anger, distress, and hard concentration.

These three brow actions can combine, and the particular combination is often the most telling clue to which emotion a face expresses.

For example, the pairing of inner brow raising with brow lowering produces a distinctive puckered, grief-like brow that is hard to make on purpose.

5.2.2 Eye actions: cheek raiser, lid tightener, eye widening

Action unit 6, the cheek raiser, is the contraction of the outer ring of the orbicularis oculi; it lifts the cheeks, narrows the eyes, and creates crow's-feet wrinkles, and it is the famous ingredient of the enjoyment smile.

Action unit 7, the lid tightener, tenses the eyelids into a narrowed, hardened gaze, common in anger and in pain.

Action unit 5, the upper lid raiser, does the opposite: it pulls the upper eyelids up so that more white of the eye shows, producing the wide, staring eyes of fear and surprise.

Because the eye region is hard to control deliberately, these actions carry special weight when observers try to judge whether an expression is genuine.

5.2.3 Mouth actions: lip corner puller, lip corner depressor, lip press, jaw drop

Action unit 12, the lip corner puller, is the zygomaticus major at work, drawing the mouth corners up and back into the shape of a smile.

Action unit 15, the lip corner depressor, pulls the mouth corners downward, a core component of the sad face.

Action unit 24, the lip presser, squeezes the lips together without pulling them in, a movement typical of suppressed anger and firm self-control, while the related action unit 23, the lip tightener, narrows and tenses the red parts of the lips.

Action unit 26, the jaw drop, lets the mouth fall open in a relaxed way, and the stronger action unit 27 stretches the mouth wide open.

Mouth actions are the most numerous group in FACS, reflecting how mobile and expressive the human mouth is.

5.3 Using FACS

5.3.1 Coder training and reliability

Learning FACS is a serious undertaking: trainees typically study the manual and practice for on the order of one hundred hours before they can code accurately.

To become certified, a trainee must pass a standardized proficiency test in which their coding of test videos is compared against expert reference coding.

Reliability means the degree to which two independent coders watching the same face produce the same codes, and published evaluations have found good to excellent reliability for the occurrence, intensity, and timing of action units.

Coding is also slow, since a single minute of video can take an hour or more to code thoroughly, which is one motivation for the automated systems described below.

5.3.2 Prototype action-unit combinations proposed for each emotion

Researchers in the basic-emotions tradition proposed prototype combinations - standard recipes of action units - for each of the proposed universal emotions.

For example, the happiness prototype is action units 6 plus 12, the cheek raiser with the lip corner puller; the sadness prototype centers on 1 plus 4 plus 15; and the surprise prototype combines 1 plus 2 plus 5 plus 26.

A companion system called the Emotion Facial Action Coding System (EMFACS) lets coders score only the action units relevant to these emotion prototypes, saving time.

It is important to keep the logic straight: FACS itself is neutral, and the prototypes are hypotheses laid on top of it about which movement patterns go with which emotions.

Later chapters of this book examine how well those hypotheses have held up, and spontaneous everyday expressions often match the prototypes only partially.

5.3.3 Extensions: baby FACS and chimpanzee FACS

Because infant faces differ from adult faces in shape, fat, and skin texture, the developmental psychologist Harriet Oster created Baby FACS, an adaptation of the system for coding the faces of infants and young children.

Baby FACS keeps the anatomy-based logic but redefines the appearance changes a coder should look for on an infant face.

Similarly, ChimpFACS, developed by Lisa Parr, Bridget Waller, and colleagues, adapts the system to the chimpanzee face, whose different proportions and coloring change how the same muscle actions look.

Comparable systems now exist for several other species, including macaque monkeys, dogs, cats, and horses.

These extensions allow direct, movement-by-movement comparison of expressions across ages and species, which is essential for studying how expressions develop and how they evolved.

5.3.4 Automated action-unit detection by machine

Computer-vision researchers have built software that detects action units automatically from video, with widely used examples including the open-source tool OpenFace and commercial systems such as FaceReader.

These systems use machine learning, a family of methods in which a computer learns patterns from many labeled examples rather than following hand-written rules.

Automated coding is vastly faster than human coding and has made large-scale studies of facial behavior practical.

However, evaluations show real limits: accuracy drops when the head turns away from the camera, when lighting or image quality is poor, and for spontaneous everyday expressions compared with deliberately posed ones.

Automated detectors also cover only a subset of action units, so human FACS coders remain the reference standard against which the machines are judged.

Chapter 6: Neural Control of Facial Movement

A striking fact about the face is that the brain reaches it through more than one route.

This chapter explains the separate voluntary and emotional pathways to the facial muscles and what their differences reveal about genuine and posed expressions.

6.1 Two Pathways to the Face

6.1.1 The voluntary pathway: motor cortex and the pyramidal system

When you deliberately smile for a photograph, the command begins in the primary motor cortex, a strip of brain tissue on the outer surface of the brain that controls voluntary movement.

Signals travel from the motor cortex down through the pyramidal system, the major bundle of nerve fibers carrying voluntary movement commands from the cortex toward the brainstem and spinal cord.

For the face, these fibers end on the facial nucleus, the cluster of nerve cells in the brainstem whose outgoing fibers form the facial nerve described in Chapter 4.

This pathway gives us intentional control of the face - the ability to pose, mime, exaggerate, and suppress expressions on demand.

Its control is finest for the lower face, which fits with the mouth's role in speech.

6.1.2 The emotional pathway: subcortical circuits driving spontaneous expression

Spontaneous emotional expressions - the smile that breaks out at a joke, the grimace at a sudden pain - do not depend on the same route.

They are driven by a partly separate, evolutionarily older set of circuits involving emotion-related regions such as the cingulate cortex and insula, which are folds of cortex on the inner and hidden surfaces of the brain, together with structures beneath the cortex.

Subcortical simply means lying below the cerebral cortex, the brain's outer layer.

These emotional circuits converge on the same facial nucleus in the brainstem, so the two pathways share their final output but differ in where the command originates.

Because the emotional pathway bypasses deliberate control, spontaneous expressions can appear before we are aware of them and can be hard to fully suppress.

6.1.3 Clinical dissociations: volitional versus emotional facial paralysis

The clearest evidence for two pathways comes from patients in whom one works while the other fails, a pattern neurologists call a dissociation.

In volitional facial paresis - paresis means partial paralysis - damage to the motor cortex or its descending fibers leaves a patient unable to move one side of the face on command, yet the same side lights up normally in a spontaneous laugh.

In the rarer emotional facial paresis, the reverse occurs: the patient can move the face perfectly well on request, but that side stays still when a genuine emotion arrives.

Emotional facial paresis has been reported after damage to structures including the insula, the thalamus, and parts of the brainstem, sites that fit an emotional motor network distinct from the voluntary one.

The Norwegian neurologist Georg Monrad-Krohn described these dissociations in the early twentieth century, and they remain textbook proof that smiling on purpose and smiling with feeling use different wiring.

6.2 Subcortical Drivers

6.2.1 The amygdala and rapid emotional expression

The amygdala is an almond-shaped cluster of nerve cells deep in each temporal lobe, the brain region behind the temples, and it is central to detecting threats and tagging events as emotionally important.

Through its connections to the brainstem and to emotional motor areas, the amygdala helps translate a sudden fright or an emotionally charged sight into an immediate facial response.

Anatomical work in primates has traced pathways by which the amygdala can influence the facial nucleus, supporting its role in fast, unplanned expression.

The amygdala is best understood as a rapid appraiser and trigger, rather than as a store of ready-made expressions.

Its contribution to perceiving expressions on other people's faces is a separate story told in Part III of this book.

6.2.2 The basal ganglia and expressive spontaneity

The basal ganglia are a group of interconnected structures deep in the brain that help select, start, and scale movements, working closely with the chemical messenger dopamine.

Their importance for the face shows most clearly in Parkinson's disease, a condition in which dopamine-producing cells die off.

Many people with Parkinson's disease develop hypomimia, often called masked face - a marked reduction in spontaneous facial expression, even though they still feel emotions and can often pose expressions when asked.

This tells us the basal ganglia are part of the machinery that lets felt emotion flow out onto the face automatically.

Conversely, some conditions involving basal ganglia dysfunction can produce excessive or poorly controlled facial movements, underlining their role in balancing facial output.

6.2.3 Brainstem pattern generators for laughing and crying

A pattern generator is a circuit of nerve cells that can produce a complete, coordinated sequence of movements once triggered, the way a music box plays its tune when released.

Laughing and crying appear to depend on such circuits in the brainstem, which coordinate the face, breathing, and voice into their familiar rhythmic patterns.

Regions including the periaqueductal gray, a core of tissue in the midbrain, help trigger these programs, while pathways from the frontal lobes normally hold them in check.

Evidence comes from pathological laughing and crying, a disorder seen in some neurological diseases in which patients burst into laughter or tears that are out of proportion to, or even unrelated to, what they feel.

The standard interpretation is that damage has weakened the brain's inhibitory control over intact brainstem programs, so the programs fire too easily.

6.3 Genuine Versus Posed Expressions

6.3.1 The Duchenne smile: the eye-crinkling marker of enjoyment

In the 1860s the French physician Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne, using mild electrical stimulation of facial muscles, observed that a smile of true enjoyment involves not only the mouth but also the muscle around the eyes.

In FACS terms, this enjoyment smile combines action unit 12, the lip corner puller, with action unit 6, the cheek raiser, which narrows the eyes and creates crow's-feet wrinkles.

Paul Ekman later named this combination the Duchenne smile in his honor.

Studies have linked Duchenne smiles to self-reported enjoyment, to pleasant situations, and to patterns of brain activity associated with positive emotion.

The Duchenne marker became one of the most cited findings in emotion science, though, as we will see, its reliability as a lie detector for smiles has been qualified by later work.

6.3.2 Why deliberate smiles usually miss the eye muscles

The outer ring of the orbicularis oculi is difficult for most people to contract on purpose - Duchenne himself remarked that it does not obey the will and is set in play only by genuine feeling.

The voluntary cortical pathway commands the mouth region much more precisely than the eye region, so a posed smile typically recruits the lip corner puller strongly while leaving the eye muscles quiet.

The result is the familiar say-cheese smile: an upturned mouth beneath eyes that remain wide and uncrinkled.

Observers are sensitive to this mismatch, and faces smiling without eye involvement are often judged as less warm and less sincere.

The asymmetry of control - easy mouth, difficult eyes - is a direct behavioral echo of the two neural pathways described earlier in this chapter.

6.3.3 Deliberate Duchenne smiles: individual differences in expressive control

The Duchenne marker is not an infallible seal of authenticity, because some people can contract the eye muscles voluntarily.

Studies asking people to pose smiles find that a substantial minority, and by some estimates the majority under favorable conditions, can produce a deliberate Duchenne smile.

Skilled social performers, actors among them, may achieve the eye crinkle by method-like means, such as summoning a happy memory so that a real trace of emotion drives the muscle.

Some researchers have also argued that the Duchenne marker tracks the intensity of a smile as much as its sincerity, since very strong mouth movements mechanically push the cheeks up toward the eyes.

The fair summary is that eye involvement makes genuine enjoyment more likely but does not guarantee it, and its absence is a stronger clue than its presence.

6.3.4 Timing signatures: onset, apex, and offset differences

An expression unfolds in time through three phases: the onset, when the movement builds; the apex, when it holds at its peak; and the offset, when it fades away.

Spontaneous smiles tend to have smooth, gradual onsets, longer-held apexes, and gentle, gradual offsets.

Posed smiles, by contrast, often switch on abruptly, peak briefly, and drop off the face suddenly, and they are more likely to be noticeably asymmetric, stronger on one side than the other.

Measurements of lip-corner movement confirm that genuine smiles have longer onset, apex, offset, and total durations than posed ones.

Because timing is hard to fake and hard to judge from a single snapshot, dynamic information of this kind is often a better guide to authenticity than any frozen feature, a theme that returns when we discuss perception in Part III.

Chapter 7: The Expressions Themselves

With the muscular alphabet and the neural control systems in place, we can now describe the expressions themselves.

This chapter walks through the classic proposed basic expressions, the candidates beyond them, the functional logic of their shapes, and the surprisingly varied family of smiles.

7.1 The Proposed Basic Expressions

7.1.1 Happiness: the smile family

The prototypical happy expression is the enjoyment smile: the lip corners pull up and back through action unit 12, the cheeks rise and the eyes narrow through action unit 6, and the lips often part slightly.

Of all expressions, the smile is the most easily recognized, and observers across many cultures identify it at very high rates.

Yet the smile is better thought of as a family than a single signal, because people also smile in politeness, appeasement, embarrassment, and even discomfort.

The word happiness itself covers a range of states - amusement, contentment, relief, pride, sensory pleasure - that may share the smile while differing in other channels such as the voice.

Section 7.4 returns to the smile family in detail.

7.1.2 Sadness: brow, lip corner, and gaze changes

The prototypical sad face combines the inner brow raiser, action unit 1, with the brow lowerer, action unit 4, producing obliquely slanted brows with a pucker between them.

The lip corners pull down through action unit 15, and the chin may push the lower lip up into a slight pout through the mentalis muscle.

The eyelids often droop, the gaze falls, and the whole face loses muscle tone, matching the slowed, de-energized quality of sadness itself.

The oblique brow configuration is particularly hard to produce deliberately, which is why genuine grief is difficult to counterfeit convincingly.

Sadness signals loss and a need for comfort, and its display reliably draws sympathy and helping from observers.

7.1.3 Anger: lowered brows, glare, and pressed lips

In the prototypical angry face, the brows pull down and together through action unit 4, the upper eyelids lift through action unit 5, and the lower lids tense through action unit 7, producing a fixed, penetrating glare.

The lips either press tightly together through actions 23 and 24 or part to bare the teeth, as in shouting.

The jaw may thrust forward, and the nostrils may flare.

The combination of lowered brows with wide, tense eyes is distinctive: the brows signal opposition while the eyes stay locked on the target.

Anger displays communicate threat and a readiness to act, and even young children and members of very different cultures read the lowered-brow glare as hostility.

7.1.4 Fear: raised brows, widened eyes, stretched mouth

The prototypical fear face raises the brows through action units 1 and 2 while also drawing them together through action unit 4, giving a raised but flattened, tense brow.

The upper eyelids lift strongly through action unit 5, exposing white sclera above the iris, while the lower lids tense.

The mouth opens and the lip corners stretch straight sideways through action unit 20, a horizontal stretch quite different from a smile's upward pull.

Fear is often confused with surprise by observers, which makes sense because the two expressions share raised brows and widened eyes.

The tell-tale differences are the brow pull of action unit 4 and the sideways lip stretch, both present in fear but absent in pure surprise.

7.1.5 Disgust: nose wrinkle and raised upper lip

The prototypical disgust face wrinkles the nose through action unit 9 and raises the upper lip through action unit 10, as if recoiling from a foul smell or taste.

The cheeks rise, the lower lip may push up or protrude, and in strong disgust the tongue may show, as if expelling something from the mouth.

Disgust plausibly began as a food-rejection response - the facial movements literally narrow the nostrils and guard the mouth - and was later extended to contamination, disease cues, and even moral offenses.

People describing moral disgust at cheating or cruelty show traces of the same nose-and-lip configuration, a striking case of an old bodily reflex recruited for social judgment.

7.1.6 Surprise: raised brows, wide eyes, dropped jaw

The prototypical surprise face lifts the brows fully through action units 1 and 2, curving them high and creating horizontal forehead furrows.

The eyes open very wide through action unit 5, and the jaw drops open loosely through action units 25 and 26, without any tension in the lips.

Surprise is the briefest of the proposed basic expressions, typically lasting well under a second before it gives way to whatever emotion follows - delight, fear, confusion, or amusement.

Some theorists question whether surprise is an emotion at all, since it carries no fixed pleasantness or unpleasantness of its own; it is better described as the face of sudden, unexpected information.

Its wide eyes and open airway fit a body preparing to take in as much new information as possible, a point developed in section 7.3.

7.2 Beyond the Basic Six

7.2.1 Contempt and the unilateral lip corner

Contempt is the feeling of looking down on someone, of regarding another person or their behavior as beneath one's standards.

Its proposed expression is unusual because it is unilateral, meaning it occurs on one side of the face only: one lip corner tightens and pulls up, through action unit 14, the dimpler, or a one-sided action unit 12, producing a slight smirk.

The head may tilt back so that the expresser literally looks down the nose at the target.

Ekman and Friesen argued in the 1980s that this expression is recognized across cultures and proposed contempt as an addition to the basic six, but recognition rates have generally been lower and more variable than for the classic expressions, so its status remains debated.

The one-sided smirk is nonetheless one of the most reliably noticed social signals in close relationships, where it is read as disrespect.

7.2.2 Embarrassment, shame, and pride displays

Embarrassment, shame, and pride are called self-conscious emotions because they depend on evaluating oneself through the eyes of others.

Dacher Keltner's research showed that embarrassment has a recognizable display that unfolds over a couple of seconds: gaze drops, the head turns down and away, a controlled, sheepish smile appears, and the person may touch their face.

Shame shows a simpler, more collapsed pattern - head down, gaze down, slumped posture - which observers across cultures, including congenitally blind athletes who could never have seen it, produce after defeat.

Pride, studied extensively by Jessica Tracy, is largely a posture display: the head tilts back, the chest expands, the body straightens, and the arms rise or rest on the hips, usually with a small smile.

These displays show that recognizable emotional expression is not confined to the face alone; the head, gaze, and body carry essential parts of the signal.

7.2.3 Interest, awe, and amusement

Researchers studying positive emotions argue that the single label happiness hides several distinct states with partly distinct displays.

Interest shows in stilled attention, slightly knitted or raised brows, forward head orientation, and steady gaze, the face of a person absorbed in something.

Awe - the feeling evoked by vast things such as grand landscapes or great ideas - has been described with raised inner brows, widened eyes, a dropped jaw, and an intake of breath.

Amusement is signaled less by any static face than by laughter, with the head tossing back, the eyes crinkling, and the mouth opening.

Recognition of these positive displays is generally above chance but weaker and more context-dependent than for the classic six, so they are best regarded as promising candidates rather than settled members of any universal list.

7.2.4 Pain expression and its distinct signature

Pain is a sensation rather than a classic emotion, but it has a facial signature consistent enough that clinicians use it to assess patients who cannot speak, such as infants and people with dementia.

The core pain face combines brow lowering through action unit 4, cheek raising and eye tightening through action units 6 and 7, nose wrinkling and upper lip raising through action units 9 and 10, and eye closure.

This pattern differs from every proposed emotional expression, supporting the idea that the pain face is a distinct signal rather than a blend of fear and disgust.

Standardized tools based on it, such as coding systems for neonatal and infant pain, are used in hospitals to score pain from the face.

The pain face both protects - squeezing the eyes shut and shielding the head - and communicates, drawing caregiving attention from others.

7.3 The Functional Origins of Expression Shapes

7.3.1 Sensory regulation: expressions as opposites (fear widens, disgust narrows)

Charles Darwin proposed in 1872 that expressions began not as messages but as practical actions serving the body, and modern work has tested this idea directly.

In a 2008 study in the journal Nature Neuroscience, Joshua Susskind and colleagues showed statistically that the fear face and the disgust face are close to geometric opposites: fear opens and expands the features while disgust closes and compresses them.

When volunteers posed fear, they reported a wider visual field, moved their eyes faster to find targets, and drew in more air through expanded nasal passages.

When they posed disgust, all these measures moved the opposite way, restricting what came in through the eyes and nose.

The conclusion is that fear configures the face to gather more information from a threatening world, while disgust configures it to shut out something noxious - sensory regulation first, signaling second.

7.3.2 Expression shapes as evolved preparation for action

On this functional view, each expression's shape makes sense as a fragment of preparation for a specific kind of action.

The fear face's wide eyes and open airway prepare vigilance and rapid breathing for escape; the anger face's lowered brows and tensed lids protect the eyes while keeping them fixed on an opponent.

The disgust face's wrinkled nose and raised lip guard the airways and mouth against contamination, and the surprise face maximizes intake of unexpected information.

Once such action fragments existed, evolution could refine them into signals, because observers who could read the fragments gained an advantage in predicting others' behavior.

Biologists call this process ritualization: a practical movement becomes exaggerated, stereotyped, and increasingly communicative over evolutionary time.

This account explains why expressions have the particular shapes they do, something a purely arbitrary signaling system would leave unexplained.

7.3.3 Blends, mixed expressions, and compound emotions

Real emotional life rarely serves emotions one at a time, and faces reflect this by blending components of different expressions.

A blend combines action units from two expressions - raised fearful brows over a social smile, for example, in nervous amusement.

Research by Shichuan Du and Aleix Martinez, published in 2014, systematically photographed and analyzed such combinations and identified around twenty-one recognizable categories they called compound expressions, including happily surprised, happily disgusted, and sadly angry.

Their analyses showed that compounds are produced in lawful ways: when two component action units are physically incompatible, such as pressing the lips and parting them, one is consistently dropped.

The existence of reliable compounds suggests the face can convey a considerably richer set of states than the basic six, while still building everything from the same action-unit alphabet.

7.4 Smiles as a Family of Signals

7.4.1 Reward smiles, affiliation smiles, and dominance smiles

Paula Niedenthal, Magdalena Rychlowska, Jared Martin, and colleagues have proposed that smiles solve three fundamental social tasks and therefore come in three functional types.

Reward smiles express and reinforce pleasure - they are the smiles of genuine enjoyment, typically symmetrical, often with eye crinkling and sometimes raised brows, and they reward the people and behaviors that produced them.

Affiliation smiles signal harmlessness and social connection; they tend to involve a gentler lip-corner pull, often with pressed lips and without teeth, and they smooth interactions, appease, and reassure.

Dominance smiles assert superiority; they are more asymmetric and can mix in nose wrinkling or upper-lip raising, giving a smug or scornful cast.

Studies suggest observers can distinguish these types, and that they even have different physiological effects on receivers, with dominance smiles raising stress hormone responses more than affiliation smiles.

7.4.2 The polite social smile

The polite smile is the everyday workhorse of the smile family - the smile given to a colleague in the corridor or a stranger holding a door.

Structurally it is usually a low-intensity action unit 12 with little or no eye involvement, brief and symmetric, switching on and off relatively quickly.

It does not claim strong inner feeling, and both parties usually know it; its job is to acknowledge, to lubricate, and to signal the absence of hostility.

Cultures differ markedly in the settings where polite smiling is expected, from service encounters to greetings between strangers, and these display rules are learned in childhood.

Far from being a fake version of a real smile, the polite smile is better understood as a distinct, honest signal of social intent rather than of emotion.

7.4.3 Laughter and its vocal-facial coupling

Laughter is a whole-body event in which the face is tightly coupled to the voice and the breathing apparatus.

During laughter, the lip corners pull up and the eyes crinkle while the chest and diaphragm produce the rhythmic, staccato exhalations - the ha-ha-ha - that give laughter its sound.

This coupling is coordinated by the brainstem pattern generators described in Chapter 6, which is why genuine laughter is so hard to perform convincingly on demand.

Laughter is deeply social: people laugh many times more often in company than alone, and most everyday laughter follows ordinary remarks rather than jokes.

Researchers extending the functional-smile framework have found that laughter, like smiles, comes in reward, affiliation, and dominance variants that listeners can tell apart from acoustic features alone.

Listeners can also distinguish spontaneous laughter from volitional, produced-on-purpose laughter, making the genuine-versus-posed distinction of this chapter audible as well as visible.

PART III: THE PERCEIVING BRAIN: READING FACES

Chapter 8: The Face Perception System

The human brain treats faces as special objects, and it devotes a whole network of regions to detecting them, identifying them, and reading their expressions.

This chapter tours that network, from the first fast glance to the slower, finer analysis that tells you exactly who is looking at you and how they feel.

8.1 The Core Face Network

8.1.1 The occipital face area: early face detection

At the back of the brain, in the occipital lobe (the brain's main vision area), sits a small patch called the occipital face area (OFA).

In an influential model proposed by the neuroscientist James Haxby and colleagues in the year 2000, the OFA is the entry point of the core face network, the trio of regions that handles the basic visual analysis of faces.

The OFA responds to the simple physical parts of a face - the eyes, the nose, the mouth - and does so very early in processing, within a fraction of a second of a face appearing.

Think of it as a doorman whose job is to say, quickly and roughly, that a face is present and to pass its parts along for closer inspection.

The OFA then sends its output forward to the two more specialized regions described next.

8.1.2 The fusiform face area: identity processing

The fusiform face area (FFA) is a region on the underside of the temporal lobe, in a fold of brain tissue called the fusiform gyrus.

It was described in detail by the neuroscientist Nancy Kanwisher and colleagues in 1997, and it responds far more strongly to faces than to houses, hands, or other objects.

In the Haxby model, the FFA specializes in the unchanging, or invariant, aspects of a face - the stable structure that tells you this is your friend and not a stranger, no matter what expression the face is wearing.

Damage to this region and its neighbors can produce prosopagnosia, a condition often called face blindness, in which people can see a face perfectly well but cannot tell whose face it is.

The FFA matters for our story because recognizing who is expressing something is often the first step in deciding what the expression means for you.

8.1.3 The superior temporal sulcus: movement and expression processing

The superior temporal sulcus (STS) is a long groove running along the side of the brain, and its rear portion is the third member of the core face network.

Where the FFA cares about the stable structure of a face, the STS cares about the changeable aspects - eye gaze shifts, lip movements, and the fleeting muscle patterns that make up expressions.

If a friend's eyes dart to the left or her mouth curls into a smirk, the STS is the region tracking those movements.

This division of labor is elegant: one stream answers the question of who the face belongs to, while the other answers the question of what the face is doing right now.

Later research has shown the two streams talk to each other more than the original model assumed, but the basic split between identity processing and expression processing has held up well.

8.2 The Extended Network

8.2.1 The amygdala: emotional significance and fear detection

Beyond the core network lies an extended network of regions that attach meaning to what the face is doing, and its most famous member is the amygdala, an almond shaped cluster of cells deep in each temporal lobe.

The amygdala responds strongly to emotionally significant faces, and it responds especially reliably to expressions of fear.

A striking case is a patient known by the initials SM, who lost both amygdalae to a rare disease and became selectively poor at recognizing fear in faces.

Research by the neuroscientist Ralph Adolphs showed why: without her amygdalae, SM stopped spontaneously looking at the eye region of faces, and wide, white rimmed eyes are the single most informative cue for fear.

Remarkably, when experimenters simply instructed her to look at the eyes, her fear recognition returned to normal, suggesting the amygdala's job is partly to steer our attention toward the most emotionally informative parts of a face.

8.2.2 The insula: disgust perception

The insula is a sheet of cortex folded deep inside the side of the brain, hidden from view unless you pull the surrounding lobes apart.

It monitors the internal state of the body - nausea, heartbeat, gut feelings - a function scientists call interoception, meaning the sense of the body's inner condition.

Brain imaging studies have repeatedly found that the front part of the insula activates both when people feel disgust themselves and when they merely watch someone else's disgusted face.

Patients with damage to the insula can show a selective problem recognizing disgust in others, much as amygdala patients show a selective problem with fear.

This pairing of feeling and perceiving in one region is a first hint of a theme we will meet again: the brain may understand other people's expressions partly by quietly recreating the matching feeling in itself.

8.2.3 The orbitofrontal cortex: value and social judgment

The orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) is the strip of frontal lobe sitting just above the eye sockets, and it is the extended network's evaluator.

It computes value - how rewarding, punishing, or socially important something is - and it applies that computation to faces.

Imaging studies link the OFC to judging the meaning of expressions such as anger, and to deciding whether a face invites approach or warns you to keep your distance.

The OFC works in a tight loop with the amygdala, and the flow of signals between the two regions changes depending on whether a face is friendly, neutral, or threatening.

People with OFC damage can struggle with exactly this kind of social judgment, misreading faces in ways that lead to awkward or risky social decisions.

8.3 Fast and Coarse Versus Slow and Fine

8.3.1 Rapid subcortical detection of threat faces

A long standing idea in neuroscience is that the brain has a quick and dirty shortcut for spotting danger, sometimes nicknamed the low road.

On this account, crude visual information travels from the eye to the superior colliculus (a small visual structure in the midbrain) and then to the pulvinar (a relay station in the thalamus, the brain's central switchboard), and from there straight to the amygdala.

Because this route skips the cortex, the brain's outer sheet where detailed conscious vision happens, it could in principle flag a fearful or threatening face in a few tens of milliseconds.

Experiments seem to support something like this: fearful faces can nudge amygdala activity even when they are flashed too briefly to be consciously seen.

The appeal of the idea is evolutionary - a system that reacts to possible threats before you have finished perceiving them would have kept our ancestors alive.

8.3.2 Cortical elaboration and conscious recognition

The shortcut, if it exists, is fast but blurry, so the fine grained work of recognizing exactly which expression you are seeing falls to the slower cortical route, sometimes called the high road.

Signals travel from the eyes through the primary visual cortex at the back of the brain, then forward through the occipital face area, the fusiform face area, and the superior temporal sulcus described earlier.

This route takes longer, on the order of one to two tenths of a second, but it delivers the sharp, detailed image needed to tell a fearful face from a surprised one, or a polite smile from a delighted one.

It is also the route associated with conscious experience: this is the processing that lets you know you are seeing a face and reflect on what it means.

The two routes are best thought of as teammates, with the fast system priming the body and steering attention while the slow system fills in the details and supports deliberate judgment.

8.3.3 Debates over the subcortical shortcut

The low road story is appealing, but honesty requires saying that it is genuinely contested.

In influential critiques published in 2010 and 2011, the neuroscientists Luiz Pessoa and Ralph Adolphs argued that the pulvinar is not a simple relay: it is densely wired to the cortex, so a supposedly subcortical route may in fact be shaped by cortical signals, and they proposed a many roads view in which threat information reaches the amygdala along multiple fast cortical pathways.

Others have pushed back with newer evidence: a study of 622 people from the Human Connectome Project, a large brain mapping effort, found an actual fiber pathway from the pulvinar to the amygdala, and people with a denser pathway were better at recognizing fearful faces.

Recordings in monkeys have also found very fast amygdala responses to threatening faces consistent with a subcortical input.

The fair summary today is that a fast pathway of some kind likely exists, but it is probably neither fully independent of the cortex nor limited to fear, and the strong version of the low road story is no longer widely accepted.

Chapter 9: How Recognition Works

Knowing which brain regions light up is not the same as knowing how recognition actually works.

This chapter looks at the perceptual tricks, mental categories, and bodily echoes that turn a pattern of light on the retina into the judgment that someone is afraid, delighted, or disgusted.

9.1 Perceptual Mechanisms

9.1.1 Featural versus configural (whole-face) processing

Psychologists distinguish two broad ways of analyzing a face.

Featural processing means reading individual parts one at a time - these eyes, that mouth - while configural processing means reading the spatial arrangement of the parts as a whole, such as the distance between the eyes and the mouth.

A classic demonstration that wholes matter is the composite effect: when the top half of one expression is aligned with the bottom half of another, the two halves fuse into a new whole and become hard to judge separately, but misaligning the halves breaks the fusion.

Turning a face upside down also selectively disrupts configural processing, which is why inverted faces are so oddly hard to read.

Research suggests both modes contribute to expression recognition, with the balance shifting by emotion: happiness can often be read from a single feature, the smiling mouth, while emotions such as sadness and anger depend more on the overall configuration.

9.1.2 Diagnostic regions: eyes for fear, mouth for happiness

Not all parts of a face are equally informative, and which part is most diagnostic depends on the emotion.

Studies that reveal only small patches of a face at a time, using a technique called Bubbles, show that observers rely on the wide open eyes to recognize fear and on the mouth to recognize happiness.

This is exactly why patient SM, introduced in the last chapter, was impaired at fear in particular: her amygdala damage stopped her from looking at eyes, and eyes are where fear lives.

Disgust tends to be carried by the nose wrinkle and raised upper lip, anger by the lowered brows and hardened eyes, and surprise by a mix of raised brows and dropped jaw.

A practical consequence appeared during the coronavirus disease (COVID) pandemic: face masks that cover the mouth hurt recognition of happiness and disgust far more than recognition of fear or anger.

9.1.3 Dynamic information: why moving faces are easier to read

Most laboratory research has used still photographs, but real expressions are movies, not snapshots.

Moving faces are generally easier to read than static ones, especially when the expression is subtle or the image is degraded.

Motion helps for several reasons: it shows the order in which facial actions unfold, it makes the changing regions pop out from the unchanging ones, and it carries timing cues, such as the slow bloom of a genuine smile versus the abrupt switch of a posed one.

People also rate dynamic expressions as more intense and emotionally arousing than the same expressions shown as stills.

The superior temporal sulcus, the motion loving member of the core face network, is especially engaged by these dynamic signals.

9.2 Categorical and Dimensional Perception

9.2.1 Categorical perception of expression morphs

Computers can blend, or morph, a photograph of a happy face into a photograph of a sad face in a series of small equal steps.

Perception, it turns out, does not treat those steps equally: in classic experiments by Nancy Etcoff and John Magee in 1992, observers saw everything on one side of a boundary as happy and everything on the other side as sad, with a sharp switch in between.

They were also better at telling apart two morphs that straddled the boundary than two morphs that sat the same physical distance apart within a category.

This pattern - sharp boundaries and better discrimination across them - is called categorical perception, and it also occurs for speech sounds and colors.

Andrew Calder, David Perrett, and colleagues later replicated the effect with photographic quality morphs, showing that the mind carves the smooth continuum of possible faces into discrete emotional categories.

9.2.2 Valence and arousal as underlying dimensions

Categories are not the whole story, because expressions also vary along smooth underlying dimensions.

The psychologist James Russell proposed a circumplex model, a circular map of feeling built on two axes: valence, meaning how pleasant or unpleasant something is, and arousal, meaning how activated or calmed the body is.

On this map, excitement is pleasant and high arousal, contentment is pleasant and low arousal, fear is unpleasant and high arousal, and sadness is unpleasant and low arousal.

Perceivers do seem to extract these dimensions: when people freely sort or rate faces, similar valence and similar arousal faces cluster together, and confusions between expressions, such as fear and surprise, happen mostly between neighbors on the map.

Brain imaging work suggests the brain represents both formats, holding continuous dimension like information alongside sharper category like information, so the categorical and dimensional views are best seen as complementary rather than rivals.

9.2.3 Context effects: the same face reads differently in different scenes

For most of the twentieth century, researchers assumed the face speaks for itself, but experiments by Hillel Aviezer and colleagues showed otherwise.

When they placed the photograph of a prototypical disgust face onto a body posed in anger, observers overwhelmingly reported that the face itself looked angry, and eye tracking showed that the body context even changed where on the face people looked.

The effect held when participants were explicitly told to ignore the body, suggesting the blending of face and context is automatic rather than a deliberate guess.

Background scenes, spoken descriptions, other nearby faces, and cultural expectations all shift face reading in similar ways.

The modern conclusion is that a facial expression is one strong cue among several, and the emotion you perceive is the brain's best joint interpretation of face, body, and situation together.

9.3 Simulation and Mimicry in Perception

9.3.1 Facial mimicry: automatically echoing observed expressions

When you watch an emotional face, your own face quietly answers it.

Electromyography (EMG), a technique that records electrical activity in muscles through small sensors on the skin, shows that viewing a smile produces tiny activations in the observer's own smiling muscle, the zygomaticus major, while viewing an angry face activates the brow knitting muscle, the corrugator supercilii.

These echoes appear within a few hundred milliseconds, are usually too small to see with the naked eye, and occur even when the faces are flashed too quickly for conscious recognition.

This automatic copying is called facial mimicry.

Mimicry is socially tuned rather than purely reflexive: people mimic friends and liked group members more than rivals, which suggests it is part of how we affiliate, not just a motor reflex.

9.3.2 Sensorimotor simulation accounts of recognition

Why would the perceiving face copy the perceived one?

Sensorimotor simulation theories propose that the brain understands an expression by partially recreating it - running the observed facial pattern through its own motor and body sensing systems and reading off the associated feeling, a bit like understanding a dance step by marking it with your own body.

Supporting evidence includes the discovery of mirror neurons, brain cells in monkeys that fire both when the animal performs an action and when it watches another perform it, and human imaging studies showing overlapping activity for feeling disgust and seeing disgust in the insula.

Experiments that block mimicry, for example by having people hold a pen sideways in their mouth or by applying gentle electrical or gel based interference to facial muscles, sometimes find small drops in recognition accuracy or speed, especially for subtle or ambiguous expressions.

The theory is attractive because it links perception, the body, and empathy in one mechanism, but as the next section shows, the strong version of the claim runs into trouble.

9.3.3 Evidence against necessity: recognition without mimicry in facial paralysis

Moebius syndrome is a rare condition present from birth in which the nerves controlling the facial muscles do not develop, leaving the face largely or completely paralyzed.

If copying an expression with your own face were necessary for recognizing it, people with Moebius syndrome should be poor at reading faces.

They are not: in the largest study to date, 37 adults with Moebius syndrome recognized facial expressions just as accurately as 37 matched control participants, and accuracy was unrelated to how much facial movement a person could produce.

Brain recording studies suggest people with congenital facial paralysis may develop an alternative processing route that leans on visual analysis rather than motor simulation.

The balanced verdict is that mimicry and simulation are one useful route to recognition, perhaps sharpening it at the margins, but they are not a requirement, because the brain has other roads to the same destination.

9.4 Individual Differences in Reading Faces

9.4.1 Emotion recognition ability and its measurement

People differ substantially and stably in how well they read faces, and psychologists treat this as a measurable ability, much like memory span or vocabulary.

Standard tests show photographs or videos of expressions and ask the test taker either to label each one, to match faces showing the same emotion, or to spot the odd one out.

Well constructed tests of this kind are reliable, meaning a person's score is similar from one testing occasion to the next, and scores in the general population spread across a wide range.

Face based scores correlate moderately with the ability to hear emotion in voices, suggesting a partly general skill for reading emotion across channels.

Emotion recognition ability also relates, modestly, to broader social outcomes such as relationship quality and workplace social effectiveness, which is why it forms a core piece of most tests of emotional intelligence, the broader capacity to perceive, understand, and manage emotion.

9.4.2 Sex, age, and personality differences

Averaged over many studies, women score slightly higher than men on facial emotion recognition tests, a real but small advantage that appears across cultures and across most emotions.

One proposed explanation is that women, on average, report richer awareness of their own emotional states, and this awareness statistically accounts for part of the recognition advantage.

Age matters too: recognition ability rises through childhood, peaks roughly between ages fifteen and thirty, and then declines gradually, with older adults finding negative expressions such as fear, anger, and sadness hardest.

Personality plays a smaller role, with traits such as extraversion and empathy showing modest positive links to recognition skill.

All of these are differences between group averages with heavily overlapping distributions, so they say very little about any particular individual.

9.4.3 Training and improvement of recognition skill

Emotion recognition is a skill, and like most skills it responds to practice.

Training programs typically show learners many examples of expressions, ask for a judgment, and then give immediate feedback, sometimes adding explicit instruction about diagnostic cues such as the fear signature in the eyes.

Reviews of such programs find genuine improvement on the trained tasks, with the honest caveats that gains are usually moderate, transfer to everyday social life is less well demonstrated, and long term durability is understudied.

Micro expression training, which claims to teach detection of very brief leaked expressions, is popular in security and business settings, but independent evaluations of its real world value remain thin.

Clinically, recognition training is used with encouraging results in conditions where face reading is often harder, including autism spectrum condition and schizophrenia, and the case of patient SM shows that even a simple instruction about where to look can restore performance.

Chapter 10: The Face and the Feeling: Feedback Loops

So far the traffic has run one way, from someone else's face into your brain.

This chapter reverses the arrow and asks whether your own face can talk back to your feelings, and how expressions leap from face to face through groups, crowds, and screens.

10.1 The Facial Feedback Hypothesis

10.1.1 The claim: making an expression influences the felt emotion

The facial feedback hypothesis is the claim that the position of your facial muscles feeds back into your emotional state, so that smiling can nudge you toward feeling pleasant and frowning toward feeling unpleasant.

The idea is old: Charles Darwin suggested in 1872 that freely expressing an emotion intensifies it, and the psychologist William James went further, arguing that bodily changes are not just consequences of emotion but part of its very substance.

Note what the hypothesis does not claim: no serious version says that forcing a smile can cure sadness or override real circumstances.

The claim is only that facial posture is one input, among many, that the brain consults when constructing how you feel.

Because the claim is easy to test - just arrange people's faces and measure their feelings - it became one of the most studied ideas in emotion science, and one of the most fought over.

10.1.2 The pen-in-teeth studies and the replication crisis

In a famous 1988 experiment, Fritz Strack and colleagues had people hold a pen either between their teeth, which forces the face into a smile shape, or between their lips, which prevents smiling, while rating how funny cartoons were.

The cover story hid the purpose, participants were told it was a study of writing without hands, and the teeth group rated the cartoons as funnier, seemingly confirming facial feedback without anyone knowing they were smiling.

The result entered textbooks, but in 2016 a registered replication report, a project in which many laboratories run the identical preregistered experiment, put it to the test.

Seventeen laboratories testing nearly 1,900 participants found essentially nothing: the original difference of 0.82 rating points shrank to 0.03, statistically indistinguishable from zero.

Debate followed about details, including the suggestion that being filmed in the replications may have dampened the effect, but the episode became a symbol of the replication crisis, the broader discovery that many celebrated psychology findings were built on samples too small to be trusted.

10.1.3 The current verdict: small but real effects in large collaborations

The story did not end with the failed replication.

In 2019, Nicholas Coles and colleagues published a meta-analysis, a statistical method for combining results across many studies, covering 138 facial feedback experiments, and found a real but small overall effect on reported feelings.

Then in 2022 the Many Smiles Collaboration, a multi laboratory project involving thousands of participants across nineteen countries and including both proponents and skeptics of the hypothesis, ran a carefully designed new test.

It found that deliberately posing a smile, whether by imitating a photograph or by consciously moving the muscles, produced a modest but reliable increase in reported happiness, while the pen in mouth version of the manipulation produced little or nothing.

The fair current verdict is that facial feedback exists but is weak: your face can gently tilt your feelings, the specific pen in teeth demonstration was oversold, and the effect is far too small to serve as a therapy on its own.

10.2 Mechanisms of Feedback

10.2.1 Muscle and skin signals returning to the brain

How could a facial pose reach your feelings at all?

Several routes are plausible.

Proprioception, the body's sense of its own muscle and joint positions, along with stretch signals from facial skin, travels back to the brain mainly through the trigeminal nerve, the large nerve serving the face, giving the brain a continuous report of the face's configuration.

The brain can then use that report as evidence when it constructs an emotional state, roughly reasoning that if the face is arranged for smiling, the situation is probably pleasant.

A more exotic proposal, the vascular theory, suggests facial muscle patterns slightly alter blood flow and thereby brain temperature, though this idea has much weaker support.

Whatever the exact channel, brain imaging shows the machinery is real: changing what the face can do measurably changes activity in emotion regions such as the amygdala, as the botulinum toxin studies below make clear.

10.2.2 Expression suppression and its costs

If feedback from the face matters, then deliberately freezing your face should have consequences, and it does.

Psychologists call the strategy expressive suppression: keeping your face still while feeling an emotion, the classic poker face.

Experiments by James Gross and colleagues show that suppression barely reduces the felt emotion itself, but it carries costs: it increases activation of the sympathetic nervous system, the body's fight or flight branch, and it impairs memory for what happened during the suppression.

The costs are social as well - conversation partners of suppressors feel less rapport and even show raised blood pressure, apparently because a frozen face starves the interaction of feedback.

By contrast, reappraisal, which means changing how you think about a situation before the emotion fully forms, reduces the feeling itself without these side effects, which is why psychologists generally consider it the healthier habitual strategy.

10.2.3 Botulinum toxin studies: paralyzed muscles and blunted feeling

Botulinum toxin, sold under the brand name Botox, smooths wrinkles by temporarily paralyzing small facial muscles, and in doing so it silences part of the face to brain feedback channel - creating an accidental natural experiment.

Brain imaging studies have found that after the frown muscles between the eyebrows are paralyzed, amygdala responses to emotional faces are altered, and the changes reverse as the toxin wears off, tying the effect directly to the missing muscle feedback.

Behavioral studies report that treated individuals show slower reading of emotional sentences and, in some studies, slightly blunted or slowed recognition of emotional expressions, consistent with a simulation role for the facial muscles.

Intriguingly, several clinical trials have found that paralyzing the frown muscles reduces symptoms in some people with depression, though the evidence is mixed and this remains an experimental idea, not an accepted treatment.

These studies are typically small and often lack ideal control groups, so they are best read as a suggestive package pointing the same way as the feedback hypothesis rather than as definitive proof.

10.3 Emotional Contagion

10.3.1 Catching expressions and moods from others

Emotional contagion is the tendency to catch other people's feelings, and the psychologist Elaine Hatfield and colleagues, who named the phenomenon in the early 1990s, proposed a simple three step mechanism.

First, you automatically mimic another person's expressions, voice, and posture, often within a fraction of a second.

Second, feedback from your own mimicking face and body nudges your feelings, exactly the mechanism of the facial feedback hypothesis.

Third, as a result, your emotional state converges toward theirs, usually without either of you noticing.

Everyday life is full of the result: laughter that sweeps a room, tension that spreads through a meeting from one anxious colleague, and the way a caregiver's calm settles a distressed child.

10.3.2 Mimicry, synchrony, and rapport

Contagion is not just leakage between individuals; it is social glue.

People who are getting along well unconsciously synchronize - matching postures, gestures, speech rhythms, and expressions - and this synchrony both reflects rapport, the sense of connection between people, and helps create it.

Experiments show that being subtly mimicked makes people like their interaction partner more and even behave more generously afterward, an effect nicknamed the chameleon effect by the psychologists Tanya Chartrand and John Bargh.

Consistent with its social function, mimicry is selective: we echo the faces of friends, allies, and in group members more readily than those of rivals.

Emotional contagion through these channels helps explain how moods scale up from pairs to whole groups, teams, and crowds.

10.3.3 Contagion through screens and media

Contagion does not require physical presence, or even a visible face.

In 2014, researchers working with Facebook published an experiment on 689,003 users, quietly adjusting news feeds to show fewer positive or fewer negative posts, and found that people's own subsequent posts shifted in the same emotional direction, demonstrating contagion through text alone, with no nonverbal cues at all.

The measured effects were tiny for any individual, but the study became famous for two reasons: it showed emotion spreading at massive scale through a platform, and it ignited an ethical controversy because users had not meaningfully consented to being experimented on.

Researchers who study digital emotion contagion note that engagement driven algorithms tend to amplify emotionally intense content, because it holds attention, which may intensify contagion online relative to everyday life.

Video adds back the face: emotional expressions in film, television, and video calls trigger the same mimicry and feedback machinery as live faces, which is why a movie can make a theater full of strangers cry together.

The practical takeaway from this whole chapter is worth stating plainly: your face is not just an output device but part of a loop, continuously shaping your own feelings and quietly transmitting them to everyone who sees it, in person or through a screen.

PART IV: DEVELOPMENT ACROSS THE LIFESPAN

Chapter 11: Infancy and Early Childhood

This chapter follows facial expression from the very first hours of life through the first years, asking which parts of the expressive system are present at birth and which are built through experience.

Along the way we will meet sleeping newborns who smile, babies who read their mothers' faces before crawling over an apparent drop, and blind athletes whose faces tell us something surprising about where expressions come from.

11.1 The Newborn Face

11.1.1 Neonatal reflex smiles and distress expressions

Newborn babies smile, but their earliest smiles are not social greetings.

These are called reflex smiles or endogenous smiles, meaning smiles generated from within the body rather than triggered by anything the baby sees or hears.

They occur mostly during active sleep, the newborn version of Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep, which is the sleep stage with bursts of brain activity and darting eye movements.

Researchers have observed this smiling in premature babies and even before birth, from around thirty-three weeks of gestation, which shows that the facial machinery for smiling is wired up before any social experience is possible.

Newborns also produce clear distress expressions, with the classic cry face of lowered brows, tightly shut eyes, and a wide open squarish mouth appearing from the first day of life.

So the face arrives at birth already equipped with working expression patterns, even though the emotional meanings adults attach to them come later.

11.1.2 Early preference for face-like patterns

Even in the first days of life, babies would rather look at faces than at almost anything else.

Experiments have shown that newborns only hours or days old will turn their eyes and heads to follow a simple face-like pattern, such as three dark blobs arranged like two eyes and a mouth, more than they follow the same blobs scrambled into a different arrangement.

Mark Johnson and John Morton explained this with a two-part theory, proposing an inborn mechanism they called CONSPEC that draws a newborn's attention to anything with the rough geometry of a face, and a second learning mechanism called CONLERN that gradually builds detailed face knowledge from experience.

On this view, the newborn brain does not know any particular face, but it comes with a built-in bias that points the eyes at faces so that learning about them can begin immediately.

This early face bias matters for expressions, because a baby who spends hours looking at faces gets a rich stream of expressive input from the very start.

11.1.3 Neonatal imitation: the claims and the controversy

In the late 1970s, the psychologists Andrew Meltzoff and Keith Moore made a famous claim, reporting that newborn babies could imitate an adult's facial gestures, such as sticking out the tongue or opening the mouth.

If true, this would mean newborns can somehow match what they see on another face to movements of their own unseen face, an impressive inborn ability.

However, in the decades since, many laboratories have tried to repeat the finding, and a large number of these attempts failed.

A major longitudinal study by Janine Oostenbroek and colleagues in 2016, which tested babies repeatedly at one, three, six, and nine weeks with many different gestures, found no convincing evidence of imitation, though Meltzoff's team responded by arguing that the study's design was flawed and that reanalysis of its data did show tongue-protrusion imitation.

Skeptics also point out that tongue protrusion, the one gesture with the strongest results, might simply be a general arousal response, meaning babies stick out their tongues at anything interesting, not just at tongues.

The honest summary is that neonatal imitation remains unresolved, with many researchers now leaning toward skepticism, and readers should treat confident claims in either direction with caution.

11.2 Expression Production in Infancy

11.2.1 The social smile at around two months

Somewhere around six to eight weeks of age, a milestone arrives that parents never forget: the first true social smile.

Unlike the reflex smiles of the newborn period, a social smile is directed at a person, appearing in response to a face, a voice, or playful interaction.

Its emergence coincides with a general increase in alertness and with maturing connections in the brain's visual and social circuits.

Social smiling appears at roughly the same age across cultures, which suggests a maturational timetable, though babies who get lots of face-to-face interaction may begin slightly earlier.

From this point on, the smile becomes a two-way social tool, rewarding caregivers for attention and pulling them into longer and richer exchanges.

11.2.2 Laughter, surprise, and fear across the first year

The expressive repertoire expands rapidly across the first year, and the different expressions come online in a fairly regular order.

Laughter typically appears at around three to four months, first in response to physical play such as tickling and later to surprising or silly events.

Expressions of surprise, sadness, and anger become clearer and more situation-specific over the middle of the first year, as displeasure differentiates from a general fussiness into recognizably distinct expressions.

Fear expressions arrive comparatively late, usually becoming obvious between about six and nine months, around the same time that stranger anxiety appears, which is the wariness or distress many babies show toward unfamiliar people.

This orderly schedule, appearing across very different rearing environments, is one reason many researchers believe basic expressions unfold partly on a biological timetable rather than being learned one by one.

11.2.3 Still-face experiments and expressive expectation

The still-face paradigm, introduced by the developmental psychologist Edward Tronick in the 1970s, is one of the most revealing experiments in infancy research.

A mother first plays normally with her baby, then on a signal holds her face completely still and expressionless for a few minutes while continuing to look at the baby.

Babies as young as two to three months react strongly, first working hard to restart the interaction by smiling, vocalizing, and reaching, and then, when the blank face persists, becoming distressed, looking away, and sometimes slumping into a withdrawn posture.

This reaction shows that very young infants are not passive receivers of expressions but hold expectations, anticipating that faces in conversation will respond, mirror, and take turns.

The still-face effect has been replicated hundreds of times and is used today to study how babies regulate emotion and how the quality of caregiver interaction shapes development.

11.3 Expression Recognition in Infancy

11.3.1 Discriminating expressions in the first months

Researchers test what babies can tell apart using habituation, a method in which a baby is shown one image repeatedly until looking time drops, and then a new image is shown to see whether looking recovers, which signals that the baby noticed the change.

Using such methods, studies find that by around three to four months babies can discriminate happy faces from expressions such as anger, sadness, and neutral faces.

By five to seven months, discrimination broadens, and infants can tell apart happiness, fear, anger, surprise, and sadness, even generalizing across different people making the same expression.

A striking shift occurs at about seven months, when infants begin to look longer at fearful faces than at happy ones, a fear bias that five-month-olds do not yet show.

Discrimination, however, is not the same as understanding, since a baby who can see that two expressions differ may not yet grasp what either one means emotionally.

11.3.2 Social referencing: reading the caregiver's face before acting

Late in the first year, babies begin to use other people's expressions as guides to action, a behavior called social referencing.

Social referencing means actively looking to another person's face for emotional information when a situation is uncertain, and then using that information to decide what to do.

A ten- to twelve-month-old encountering a strange toy, a loud new appliance, or an unfamiliar visitor will often pause, look back at the caregiver's face, and proceed happily if the face shows pleasure or hold back if it shows fear.

This is a genuinely sophisticated achievement, because the baby is treating an expression not just as a display but as information about a third thing in the world.

Social referencing marks the point where expression reading becomes a practical survival tool, letting infants borrow their caregivers' knowledge about what is safe.

11.3.3 The visual cliff and maternal signals

The clearest demonstration of social referencing came from a classic 1985 study by James Sorce, Robert Emde, and colleagues using the visual cliff, an apparatus with a sturdy glass surface over what looks like a sudden drop.

The apparent drop was set at an ambiguous height, deep enough to make one-year-old babies hesitate but not deep enough to make them refuse outright.

When mothers stationed on the far side posed a happy or interested face, most babies crossed the glass over the apparent drop.

When mothers posed a fearful or angry face, almost no babies crossed, and a posed sad face produced an in-between result.

A single silent facial expression, in other words, was enough to decide whether a one-year-old would venture over a visual precipice, which is powerful evidence that infants extract real meaning from faces before they can speak.

11.4 Congenitally Blind Individuals

11.4.1 Spontaneous expressions without visual learning

People who are congenitally blind, meaning blind from birth, have never seen a smile, a frown, or any facial expression, so their faces provide a natural test of whether expressions must be learned by watching others.

The most famous evidence comes from a 2009 study by David Matsumoto and Bob Willingham, who analyzed more than 4,800 photographs of judo athletes at the 2004 Olympic Games and Paralympic Games, including congenitally blind competitors.

Blind athletes produced the same spontaneous expressions in the same situations as sighted athletes, smiling broadly after victories and showing sadness and disappointment after defeats.

Even a subtle social pattern held: blind silver medalists, like sighted ones, tended to produce polite non-genuine smiles on the podium rather than the full smiles of gold medalists.

Since the congenitally blind athletes could never have copied these displays visually, the match with sighted athletes is hard to explain by imitation.

11.4.2 What blindness reveals about innate expression programs

The blind-athlete findings, together with earlier observations of blind children by researchers going back to Charles Darwin and the ethologist Irenaeus Eibl-Eibesfeldt, point toward inborn programs for producing spontaneous emotional expressions.

An ethologist is a scientist who studies natural behavior, and Eibl-Eibesfeldt filmed children born both blind and deaf who nonetheless smiled, laughed, and cried in the expected situations.

The reasonable conclusion is that the core muscle patterns of spontaneous expressions do not require visual learning.

At the same time, the evidence has limits, since blind individuals often differ from sighted people in their deliberate, posed expressions and in the fine social tuning of their displays, which suggests that watching others polishes and regulates a system that nature roughs out.

So blindness research supports innate expression programs for spontaneous emotion while leaving plenty of room for social learning in how, when, and how strongly those programs are displayed.

Chapter 12: Childhood, Adolescence, and Aging

Expression skills do not finish developing in infancy; recognition sharpens across a surprisingly long stretch of childhood, social rules for displaying emotion are gradually mastered, adolescence brings its own emotional intensities, and aging changes both the reading and the sending of facial signals.

This chapter traces that long arc from the preschool years to old age.

12.1 Growing Recognition Skill

12.1.1 The long developmental course of expression recognition

Although babies can discriminate expressions within months of birth, accurately recognizing what expressions mean takes many years to mature.

Preschoolers make frequent errors, often lumping all negative expressions together as simply mad or sad.

Accuracy climbs steadily through the elementary school years, with recognition of the six classic basic expressions reaching a reasonably stable level around eight to nine years of age.

Even then, development is not finished, because fine-grained skills such as recognizing subtle or low-intensity expressions continue improving into adolescence and early adulthood.

This long course tells us that expression recognition is not a simple inborn lookup table but a skill that is tuned by years of social experience.

12.1.2 From sensory to prefrontal representation with age

The brain systems that handle expressions reorganize as children grow.

In young children, responses to emotional faces lean heavily on early-developing structures, including sensory areas that process what a face looks like and the amygdala, an almond-shaped structure deep in the brain that responds quickly to emotionally significant sights.

Brain-imaging studies suggest that in school-age children the amygdala responds broadly to many kinds of faces, emotional or not, and only gradually becomes more selective.

With age, the prefrontal cortex, the front region of the brain responsible for planning, judgment, and self-control, becomes increasingly involved, connecting more strongly with the amygdala and helping to interpret and regulate reactions to expressions.

The overall developmental story is a shift from fast, raw sensory and emotional responding toward representations that mix perception with context, knowledge, and control.

12.1.3 Happy first, fear and disgust later: order of mastery

Children do not master all expressions at once, and the order is remarkably consistent across studies.

Happiness comes first, being recognized well by around age three, which makes sense because smiles are frequent, distinctive, and heavily rewarded in a child's daily life.

Sadness and anger follow across the ages of roughly five to nine, with studies varying somewhat on the exact order.

Fear and disgust are reliably the hardest, often not recognized at adult-like levels until around age nine or ten, with improvement continuing into the teenage years.

Part of the difficulty is that fear is easily confused with surprise and disgust with anger, because those expression pairs share overlapping facial movements.

The general rule is that the more visually distinctive and more frequently encountered an expression is, the earlier children master it.

12.2 Learning Display Rules

12.2.1 Masking, minimizing, and exaggerating expressions

Every culture teaches rules about when emotions may be shown on the face, and psychologists call these display rules.

The main techniques children must learn are masking, which means covering a felt emotion with a different expression such as a polite smile; minimizing, which means showing the emotion but at reduced strength; exaggerating, which means amplifying an expression beyond what is felt, as when a child theatrically wails over a small bump; and neutralizing, which means showing nothing at all.

First attempts at these skills appear surprisingly early, by around age three or four, but early efforts are leaky and unconvincing.

Genuine skill develops across middle childhood as children gain both the motor control to manage their faces and the social understanding of why hiding a feeling can protect another person or themselves.

Learning display rules is thus a meeting point of emotion, self-control, and social insight.

12.2.2 The disappointing-gift paradigm

The classic laboratory test of display rules is the disappointing-gift paradigm, introduced by the psychologist Carolyn Saarni in 1984.

A child first receives an attractive reward for helping with a task, and later, after helping again, is handed a deliberately disappointing gift such as a babyish toy, while researchers film the child's face.

The socially expected response is to conceal disappointment and produce a polite show of thanks, which requires suppressing the true feeling and summoning a positive display at the same time.

Young children often fail visibly, letting frowns and complaints escape, while by around age ten most children can produce a fairly convincing gracious front.

Girls in many studies mask disappointment somewhat more than boys, and children with better inhibitory control, the mental ability to stop an impulsive response, tend to perform better.

The paradigm neatly captures, in a single awkward moment, the whole developmental project of bringing a spontaneous expressive system under social management.

12.2.3 Family and cultural shaping of expressiveness

Display rules are not learned from a textbook but absorbed from family life and the surrounding culture.

Children of highly expressive parents tend to become more expressive themselves, both through imitation and through households where showing feelings is accepted.

Parents also teach directly, coaching children to say thank you and smile, or telling them that big boys and girls do not cry in public.

Cultures differ in the rules they transmit, and studies comparing preschoolers across countries find, for example, that children raised in cultures prizing group harmony often damp down negative displays toward disappointment more than children raised in cultures that prize individual self-expression.

By the end of childhood, each person carries an internalized rulebook of expressiveness, so alike within a community and so varied across communities that faces themselves come to carry a cultural accent.

12.3 Adolescence

12.3.1 Heightened amygdala response to emotional faces

Adolescence brings a distinctive change in how the brain reacts to emotional faces.

Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), a brain-scanning method that tracks activity by measuring blood flow, shows that adolescents often produce stronger amygdala responses to emotional faces than either children or adults do.

Researchers interpret this as a developmental mismatch: the emotional reactivity systems of the brain surge ahead during puberty, while the prefrontal control systems that regulate them mature slowly into the mid twenties.

The result is a period in which emotional faces, and emotional events generally, hit harder and are damped down less.

This heightened reactivity is thought to contribute to the emotional intensity, sensitivity to evaluation, and greater risk-taking that characterize the teenage years, although individual adolescents vary enormously.

12.3.2 Peer faces and social sensitivity

Not all faces are equal in the adolescent brain, and faces of peers, meaning people of one's own age group, gain special power.

Adolescents show distinctive brain and behavioral responses to peer faces, and research suggests the maturing amygdala is especially responsive to the happy faces of peers, consistent with the growing reward value of peer approval.

Everyday experience agrees, as teenagers become acutely attuned to signs of acceptance or rejection on the faces of classmates, often caring more about a peer's smirk than a parent's frown.

Some studies even find that the strength of a young adolescent's brain response to emotional faces predicts aspects of their social life years later.

This peer sensitivity is not a design flaw but preparation, since adolescence is exactly the period when humans must learn to navigate a social world beyond the family.

12.4 Aging

12.4.1 Recognition declines for specific expressions in older age

At the other end of life, expression recognition changes again, and not uniformly.

Meta-analyses, which are studies that statistically combine the results of many earlier studies, show that older adults are on average less accurate than younger adults at recognizing certain expressions, with the largest declines for anger, sadness, and fear.

Recognition of happiness holds up well, and disgust recognition is often preserved or even relatively strong in older adults.

The causes are debated and probably mixed, including gradual changes in brain regions involved in processing negative emotion, changes in where older adults look on a face, and motivational shifts in what older adults attend to.

It is worth stressing that these are average differences on laboratory tasks, and many older individuals read faces as well as people decades younger.

12.4.2 The positivity bias in older adults' attention to faces

Older adults do not just recognize expressions differently; they attend to them differently, showing what researchers call the positivity effect or positivity bias.

In attention experiments, older adults tend to look toward happy faces and away from angry or sad ones, and in memory tests they remember positive faces and images relatively better than negative ones.

The leading explanation is socioemotional selectivity theory, developed by the psychologist Laura Carstensen, which proposes that when people perceive their remaining time as limited they shift priorities toward emotional wellbeing and therefore favor positive information.

On this view, the positivity bias is not a defect but a motivated strategy that helps older adults maintain good moods and smooth relationships.

Some researchers add a cautionary note, since a strong bias toward the positive could occasionally cost older adults by making warning signals on other faces, such as anger or fearful alarm, easier to miss.

12.4.3 Structural facial change and expression readability

Aging changes not only the reading of faces but the faces themselves, and this affects how well older adults' expressions come across.

Wrinkles, folds, and sagging tissue accumulate over decades, and research by Ursula Hess and colleagues shows that these changes reduce what they call signal clarity, meaning the crispness with which an expression stands out on the face.

Permanent creases can also mimic expression, so a neutral older face may be misread as sad, angry, or tired simply because of resting lines, and observers often judge neutral older faces as more expressive than neutral young faces.

The result is a noisier channel: the same felt emotion produces a facial signal that observers of all ages find somewhat harder to decode from an older face.

This helps explain some everyday misunderstandings across generations, and it is a useful reminder that communication difficulties can lie in the sending as well as the receiving of expressions.

It is a fitting close to the lifespan story: the face that begins as a reflexive newborn instrument ends as a richly lined record of a lifetime of expression.

PART V: EVOLUTION, CULTURE, AND THE UNIVERSALITY DEBATE

Chapter 13: Evolutionary Origins

This chapter asks where facial expressions came from in the first place, by looking at the faces of monkeys, apes, and other animals, and at the evolutionary pressures that turn a bodily reflex into a signal.

Understanding these origins sets the stage for the great debate, taken up in the next two chapters, over how much of human expression is inherited and how much is learned.

13.1 Expression in Nonhuman Animals

13.1.1 Primate facial displays: the bared-teeth display and the play face

Monkeys and apes have expressive faces, and two of their displays have been studied in great detail.

The bared-teeth display is an expression in which the lips are pulled back to expose the teeth while the mouth stays mostly closed, and in many monkey species it is given by a lower-ranking animal to a higher-ranking one as a signal of submission, meaning roughly I am not a threat.

The play face is a relaxed, open-mouthed expression, often with the upper teeth covered, that primates show during rough-and-tumble play.

Because play involves chasing, grabbing, and mock biting, the play face seems to tell the partner that everything happening is friendly and voluntary, not a real attack.

Both displays are exclusively social: they appear when another animal is present to see them, which is a first clue that faces evolved for communication.

13.1.2 Homologies between primate and human expressions

A homology is a trait shared by two species because they inherited it from a common ancestor, the way a bat's wing and a human hand share the same underlying bones.

Ethologists - scientists who study animal behavior in natural settings - have argued that the human smile is homologous with the primate silent bared-teeth display, and that the face we make while laughing is homologous with the primate play face.

On this account, the smile began as an appeasement signal that says I am friendly and not dangerous, and laughter began as a play invitation, and the two only later blended together in humans.

Supporting this idea, the facial muscles of chimpanzees are remarkably similar to our own, and researchers have even built a ChimpFACS, a chimpanzee version of the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), the human muscle-by-muscle scoring tool, to compare expressions movement by movement.

The close match in both muscle anatomy and social use is some of the strongest evidence that human expressions have deep evolutionary roots.

13.1.3 Facial signaling in dogs, horses, and other mammals

Expressive faces are not limited to primates.

Domestic dogs have a special muscle around the eyes, largely absent in wolves, that lets them raise the inner eyebrow to make the famous puppy-dog eyes, a movement that reliably tugs at human sympathy and may have been favored during domestication.

Horses have such mobile faces that researchers created an Equine Facial Action Coding System (EquiFACS) to catalogue their eye, lip, nostril, and ear movements, and horses turn out to raise their inner brows and widen their eyes in alarming situations much as humans do.

Veterinarians now use grimace scales - standardized checklists of facial signs such as ear position, eye tension, and muzzle tightening - to measure pain in horses, mice, rabbits, and other animals.

The lesson is that facial signaling is a widespread mammalian tool, not a human invention, although the richness of the human repertoire remains unusual.

13.2 Why Expressions Evolved

13.2.1 From physiological reflex to communicative signal: ritualization

Many expressions probably started out as practical bodily actions rather than messages.

Wrinkling the nose and narrowing the eyes can protect the face from bad smells and splashes, and widening the eyes can expand the field of view when danger appears.

Ritualization is the evolutionary process by which such a useful reflex gradually becomes exaggerated, stereotyped, and detached from its original job, precisely because other individuals benefit from noticing it.

Once observers start reading the movement as information, natural selection can favor senders who make it bigger, clearer, and more consistent, until it functions as a signal in its own right.

Charles Darwin anticipated part of this story in 1872, and modern ethology filled in the mechanism: expressions are, in this view, fossilized fragments of ancient practical behavior polished into communication.

13.2.2 Honest signaling and the problem of faking

A signal only stays useful if receivers can generally trust it, which raises the question of why animals do not simply fake beneficial expressions all the time.

Honest signaling theory addresses this puzzle: signals can remain trustworthy when they are costly to produce, hard to counterfeit, or policed by the social group.

For example, some genuine human expressions recruit muscles that most people cannot easily control on command, such as the muscle circling the eye that tightens in a heartfelt smile, which makes convincing fakes difficult.

Social punishment also matters, because an animal caught bluffing a threat display may be attacked, and a person caught faking distress may lose trust and cooperation.

The result is an evolutionary arms race: senders gain some ability to pose and conceal, receivers gain some ability to detect fakes, and expressions end up partly honest and partly managed - a tension that runs through the rest of this book.

13.2.3 Co-evolution of expression production and perception

A signal is useless without a receiver, so the sending side and the reading side of expression must have evolved together.

Co-evolution means that changes in one trait drive changes in another: as faces became more expressive, brains became better at decoding them, and better decoding in turn favored clearer sending.

Human anatomy shows possible traces of this partnership, such as our unusual white sclera, the visible white of the eye, which makes gaze direction and eye widening far easier for others to read than in most other primates.

Likewise, the human brain contains regions that respond strongly to faces and facial motion, suggesting perceptual machinery tuned to exactly the signals our species produces.

This co-evolutionary logic is one reason many scientists expect at least some correspondence between expressions and their interpretation across all human groups - the claim examined next.

Chapter 14: The Universality Evidence

This chapter lays out the classic evidence that facial expressions of emotion are universal, meaning shared by all human beings regardless of culture.

It then describes the refinements researchers added as the picture grew more complicated.

14.1 The Classic Cross-Cultural Studies

14.1.1 Ekman and Friesen's multi-nation judgment studies

In the late 1960s the psychologists Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen showed photographs of posed facial expressions to observers in countries including the United States, Japan, Brazil, Chile, and Argentina.

Observers in every country matched the same faces to the same emotion words - happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise - at rates far above what guessing would produce.

Similar results were reported around the same time by Carroll Izard using a different set of photographs and countries.

These judgment studies, so called because observers judge what a face shows, became the foundation of the universality thesis, the claim that a small set of basic emotions is expressed and recognized the same way everywhere.

Critics noted, however, that people in all of these countries watched movies and television, so shared media rather than shared biology might explain the agreement - which motivated the next, more dramatic study.

14.1.2 The isolated-culture test: the Fore of Papua New Guinea

To rule out media exposure, Ekman and Friesen traveled in the late 1960s to the highlands of Papua New Guinea to study the Fore people, a group who had lived in near-total isolation from Western culture.

Because most Fore participants could not read, the researchers told short stories, such as one about a person whose child had died, and asked listeners to point to the photographed face that matched the story from a set of two or three.

Published in 1971, the results showed that the Fore chose the predicted face well above chance for most emotions, although they could not reliably distinguish fear faces from surprise faces.

In a second part, Fore volunteers were filmed posing the expressions the stories described, and American college students later identified most of those poses correctly.

Ekman regarded this two-way agreement between cultures with essentially no shared media as decisive evidence for universality, and the Fore study remains the single most cited experiment in the field - as well as a major target of later criticism, discussed in Chapter 15.

14.1.3 Spontaneous expression studies: athletes, infants, and the blind

Judgment studies only test recognition, so researchers also looked for evidence that people everywhere produce similar faces in similar situations without posing.

The strongest such study, by David Matsumoto and Bob Willingham in 2009, analyzed photographs of judo medal matches at the 2004 Olympic and Paralympic Games and found that congenitally blind athletes - people blind from birth, who could never have seen a face - made the same victory and defeat expressions as sighted athletes from dozens of countries.

Because the blind athletes could not have learned these faces by watching others, the finding suggests that at least some expression patterns are part of our biological inheritance.

Newborn infants add converging evidence, since they show recognizable distaste faces to bitter tastes and social smiles within weeks of birth, long before much learning could occur.

Skeptics reply that even blind children receive verbal coaching about faces, and that a few dramatic situations like winning and losing do not prove that every emotion has its own universal face.

14.2 The Case for Universality

14.2.1 Above-chance recognition across cultures

The core statistical fact supporting universality is simple: in well over a hundred studies across dozens of literate and nonliterate cultures, observers match prototype expression photographs to the intended emotion labels more often than chance guessing would allow.

Hillary Anger Elfenbein and Nalini Ambady confirmed this in a 2002 meta-analysis - a statistical method that combines the results of many studies into one overall estimate - covering ninety-seven separate samples.

Recognition rates are highest for happiness, often above ninety percent, and lower but still above chance for fear and disgust.

Even the strongest critics accept this above-chance agreement as a real phenomenon that any theory must explain.

The dispute, as we will see, is over what the agreement means and whether the methods inflate it.

14.2.2 Similar expressions in similar situations worldwide

A second line of support looks at production: do people in different cultures actually make similar faces in similar circumstances?

Field observations and film studies have reported smiling in greeting, disgust faces at spoiled food, and crying in grief across widely separated societies.

The ethologist Irenaus Eibl-Eibesfeldt filmed people in many traditional societies and documented the eyebrow flash, a rapid raising of both brows used as a friendly greeting on every continent he visited.

Large-scale modern studies, including machine-learning analyses of millions of video clips from many countries, report that certain facial movements occur in similar contexts, such as celebration or pain, around the world.

Proponents read this as evidence that expressions are part of a shared human nature, while critics note that the overlap in production is far looser and more variable than the tidy textbook photographs suggest.

14.2.3 Cross-cultural production studies and in-group advantage

When researchers compare expressions posed or produced in different cultures, an interesting pattern appears: observers everywhere can read foreign faces above chance, but they read faces from their own culture best.

This boost is called the in-group advantage, where an in-group is simply the cultural group a person belongs to.

Elfenbein and Ambady's meta-analysis found the advantage in most comparisons and, crucially, found that it shrinks when two cultures have had a lot of contact with each other, for example through geography or shared media.

That pattern suggests the advantage comes from learnable stylistic details in how each culture performs expressions, not from motivation or prejudice in the judges.

The in-group advantage sits neatly between the two camps: it confirms substantial cross-cultural agreement while proving that culture leaves a measurable fingerprint on the face.

14.3 Refinements

14.3.1 Universality of production versus universality of recognition

As the debate matured, researchers realized that universality is not one claim but at least two.

Universality of production is the claim that people everywhere spontaneously make similar faces when feeling similar emotions.

Universality of recognition is the claim that people everywhere interpret those faces the same way.

The classic evidence is much stronger for recognition, which was tested directly in judgment studies, than for production, which was mostly assumed; studies that actually measure spontaneous faces find that a person feeling a given emotion often does not show the textbook expression at all.

Separating the two claims lets scientists agree on some points while disputing others, and much of the modern argument is about how weak the production evidence really is.

14.3.2 Minimal universality: shared cores with cultural accents

The psychologist James Russell proposed a middle position he called minimal universality.

On this view, what all humans share is not a fixed dictionary of emotion faces but some basic common ground: people everywhere make facial movements, everywhere draw inferences from them, and everywhere can read at least broad qualities such as pleasantness versus unpleasantness and high versus low arousal, meaning the level of bodily activation.

The specific pairing of a full facial pattern with a specific emotion category like anger is, in this account, filled in differently by different cultures.

Minimal universality explains why cross-cultural agreement is always above chance yet almost never as high within distant cultures as within the culture that made the photographs.

Many researchers on both sides now treat something like this shared-core-plus-local-elaboration picture as the most defensible summary of the data.

14.3.3 Dialect theory: cultural accents in expression style

Elfenbein and Ambady turned the in-group advantage into a positive theory by borrowing a metaphor from language.

Dialect theory proposes that emotional expression is like a universal language spoken in local dialects: the underlying vocabulary of facial movements is shared, but each culture performs the expressions with subtle stylistic accents.

Just as an accent rarely blocks understanding but is instantly noticeable, these expressive accents allow communication across cultures while giving locals a decoding edge.

Supporting experiments show that people recognize emotions faster and more accurately from expressions posed in their own culture's style, and that exposure to another culture reduces the gap, exactly as with linguistic accents.

Dialect theory preserves a universal core while taking cultural variation seriously, and it has become one of the most influential compromise positions in the field.

Chapter 15: The Challenges to Universality

This chapter presents the case against strong universality: methodological objections, two rival theories, and a wave of new cross-cultural findings.

It closes with an honest accounting of what is settled and what is still fought over.

15.1 Methodological Critiques

15.1.1 Forced-choice inflation of recognition scores

In 1994 James Russell published a sweeping critique arguing that the classic studies' methods inflated agreement.

The central target was the forced-choice format, in which observers must pick an answer from a short list of emotion words supplied by the experimenter rather than describing the face in their own words.

Forced choice lets people succeed by elimination: an observer unsure what a wide-eyed face means can still pick fear if the only remaining options are obviously wrong, so high scores can appear without genuine recognition.

When researchers instead use free labeling, where observers say whatever they think the face shows, agreement drops substantially, especially outside Western samples.

Ekman and Izard replied forcefully that the results held up under many method variations, but the debate established that the headline recognition percentages depend partly on how the question is asked - a point now conceded on all sides.

15.1.2 Posed prototype photographs versus real-life faces

A second critique targets the stimuli themselves.

The classic studies used carefully posed, exaggerated, frozen photographs of prototype expressions, often produced by actors following muscle-by-muscle instructions.

Real emotional faces are usually briefer, weaker, mixed, and embedded in a moving body and a rich situation, and recognition of such spontaneous expressions is markedly lower and more culturally variable.

Critics argue that the prototypes are best understood as culturally refined symbols of emotion, a bit like emoji, rather than accurate samples of what feeling people actually do with their faces.

Defenders respond that clear stimuli are a legitimate way to test whether a signal system exists at all, but most researchers now agree that results from posed prototypes cannot be assumed to transfer to everyday faces.

15.1.3 Gradient effects: recognition falls with cultural distance

If expressions were read identically everywhere, an observer's culture should not matter, yet it clearly does.

Across the literature, recognition accuracy declines steadily as the cultural distance between the person posing the face and the person judging it grows - Western observers judging Western faces score highest, distant small-scale societies judging those same faces score lowest.

This gradient effect is awkward for strong universality, because a purely innate signal should not fade with unfamiliarity.

It is, however, exactly what dialect theory and minimal universality predict, since both expect a shared core overlaid with learned local detail.

The gradient thus became a key piece of evidence pushing the field away from all-or-nothing positions.

15.2 The Behavioral Ecology View

15.2.1 Faces as social tools, not emotion readouts

The psychologist Alan Fridlund proposed a radical alternative called the behavioral ecology view.

In this account, facial displays did not evolve to leak our inner feelings; they evolved as tools for influencing other people.

A smile, on this view, is not a readout of happiness but a declaration of readiness to affiliate, roughly let us be friends, and a scowl is not leaked anger but a negotiating move that says back off or change course.

Because signals that broadcast private states to competitors would be evolutionarily costly, Fridlund argues that selection favored displays tied to social intentions rather than to emotions.

The view does not deny that people have feelings; it denies that the face is designed to report them.

15.2.2 Audience effects: smiling more when watched

The behavioral ecology view's best-known evidence is the audience effect, the finding that expressions depend on who is present to see them.

In a classic observational study, bowlers who scored a strike rarely smiled while facing the pins, but smiled broadly when they turned around to face their friends - the smile tracked the audience, not the moment of triumph.

Fridlund also showed that people watching a pleasant video smile more when a friend is present, and even when they merely believe a friend is watching the same video in another room, an implicit audience.

Olympic gold medalists show the same pattern, smiling mainly during the social moments of the medal ceremony rather than continuously through their happiest hour.

If expressions were automatic readouts of emotion, the audience should not matter this much, so these findings are a genuine challenge that any complete theory must absorb.

15.2.3 Reinterpreting classic findings as social signaling

Behavioral ecologists do not dismiss the classic data; they reread it.

Cross-cultural agreement, they argue, may reflect universally useful social messages rather than universal emotions: every society needs signals for affiliation, appeasement, threat, and refusal, so some worldwide overlap in displays is expected without any link to inner feeling.

The Fore results, on this reading, show that people can match faces to social scenarios, not that faces express basic emotions.

Even the blind-athlete findings can be reframed, since displays aimed at social ends could be innate while still being about influencing others rather than venting feelings.

Critics of the view reply that people demonstrably do express emotion when entirely alone, and that imagined audiences can be invoked to explain away almost anything, which makes the theory hard to disprove - a criticism its defenders in turn contest.

15.3 The Constructionist View

15.3.1 Expressions as culturally learned categories

Constructionism, championed most prominently by the psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett, goes further than the behavioral ecology view.

The theory of constructed emotion holds that emotions themselves are not biological modules with dedicated faces but categories that each brain builds from more basic ingredients - bodily sensations, past experience, and, crucially, the concepts a culture teaches.

On this view, the concept anger, and the idea that a scowl is its face, are learned much as words are learned, which is why emotion categories differ across languages and societies.

Facial movements are real and meaningful, but their meaning is constructed in context rather than fixed by evolution.

Constructionists therefore predict exactly what the gradient and free-labeling data show: agreement rises with shared concepts and falls without them.

15.3.2 Low correlation between felt emotion and produced expression

A central empirical plank of constructionism is the weak link between what people feel and what their faces do.

Studies that induce genuine emotion in the laboratory and measure the face with electrodes or the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) find that only a minority of angry people scowl, only a minority of happy people smile in that moment, and the same movement appears across many different emotional states.

In statistical terms the correlation - the degree to which two measures rise and fall together - between reported feeling and predicted facial pattern is reliably positive but small.

This is a serious problem for the idea of the face as a readout, because a readout that fires perhaps a quarter of the time and also fires for other reasons is a very poor indicator.

Basic-emotion theorists respond that display rules, regulation, and measurement noise mask a real underlying connection, but the low raw numbers are not themselves in dispute.

15.3.3 The 2019 Barrett-led review: context matters more than the face alone

In 2019 five senior scientists from different camps - Lisa Feldman Barrett, Ralph Adolphs, Stacy Marsella, Aleix Martinez, and Seth Pollak - published a monumental review in the journal Psychological Science in the Public Interest titled Emotional Expressions Reconsidered.

Surveying more than a thousand studies, they concluded that facial movements have reliability, specificity, and generalizability far too low to justify inferring a person's emotional state from the face alone.

Their summary formula: a scowl is neither necessary nor sufficient for anger; people scowl for many reasons and are often angry without scowling.

The review emphasized context - the situation, the body, the voice, the culture - as carrying much of the meaning that had been credited to the face.

Its practical warning was blunt: technologies and policies that claim to read emotion from faces, from hiring software to security screening, rest on shaky science, a conclusion that reached courts, companies, and regulators and made the academic debate a public issue.

15.4 New Cross-Cultural Evidence

15.4.1 Trobriand Islands findings: the fear face read as threat

The psychologist Carlos Crivelli and colleagues returned to Papua New Guinea in the 2010s with updated methods, working among the Trobriand Islanders, a society with substantial isolation from Western media.

In studies published in 2016, Trobriand participants shown the classic wide-eyed, gasping fear face did not read it as fear or submission the way Spanish participants did.

Instead they most often interpreted it as anger and threat - the face of someone about to attack.

When asked to point to the threatening face, Trobrianders chose the fear gasp while Spaniards chose the scowl, a direct reversal on a supposedly universal signal.

Crivelli notes that in some Melanesian and other cultures a wide-eyed face is a conventional threat display, and he argues the finding shows that even the most basic expressions can carry opposite meanings in different societies - though defenders of universality question the translations and tasks involved.

15.4.2 Free-labeling studies in remote societies

Maria Gendron, Barrett, and colleagues tested emotion perception in remote groups using free labeling and free sorting, methods that supply no answer list.

Working with the Himba, a herding people of northwestern Namibia, and later the Hadza of Tanzania, they found that participants asked to sort expression photographs into piles did not spontaneously produce the six presumed universal categories, though they did group faces by broad qualities such as pleasantness and by visible actions like laughing or looking.

When experimenters supplied emotion concepts as hints, the sorts moved closer to the Western pattern, suggesting that the concepts themselves do much of the organizing work.

Across the Himba, Hadza, and Trobriand studies, remote participants rarely offered unprompted the emotion labels that the universality thesis predicts.

Universalists counter that above-chance matching still appears under many conditions and that free labeling sets an unrealistically strict bar, since even English speakers use varied words for the same face; the disagreement over which method is fairest remains unresolved.

15.4.3 Data-driven approaches: mapping expression meaning across cultures

A newer wave of research replaces preselected photographs with data-driven methods that let patterns emerge from the data rather than from the experimenter's assumptions.

Rachael Jack, Philippe Schyns, and colleagues at the University of Glasgow use a computer-graphics system that generates random combinations of facial movements on an animated face, and observers judge each one; a statistical technique called reverse correlation then reconstructs each observer's internal model of what, say, an angry face looks like.

Their studies find that Western observers' models fit the six classic categories reasonably well, but East Asian observers' models overlap more between categories, rely more on the eye region, and use dynamic cues differently, leading Jack to argue that expressions are not culturally universal in their fine structure.

Related work suggests the deep shared layer may be smaller than six: early in a facial movement observers everywhere extract broad information such as valence, meaning pleasantness versus unpleasantness, and arousal, with finer categories resolved later and more culturally.

On the other side, large data-driven projects by Alan Cowen, Dacher Keltner, and colleagues, analyzing millions of naturalistic videos worldwide, report dozens of expression-context associations that recur across cultures, so the data-driven era has produced ammunition for both camps rather than a verdict.

15.5 Where the Field Stands

15.5.1 Points of genuine consensus

Beneath the noise, several conclusions now command broad agreement across rival camps.

Facial movements are meaningful social signals in every known human society, and observers everywhere draw inferences from them.

Cross-cultural agreement in matching prototype faces to emotion labels is real and above chance, but it is far from perfect, it declines with cultural distance, and it depends on the method used to measure it.

Some expressive raw material - the smile of affiliation, the disgust nose wrinkle, infant expressions, the faces of the congenitally blind - has a genuine biological basis.

At the same time, everyone now accepts that no single facial configuration is a necessary and sufficient sign of a specific emotion in every person and context, and that culture measurably shapes both how faces are made and how they are read.

15.5.2 Points of active dispute

The live disagreements are nonetheless substantial.

Researchers still dispute whether a small set of basic emotions with privileged facial signatures exists in nature, as Ekman's tradition holds, or whether emotion categories are constructed and culturally variable, as Barrett's tradition holds.

They dispute what faces primarily signal: inner feelings, social intentions in the sense of Fridlund, appraisals of the situation, or flexible combinations of all three.

They dispute how to weigh methods, since forced choice, free labeling, sorting, and data-driven tasks each paint a different picture and each side prefers the tasks that favor it.

And they dispute how to interpret the remote-society findings, which one camp reads as decisive refutations and the other as artifacts of translation and task difficulty; readers should treat confident blanket statements from either side with caution.

15.5.3 Implications for applied uses of expression reading

The debate is not merely academic, because institutions increasingly want to read emotions from faces.

Automated emotion-recognition software is marketed for hiring interviews, classroom monitoring, driver safety, advertising tests, and border security, and much of it simply maps the six classic prototypes onto whatever faces it sees.

The 2019 review and related work warn that this mapping is scientifically unsound, since real faces express emotion variably, context does heavy lifting, and cultural differences can flip a face's meaning entirely, as the Trobriand fear-as-threat finding shows.

Misreading faces has real costs: a job candidate scored as insincere, a student scored as inattentive, or a traveler scored as deceptive may be judged by an algorithm built on contested science.

The responsible conclusion is modest: facial information is one useful clue among many, most trustworthy when combined with context, voice, body, and knowledge of the person and culture, and least trustworthy when a single frozen face is asked to testify about a hidden inner state.

PART VI: THE SOCIAL FACE

Chapter 16: Expressions in Social Interaction

Facial expressions do not just broadcast inner feelings - they are working parts of conversation, negotiation, and relationships.

This chapter looks at how faces keep talk flowing, how they signal who has power, and how they reveal the health of our closest bonds.

16.1 Conversation and Coordination

16.1.1 Facial back-channels: nods, brow flashes, and listener smiles

A back-channel is any small signal a listener sends while someone else is talking, such as a nod, a smile, a raised eyebrow, or a murmured "mm-hm".

These signals tell the speaker "I am with you, keep going" without interrupting the flow of speech.

Researchers who film conversations find that listeners produce a steady stream of these facial responses, and that speakers adjust what they say based on them.

One famous example is the eyebrow flash, a raising of both eyebrows lasting about a fifth of a second, which the Austrian ethologist Irenaeus Eibl-Eibesfeldt filmed as a greeting signal in cultures from Samoa to the Yanomami of Venezuela.

When back-channels are missing, as in a video call with a frozen screen, speakers become hesitant and conversation quickly feels awkward.

That discomfort shows how much invisible work the listening face normally does.

16.1.2 Gaze, eye contact, and expression together

Gaze means where the eyes are pointed, and it works hand in hand with expression to manage conversation.

Studies of turn-taking, which is the way people alternate who speaks, show that speakers often look away when they begin a turn and look back at the listener as they finish, effectively handing over the floor with their eyes.

Listeners, in turn, gaze at the speaker to show attention, and may look away when they want to think or to avoid being handed the next turn.

A smile delivered with direct eye contact reads very differently from the same smile aimed at the floor, because gaze tells the receiver who the expression is for.

Eye contact also regulates intimacy: more mutual gaze usually signals closeness or intensity, while averted gaze can signal shyness, deference, or discomfort.

16.1.3 Synchrony between interaction partners

Interactional synchrony is the tendency of two people in conversation to fall into matching rhythms - nodding together, smiling at the same moments, and mirroring each other's expressions.

A closely related phenomenon is facial mimicry, the automatic copying of another person's expression, often within a fraction of a second and without awareness.

Studies using facial electromyography, a technique that measures tiny electrical signals from facial muscles, show that partners' faces track each other in time even when the movements are too small to see.

This matching is linked to rapport, which is the feeling of connection and smooth cooperation between people: pairs who synchronize more tend to like each other more and cooperate better.

Synchrony seems to work in both directions, as feeling connected increases matching and matching increases the feeling of connection.

16.2 Status, Power, and Persuasion

16.2.1 Dominance and submission signals in the face

Across many species, including humans, faces carry signals about who ranks above whom.

Dominance displays typically include lowered brows, a level or slightly raised chin, sparing smiles, and steady direct gaze, while submission displays include raised brows, frequent appeasing smiles, and gaze aversion, which means looking away.

One well studied measure is the visual dominance ratio, the proportion of time a person looks at their partner while speaking compared with while listening.

High status people tend to look plenty while speaking and less while listening, giving a ratio near or above one, while lower status people show the reverse pattern.

These signals are usually produced without conscious planning, yet observers pick them up quickly and use them to judge who is in charge.

16.2.2 Expressions in leadership and politics

Political life is a natural laboratory for facial signaling, because candidates are judged by millions of strangers who mostly see their faces on screens.

Research by the psychologist Alexander Todorov and colleagues found that snap judgments of competence, made from candidates' photographs alone, predicted the winners of United States (US) Senate races at rates well above chance - around 69 percent in one election cycle.

Remarkably, judgments made after seeing a face for only a tenth of a second predicted outcomes about as well as judgments made with unlimited time.

This does not mean the face reveals true leadership ability; it means voters are influenced by rapid appearance-based impressions that may have little to do with actual skill.

Leaders also manage their expressions strategically, using reassuring smiles, stern resolve faces, and displays of anger or empathy calibrated to the moment, and audiences reward or punish them accordingly.

16.2.3 First impressions: trustworthiness and competence read from faces

People form impressions of a stranger's character from the face with astonishing speed.

In a classic study by Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov, participants who saw a face for just 100 milliseconds, which is one tenth of a second, made trustworthiness judgments that closely matched judgments made without any time limit.

Longer viewing did not change the impressions much; it mainly made people more confident in them.

Trustworthiness impressions appear to ride partly on expression-like features: faces whose neutral structure resembles a faint smile tend to look trustworthy, while faces whose structure resembles a faint scowl tend to look untrustworthy.

The crucial caveat is that agreement is not accuracy - observers agree with each other about who looks trustworthy, but these impressions are poor guides to how trustworthy a person actually is.

16.3 Relationships

16.3.1 Expressions in couples and the prediction of relationship outcomes

The psychologist John Gottman brought couples into a laboratory, asked them to discuss a disagreement, and coded every facial expression, vocal tone, and gesture using a system called the Specific Affect Coding System (SPAFF).

From a short slice of interaction, his team reported being able to predict which couples would later divorce with accuracy figures above 90 percent in some studies.

Those headline numbers should be read cautiously, because they came from fitting predictions to couples already observed rather than forecasting new couples in advance, and prediction on fresh samples is harder.

Still, the core finding is robust: the emotional texture of everyday disagreements, written largely on the face, tracks the long term health of a relationship.

Couples who leaven conflict with warmth, humor, and interest fare better than couples whose faces show escalating criticism, defensiveness, and disdain.

16.3.2 Contempt as a corrosive signal

Contempt is the feeling of looking down on someone, and its facial signature is distinctive: a tightening and slight raising of one corner of the mouth, often with an eye roll.

In Gottman's research, contempt was the single strongest predictor of divorce among his "four horsemen" of criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling.

What makes contempt so corrosive is its message: not "I am angry at what you did" but "I am superior to what you are".

Anger between partners can coexist with respect, but contempt communicates that respect is gone.

Frequent contempt in a relationship is also associated with worse health outcomes in the partner on the receiving end, which suggests the signal carries real physiological weight.

16.3.3 Shared laughter and bonding

Laughter is one of the most social of all expressive behaviors, and it does its best work when it is shared.

In a study that video recorded conversations of 71 romantic couples, the proportion of time partners spent laughing at the same moment predicted their relationship quality, closeness, and sense of support.

Researchers think shared laughter works as a signal of similarity - laughing at the same thing at the same time tells both partners that they see the world the same way.

Laughter also triggers the release of endorphins, which are the brain's natural opioid chemicals associated with pleasure and bonding.

Even remembering past shared laughter boosts reported satisfaction, which is one reason couples' private jokes matter more than they might seem.

Chapter 17: Deception, Suppression, and the Managed Face

We do not simply have expressions - we manage them, amplifying some, hiding others, and sometimes faking what we do not feel.

This chapter examines the rules and costs of that management, and takes a hard, evidence-based look at whether the face betrays lies.

17.1 Display Rules and Emotion Work

17.1.1 Cultural display rules: when to show and hide

A display rule is a socially learned norm about which emotions may be shown, to whom, and how strongly.

The idea comes from work by Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen, who proposed that people everywhere feel similar emotions but manage their faces according to local rules.

In a famous study, Japanese and American participants made similar disgust faces while watching an unpleasant film alone, but the Japanese participants masked those expressions with polite smiles when an authority figure was present.

Later research by David Matsumoto and colleagues surveyed more than five thousand people in 32 countries and found systematic cultural differences in how much open expression is endorsed, alongside some universal patterns, such as freer expression toward close in-group members than toward strangers.

Display rules explain how expressions can be biologically rooted and culturally variable at the same time.

17.1.2 Occupational emotion labor: service smiles

The sociologist Arlie Hochschild coined the term emotional labor in her 1983 book The Managed Heart to describe jobs that require workers to display particular emotions as part of the work itself.

Her central example was flight attendants, who were trained to smile warmly at passengers regardless of how they actually felt, and to be, in the airline's words, nicer than natural.

Hochschild distinguished surface acting, which is faking the expression while the feeling stays unchanged, from deep acting, which is working on oneself internally to actually summon the required feeling.

Emotional labor is common in service work, health care, teaching, and call centers, and it falls disproportionately on women, whose jobs more often carry expectations of warmth and accommodation.

Hochschild warned that constantly selling one's smile can leave workers feeling estranged from their own emotions, as though the smile no longer belongs to them.

17.1.3 The cost of chronic suppression

Expressive suppression means deliberately holding back the outward signs of an emotion while the feeling itself continues inside.

Laboratory research by James Gross and colleagues shows that suppression is surprisingly expensive: it consumes mental effort, and people who suppress while viewing emotional material later remember that material worse.

Suppression also raises blood pressure - not only in the person doing the suppressing but, strikingly, in their conversation partner as well.

Socially, suppressors are liked less, build rapport more slowly, and report having less social support and lower life satisfaction than people who regulate emotion in other ways.

An alternative strategy called reappraisal, which means changing how you think about a situation so the feeling itself changes, avoids most of these costs.

The lesson is not that self-control is bad, but that chronically bottling up visible emotion is a costly way to achieve it.

17.2 Microexpressions

17.2.1 The claim: brief leakage of concealed emotion

A microexpression is claimed to be a full facial expression of a hidden emotion that flashes across the face for a fraction of a second before the person regains control.

The idea traces to clinical film analyses in the 1960s and was popularized by Paul Ekman, who argued that concealed feelings leak out because the fast, involuntary emotion system fires before the slower, deliberate control system can clamp down.

On this account, a trained observer who catches the flash can detect what someone truly feels, and by extension, when someone is lying about their feelings.

The claim inspired training programs, security applications, and the television drama Lie to Me.

It is an appealing idea, which is exactly why it deserves careful testing against evidence.

17.2.2 The evidence: rarity, and weak links to deception

When researchers finally examined real faces frame by frame, microexpressions turned out to be rare.

In a careful study by Stephen Porter and Leanne ten Brinke, in which people genuinely felt or concealed emotions on camera, complete microexpressions of the classic type were essentially absent, and even partial fleeting expressions appeared in only about a fifth of participants and about 2 percent of expressions.

Crucially, those fleeting expressions occurred in truth tellers almost as often as in liars, so spotting one tells you little about deception.

Analyses of high stakes real world footage, such as televised pleas by relatives of missing persons, likewise found that microexpressions did not reliably separate the deceptive pleaders from the genuine ones.

Partial and inconsistent emotional leakage does occur, but it is neither the clean, complete flash of the popular story nor a dependable marker of lying.

17.2.3 Why microexpression-based lie detection performs near chance

Human lie detection in general hovers barely above coin flipping: a large meta-analysis, which is a study that statistically combines many previous studies, by Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo covering more than 24,000 judges found average accuracy of about 54 percent, where 50 percent is chance.

Microexpression training has not been shown to lift this figure meaningfully, and it is easy to see why.

If the cue you are trained to spot is rare, appears in honest people too, and signals at most an emotion rather than a lie, then it cannot carry much diagnostic weight.

There is also a logical gap known as the Othello error: a truthful person accused of lying may feel fear, and their fearful face can be misread as guilt.

The candid summary of the scientific literature is that microexpression-based deception detection performs near chance, and confident claims to the contrary outrun the evidence.

17.3 Deception and the Face

17.3.1 Leakage cues and the deception literature's sobering verdict

Leakage is the general idea that concealed feelings escape through channels a liar cannot fully control, such as fleeting facial movements, voice pitch, or fidgeting.

Bella DePaulo and colleagues combined over 1,300 statistical estimates linking specific behaviors to lying and found that almost all supposed cues are weak, unreliable, or absent.

Popular beliefs - that liars avert their gaze, touch their faces, or fidget - are not supported by the data.

Professionals such as police officers and customs agents perform about as poorly as students, and their training and experience mainly increase confidence, not accuracy.

The sobering verdict is that there is no Pinocchio's nose: no single facial or bodily cue reliably marks a lie in all people and situations.

17.3.2 Duping delight and squelched expressions

Duping delight is Ekman's term for the flicker of pleasure some people feel when they believe they are successfully fooling someone, which can leak out as an ill-timed smirk.

A squelched expression is an expression that starts, is noticed by its owner, and is cut off or covered partway through, leaving a briefly distorted face.

Both are plausible occasional leaks, and anecdotes of smirking suspects are memorable.

But as systematic evidence they suffer the same weaknesses as microexpressions: they are infrequent, they have innocent explanations such as nervousness or embarrassment, and they have not been validated as dependable lie signs in controlled research.

They are best treated as interesting possibilities that might prompt further questions, never as proof of deceit.

17.3.3 Applied claims versus laboratory reality: security screening programs

The gap between claim and evidence became a public policy issue with the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) program called Screening of Passengers by Observation Techniques (SPOT), which trained officers to spot would-be attackers from behavioral and facial signs of stress and deception.

The US Government Accountability Office (GAO), the auditing arm of Congress, reviewed the program and concluded that the ability to identify deception from behavioral indicators is the same as or only slightly better than chance.

A follow-up GAO review found that 98 percent of the sources the TSA cited in support of its indicator list did not actually provide valid evidence for them.

The program cost roughly 900 million dollars in its first years and produced no demonstrated security benefit, while raising concerns about profiling.

The episode is a cautionary tale: when face reading claims move from the laboratory to high stakes screening, the standard of evidence should go up, not down.

Chapter 18: Culture, Identity, and the Expressive Face

The same facial machinery is used differently around the world and across social groups.

This chapter explores how culture, history, group membership, and gender shape both how faces express and how faces are read.

18.1 Cultural Styles of Expressiveness

18.1.1 Individualism, collectivism, and display norms

Individualism is a cultural orientation that emphasizes the independent self and personal goals, while collectivism emphasizes group harmony and interdependence.

Matsumoto's 32-country study of display rules found that people in more individualistic countries endorse more overall expressivity, especially of positive emotions and of personal feelings generally.

People in more collectivistic countries endorse greater moderation of expression, particularly of emotions that could disturb group harmony, such as anger toward in-group members.

At the same time, some patterns held everywhere: all cultures endorse more open expression with close others than with strangers, and all endorse some regulation rather than unfiltered display.

Culture, in other words, does not create expression from scratch - it tunes the volume and the audience.

18.1.2 Historical heterogeneity and emotional expressiveness of nations

Why are some nations famously expressive and smiley while others are more reserved?

One influential answer involves historical heterogeneity, which is the degree to which a country's present population descends from many different source countries rather than few.

Research by Magdalena Rychlowska, Paula Niedenthal, and colleagues found that countries built from many migration streams, such as the United States (US), Canada, and Brazil, endorse more expressive display norms and report more smiling and laughter, even after accounting for national wealth.

The proposed reason is that when strangers lack a shared language and shared customs, clear and generous facial signaling becomes essential for cooperation, so expressive norms take root.

In long-settled homogeneous countries, where everyone shares dense common norms, subtlety works fine and smiles are reserved for specific meanings.

This research reframes national expressive styles as reasonable adaptations to different social histories rather than quirks of temperament.

18.1.3 Cultural fit: expressions in immigrants and bicultural individuals

When people move between cultures, their emotional lives gradually shift in a process researchers call emotional acculturation.

Studies by Jozefien De Leersnyder, Batja Mesquita, and colleagues, tracking groups such as Korean immigrants in the US and Turkish immigrants in Belgium, show that immigrants' patterns of emotional experience and expression come to fit the host culture more closely the longer and more deeply they engage with it.

Fit matters: people whose emotional patterns match their cultural surroundings report better relationships and higher wellbeing.

Importantly, gaining fit with the new culture does not require losing fit with the heritage culture - bicultural individuals often develop two emotional repertoires and switch between them depending on context.

Something as intimate as when you smile turns out to be partly a learned cultural skill, and one that can be learned again.

18.2 Reading Across Groups

18.2.1 The in-group advantage in expression recognition

The in-group advantage is the finding that people recognize emotional expressions more accurately when the expresser comes from their own cultural group.

A landmark meta-analysis of 97 studies by Hillary Anger Elfenbein and Nalini Ambady found this advantage across facial expressions, voices, and body postures.

The leading explanation is dialect theory: just as spoken languages have regional accents, emotional expressions have subtle cultural dialects in how they are produced, and perceivers decode their own dialect best.

Consistent with this, the advantage shrinks between cultural groups that have had lots of exposure to one another, the way accents become easier with familiarity.

The picture that emerges is universality with an accent - a shared expressive vocabulary spoken slightly differently everywhere.

18.2.2 Own-race and other-race effects in face reading

The own-race effect, sometimes called the other-race effect, is the well documented finding that people are better at recognizing and remembering faces of their own racial group than faces of other groups.

It arises mostly from experience: we develop finely tuned perceptual expertise for the kinds of faces we see most, and we tend to process other-group faces more superficially.

This matters for emotion reading, because decoding an expression requires noticing small changes against a particular face's baseline, which is harder on faces we individuate poorly.

Studies accordingly find lower accuracy and different error patterns when people judge emotions on other-group faces.

The effect is not fixed - contact, motivation, and training all reduce it - but it is a reminder that face reading skill is shaped by the diet of faces a person grows up with.

18.2.3 Stereotypes and biased perception of expressions

Expression reading is not a neutral measurement; stereotypes about groups can bend what perceivers see.

Research by Amy Halberstadt and colleagues found that both adults and children rate Black faces as angry more often than White faces showing the same expressions, a pattern called anger bias.

In studies of teachers, the misbehavior of Black boys was judged more hostile than identical misbehavior by White boys, a distortion that may feed the large racial gaps in school discipline.

Similar biases operate along gender lines, with ambiguous expressions on male faces more readily seen as anger and on female faces more readily seen as sadness or fear.

These findings show that an expression is not simply read off a face; it is constructed by a perceiver whose expectations are part of the result.

Knowing this is the first step toward correcting for it in classrooms, courtrooms, and policing.

18.3 Gender and Expression

18.3.1 Observed differences in expressiveness and smiling

On average, women and adolescent girls smile more than men and adolescent boys.

A meta-analysis by Marianne LaFrance and colleagues, combining 448 statistical effects from 162 reports, put the difference at a moderate size, and large scale automated analyses of facial video have found the same direction.

Women also self-report greater overall emotional expressiveness, and observers rate them as more facially expressive, particularly for happiness, sadness, and fear.

Men, by contrast, are somewhat more likely to express anger openly.

These are averages with wide overlap - many individual men are more expressive than many individual women - and the differences say nothing by themselves about what each sex actually feels inside.

18.3.2 Social expectation versus biological difference

Are these differences wired in or trained in?

The evidence points mostly, though perhaps not entirely, toward social shaping.

The smiling gap is small or absent in infancy, grows through adolescence, and varies substantially by culture, age, and situation - exactly the fingerprint of learned norms rather than fixed biology.

LaFrance's meta-analysis found the gap widens when people know they are being observed and when gender expectations are salient, and it shrinks when men and women occupy the same social role, such as the same caretaking job or the same position of power.

Display rules for gender begin early, as adults encourage emotional talk and expression more with daughters and discourage displays of sadness or fear more with sons.

A modest biological contribution cannot be ruled out, but much of the difference behaves like a costume that changes with the social scene.

18.3.3 Consequences of expressive stereotypes

Stereotypes about who should express what carry real penalties for those who deviate.

Women who fail to smile in social or service settings are judged colder and less likable than unsmiling men, and strangers feel freer to instruct women to smile.

Research by Victoria Brescoll and colleagues found that men who express anger at work can gain perceived status, while women expressing identical anger lose status and are seen as out of control.

Men face the mirror-image penalty, as displays of sadness, fear, or tears can be read as weakness and cost them perceived competence.

Emotional labor expectations also track gender, concentrating the burden of mandatory pleasantness in female-dominated occupations.

The stereotype thus becomes self-reinforcing: people display what they are rewarded for displaying, and observers take the resulting pattern as proof that the stereotype was true all along.

PART VII: THE CLINICAL FACE

A note before we begin: this part of the book discusses medical conditions that affect the face.

It is written for general education only, and it is not medical advice.

If you or someone you know has symptoms like the ones described here, the right next step is a conversation with a qualified clinician.

With that said, the clinical face is one of the most revealing subjects in the whole science of expression.

When the machinery of making or reading expressions breaks down, we learn what each part of that machinery actually does.

Chapter 19: When Expression Production Fails

This chapter looks at conditions in which the face itself stops moving, either suddenly, from birth, or gradually, even though the person inside may be feeling emotions as vividly as anyone else.

Along the way, these conditions answer a deep scientific question: how much of our emotional life depends on the ability to show it.

19.1 Facial Paralysis

19.1.1 Bell's palsy: sudden one-sided paralysis and recovery

Bell's palsy is a sudden weakness or paralysis of the muscles on one side of the face, usually appearing over hours to a couple of days.

It happens when the facial nerve, the nerve that carries movement commands from the brain to the facial muscles, becomes inflamed and stops conducting signals properly.

The affected side of the face droops, the eye may not close fully, and the smile pulls to one side, which can be frightening because it resembles a stroke, although it is a different and far less dangerous condition.

The outlook is generally good: roughly seventy to eighty percent of people recover completely even without treatment, and early treatment with corticosteroids, a type of anti-inflammatory medicine, raises the recovery rate further.

Most people show clear improvement within about three weeks, though full recovery can take several months.

A minority are left with lingering weakness or with synkinesis, a condition in which recovering nerve fibers rewire to the wrong muscles, so that, for example, the eye squeezes shut whenever the person smiles.

19.1.2 Moebius syndrome: congenital inability to make expressions

Moebius syndrome is a rare condition, present from birth, in which the nerves that control facial movement and sideways eye movement fail to develop properly.

The word congenital simply means present from birth.

People with Moebius syndrome typically cannot smile, frown, raise their eyebrows, or make most other facial expressions, and the paralysis usually affects both sides of the face.

Their emotions are intact - the joy, sadness, and humor are all there - but the face cannot broadcast them.

The condition is very rare, which is one reason careful studies of it are scientifically precious: it is a natural test case of what happens when a person grows up without ever producing a facial expression.

19.1.3 Social consequences of a still face

Because humans lean so heavily on faces to judge each other, a face that does not move is often misread as boredom, unfriendliness, sadness, or low intelligence, none of which is actually true of the person.

Research on people with Moebius syndrome and other facial palsies reports real social costs: strangers misinterpret them, conversations feel harder to start, and some people withdraw from social activities, school, or work out of frustration or embarrassment.

Studies of psychosocial adjustment find that people with facial palsy often report lower social competence than comparison groups, although, encouragingly, many do not differ much on measures of anxiety, depression, or life satisfaction.

The difficulty, in other words, lies less inside the person and more in the reactions of other people, who are unconsciously expecting facial feedback and are unsettled when it does not come.

Many people with facial paralysis learn to compensate through their voice, words, gestures, and posture, channels that can carry a great deal of warmth once a listener learns to attend to them.

19.1.4 What paralysis reveals: recognition can survive without mimicry

One influential theory of emotion holds that we understand other people's expressions partly by subtly imitating them with our own faces, a process called facial mimicry, and then reading our own internal reaction.

If that theory were the whole story, people who cannot move their faces should be poor at recognizing emotions in others.

The evidence says otherwise.

In the largest study of its kind, thirty seven adults with Moebius syndrome performed just as well as thirty seven matched comparison participants on a facial expression recognition task, and accuracy was unrelated to how much facial movement each person could produce.

This is a beautiful example of how a rare condition can settle a theoretical argument: mimicry may normally assist or fine-tune emotion reading, but it is not necessary for it.

The brain evidently has other routes, such as visual analysis and learned knowledge about expressions, that can do the job on their own.

19.2 Neurological Conditions

19.2.1 Parkinson's disease and the masked face (hypomimia)

Parkinson's disease is a progressive brain condition in which cells that produce dopamine, a chemical messenger the brain uses to control movement, gradually die off.

One common result is hypomimia, often called the masked face: a reduction in spontaneous facial movement, so the face appears flat, still, and less animated than the person feels.

Smiles become smaller and slower, blinking decreases, and subtle conversational signals such as eyebrow flashes fade.

The emotional life underneath is usually intact, but listeners often misread the still face as disinterest, coldness, or depression, which can strain relationships and lead others to talk past the person.

Levodopa, the standard medicine that restores some of the missing dopamine, can partially improve facial movement, and speech and physical therapists can teach deliberate techniques and exercises that make expressions larger and more legible.

Researchers are even exploring automated video analysis of hypomimia as a possible early warning sign of the disease.

19.2.2 Stroke: volitional versus emotional paralysis dissociations

A stroke is an injury to the brain caused by a blocked or burst blood vessel, and depending on where the damage lands, it can produce a remarkable split in facial control.

Some stroke patients cannot smile on command - the deliberate, volitional route is broken - yet when a joke genuinely amuses them, a full, symmetrical smile blooms across the supposedly paralyzed side.

Other patients show the mirror image: they can pose a smile perfectly well, but their face stays still during real emotion.

This double pattern, called a dissociation, exists because voluntary commands and emotional commands travel to the facial muscles along partly separate pathways in the brain - roughly, a deliberate route from the motor cortex and a spontaneous route involving deeper emotional structures - and a stroke can sever one route while sparing the other.

Neurologists actually use this sign in diagnosis, because a split between voluntary and emotional movement points to damage inside the brain rather than in the facial nerve itself.

For the science of expression, it is the strongest evidence that the honest, spontaneous smile and the polite, posed smile really are driven by different machinery.

19.2.3 Pseudobulbar affect: involuntary laughing and crying

Pseudobulbar affect, abbreviated as PBA, is a neurological condition in which a person breaks into sudden, uncontrollable laughing or crying that does not match what they actually feel.

Someone with PBA may sob during a pleasant conversation, or laugh at a funeral, while feeling neither sad nor amused, and they typically cannot stop the episode once it starts.

The condition appears after damage to brain networks that normally keep emotional motor programs in check, and it occurs in stroke, multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), traumatic brain injury, and some dementias.

The best current explanation is that the brain contains something like a laughing-and-crying pattern generator in the brainstem and cerebellum, and PBA is what happens when the cortex loses its inhibitory grip on that generator.

PBA is frequently mistaken for depression, but it is a disorder of emotional expression, not of mood, and this distinction matters for treatment.

Medications can reduce the episodes, including a combination of dextromethorphan and quinidine that has been specifically approved for PBA, along with certain antidepressants used for their effect on these circuits.

19.3 Restoring the Face

19.3.1 Smile surgery and facial reanimation

When facial paralysis is permanent, surgeons can sometimes rebuild the ability to smile through procedures collectively called facial reanimation.

The workhorse operation is the gracilis free muscle transfer: a small strip of the gracilis muscle is taken from the inner thigh, along with its nerve and blood vessels, and transplanted into the cheek, where it is connected to a working nerve and takes over the job of pulling the corner of the mouth into a smile.

The donated muscle can be wired to a chewing nerve, in which case the patient learns to smile by gently biting, or to a facial nerve branch from the healthy side of the face, which can eventually allow smiles that appear spontaneously with genuine emotion.

Recovery is gradual: in reported case series, facial symmetry at rest appears after several months, and by around a year many patients can produce an emotionally triggered smile without consciously thinking about it.

These operations are especially meaningful for children with Moebius syndrome, for whom the surgery is sometimes simply called smile surgery.

Results vary from person to person, and the goal is a functional, recognizable smile rather than a perfect replica of the original.

19.3.2 Facial retraining therapy

Not every recovery requires an operation; for many people, the path back runs through therapy.

Facial retraining, also called facial neuromuscular retraining, is a specialized form of physical therapy in which a therapist teaches slow, small, precise facial movements, often practiced in front of a mirror so the patient can see exactly what each muscle is doing.

This visual feedback matters because we normally cannot see our own faces, and after nerve injury the brain needs help re-learning which command produces which movement.

Retraining is particularly useful for synkinesis, the miswiring problem described earlier, where the goal is to relax unwanted co-movements while strengthening the intended ones, sometimes with the help of small botulinum toxin injections that quiet overactive muscles.

Evidence suggests that structured retraining can improve facial symmetry and function after Bell's palsy and other nerve injuries, especially when recovery is incomplete.

Progress is slow and measured in months, which makes patience and daily practice central parts of the treatment.

Chapter 20: When Expression Reading Fails

The previous chapter asked what happens when a face cannot speak; this one asks what happens when a brain cannot listen.

Difficulties in reading facial expressions appear across many different conditions, and each condition mis-reads the face in its own revealing way.

20.1 Autism Spectrum Conditions

20.1.1 Expression recognition findings and their heterogeneity

Autism spectrum conditions are lifelong differences in how a person communicates, socializes, and experiences the world, present from early childhood.

Many studies report that autistic people, on average, score somewhat lower on laboratory tests of facial expression recognition, especially when expressions are subtle or shown briefly.

But the single most consistent finding is inconsistency: some autistic individuals perform entirely typically, others struggle, and averages hide this spread.

The word researchers use for this is heterogeneity, meaning variation within a group, and it warns us against any simple claim that autistic people cannot read faces.

Performance also depends heavily on the test itself - the emotions used, the time allowed, and whether the faces are photographs or real moving people - so a difference on one task often fails to appear on another.

Many autistic adults additionally describe using deliberate, learned strategies to decode expressions that non-autistic people process automatically, which can produce accurate answers achieved by a different mental route.

20.1.2 Gaze to eyes versus mouth

Eye-tracking is a technique that records exactly where a person's gaze lands while they look at an image.

Eye-tracking studies have often found that autistic viewers spend, on average, less time looking at the eye region of faces and relatively more time on the mouth or on non-face details.

This matters because the eyes carry disproportionate information for certain emotions, especially fear, so looking less at the eyes could, by itself, lower scores on recognition tests.

Several researchers have suggested that for some autistic people the eyes feel uncomfortably intense, while others may simply find the mouth more informative, particularly because the mouth moves during speech.

As with recognition itself, the gaze findings are variable across individuals and studies, and reduced eye-looking is a tendency, not a universal rule.

The practical lesson is gentle but important: someone who does not meet your eyes may be processing you closely all the same.

20.1.3 Alexithymia as a hidden factor in autism findings

Alexithymia is a trait, not a disorder, in which a person has difficulty identifying and describing their own emotions.

It occurs in roughly ten percent of the general population, but studies estimate that around half of autistic people meet criteria for it, which creates a serious scientific confound - a hidden third factor that can masquerade as the thing you are studying.

The alexithymia hypothesis proposes that many of the emotion-recognition difficulties attributed to autism are actually driven by co-occurring alexithymia.

Supporting studies have found that when researchers measure both traits, alexithymia predicts poorer expression recognition while autism severity does not, and one brain-imaging study found that alexithymia, not autism, tracked activity in emotion-related brain regions during face tasks.

Other studies push back, finding that autistic traits still predict recognition difficulties after accounting for alexithymia, so the debate is genuinely unsettled.

The honest summary is that emotion reading in autism is shaped by at least two partly separate ingredients, and untangling them is an active research frontier.

20.2 Psychiatric Conditions

20.2.1 Depression: negative bias in reading ambiguous faces

Depression is a mood condition marked by persistent low mood and loss of pleasure, and it changes not only how people feel but how they interpret what they see.

A well-documented effect is negative interpretation bias: when shown an ambiguous face, such as a neutral expression or a morph halfway between happy and sad, people with depression are more likely than others to read it as sad, rejecting, or critical.

Neutral faces are especially treacherous, because a face carrying no signal at all leaves maximum room for the viewer's mood to fill in the blank.

Brain-imaging work links this bias to emotion-evaluation regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex, a strip of cortex on the brain's inner surface involved in weighing emotional significance.

The bias can feed a vicious cycle: reading blank faces as disapproval makes social contact feel punishing, which deepens withdrawal and low mood.

Some researchers are testing whether deliberately retraining this interpretation bias can help, though such training is best understood as experimental rather than established treatment.

20.2.2 Anxiety: heightened vigilance for threat faces

Anxiety disorders involve excessive worry and a nervous system tuned for danger, and that tuning shows up in how anxious people scan faces.

In a classic laboratory task called the dot-probe task, two faces flash on a screen and a small target then appears where one of them was; response speeds reveal where attention had been pointing.

Meta-analyses, which are studies that statistically combine many previous studies, find that anxious participants tend to show an attentional bias toward angry and threatening faces that non-anxious participants do not show.

Anxiety seems to involve both fast vigilance - threat faces grab attention quickly - and slow disengagement, meaning that once attention lands on a threatening face it is hard to pull away.

A fair caveat: the reaction-time version of the dot-probe task has been criticized for poor reliability, and newer eye-tracking versions are giving cleaner measurements.

In everyday terms, an anxious brain is running a threat-detection system with the sensitivity dial turned up, and other people's faces are among the first places it looks.

20.2.3 Schizophrenia: recognition deficits and misattribution

Schizophrenia is a serious mental health condition involving alterations in perception, thinking, and motivation, and difficulty reading facial emotion is one of its most consistent cognitive findings.

People with schizophrenia score lower, on average, across most expression categories, with the difficulty tending to worsen with longer illness and tracking problems in attention and executive function, the mental skills used for planning and flexible thinking.

Beyond simple inaccuracy there is a characteristic direction of error called misattribution: neutral faces are disproportionately read as showing negative or threatening emotions such as anger, fear, or disgust.

You can see how painful this bias could be in daily life - a world of blank faces that all seem hostile - and researchers believe it may feed into paranoid interpretations of other people's intentions.

These recognition difficulties appear largely independent of the condition's more famous symptoms, such as hallucinations, and they are strong predictors of how well a person manages socially.

That is why emotion recognition has become a specific target of the training programs described later in this chapter.

20.2.4 Psychopathy and blunted response to fear and distress faces

Psychopathy is a personality pattern combining shallow emotion, lack of empathy and remorse, and often manipulative or antisocial behavior; it is a research construct measured by clinical checklists, not a casual insult.

A long line of studies reports that people high in psychopathic traits are specifically poor at recognizing fearful faces, and often sad ones, while recognizing happiness and anger relatively normally.

The leading explanation is that distress cues such as fear and sadness normally act as a brake on aggression - they make most people feel bad about causing harm - and that this brake depends on the amygdala, a brain structure that appears to respond weakly in psychopathy.

Later meta-analyses complicated the neat story, finding milder impairments spread across several emotions rather than fear alone, and some individual studies find no fear-specific deficit at all.

So the fair summary is a blunted sensitivity to other people's distress signals, clearest for fear, but neither perfectly selective nor found in every study.

Understanding this blunting matters because it points toward possible early interventions for children who show these traits, such as explicitly training attention to the eye region of faces.

20.3 Brain Injury and Degeneration

20.3.1 Amygdala damage and the loss of fear recognition

The most famous case in this entire field is a woman known in the scientific literature by her initials, S.M., whose amygdala - a small almond-shaped structure deep in each half of the brain - was destroyed on both sides by a rare genetic condition called Urbach-Wiethe disease.

S.M. recognizes happiness, sadness, and anger in faces reasonably well, but she is strikingly impaired at recognizing fear, and she reports experiencing almost no fear herself in daily life.

Eye-tracking revealed something remarkable about why: S.M. does not spontaneously look at the eyes in faces, and the eyes are precisely where fear announces itself, through widened lids and exposed white sclera.

When researchers explicitly instructed her to look at the eyes, her fear recognition temporarily normalized, suggesting the amygdala's job is partly to steer our attention toward the most emotionally informative part of a face.

Other patients with similar amygdala damage do not all show the same pattern, so the amygdala is best understood as a crucial hub for detecting threat-relevant signals rather than a simple fear center.

Few single patients have taught neuroscience as much as S.M. has about how one small structure shapes what we notice in each other.

20.3.2 Huntington's disease and disgust recognition

Huntington's disease is an inherited, progressive brain condition caused by a single faulty gene, best known for involuntary dance-like movements and gradual cognitive decline.

It carries a surprising and specific perceptual signature: a disproportionate difficulty recognizing facial expressions of disgust.

In early studies, when each of the six classic emotions was tested separately, disgust was the only one significantly impaired, and the deficit even appeared in gene carriers who had no visible symptoms yet.

The problem extends beyond faces to disgusted tones of voice and even to foul smells and tastes, suggesting a general disruption of the brain's disgust system rather than a narrow visual problem.

Researchers link this to the basal ganglia and the insula, brain regions damaged early in Huntington's disease and known to be central to processing disgust.

Together with the amygdala findings above, this forms a double dissociation - amygdala damage spares disgust but impairs fear, while Huntington's disease spares fear relatively and impairs disgust - which is the neuroscientist's gold-standard evidence that different emotions rely on partly different brain circuits.

20.3.3 Frontotemporal dementia and the eroding social face

Frontotemporal dementia, abbreviated as FTD, is a group of degenerative brain diseases that attack the frontal and temporal lobes, the brain regions behind the forehead and beside the ears that manage social conduct and meaning.

In its behavioral variant, the earliest casualties are not memories but manners: empathy, tact, and the ability to read other people begin to erode while memory may remain fairly intact.

Studies consistently find impaired recognition of facial emotion early in the disease, most severely for negative emotions such as fear, anger, sadness, and disgust, and the impairment extends to voices and body postures as well.

Brain imaging ties these losses to shrinkage and reduced activity in the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula, temporal poles, and amygdala - essentially the brain's social-emotional network failing as a system.

Families often describe this phase as losing the person's social self while the body remains, and misdiagnosis as depression or a midlife crisis is common early on.

For science, FTD is sobering proof that reading faces is not a luxury skill but a load-bearing pillar of personhood, maintained by dedicated brain tissue that can be lost.

20.4 Assessment and Intervention

20.4.1 Clinical tests of expression recognition

To study or diagnose expression-reading difficulties, clinicians need standardized tests, meaning tests given the same way to everyone so scores can be compared against established norms.

A widely used example is the Ekman 60 Faces test, part of a battery called the Facial Expressions of Emotion: Stimuli and Tests (FEEST), in which the person labels sixty photographs showing the six classic emotions.

Another famous instrument is the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET), which shows only the narrow eye region of faces and asks the viewer to choose which complex mental state - such as doubtful, playful, or worried - best fits.

More modern computerized tasks use morphed faces, images blended by software between two expressions or between neutral and full intensity, to measure exactly how much expression a person needs before they can identify it.

Each format has weaknesses: posed photographs are unrealistically exaggerated, multiple-choice labels test vocabulary as well as perception, and a quiet laboratory is nothing like a noisy, fast-moving social world.

For that reason, clinicians treat these scores as one informative piece of a larger assessment, never as a verdict on a person's social capacity.

20.4.2 Emotion-recognition training programs

If expression reading is partly a skill, it should be trainable, and a number of structured programs have been built on that premise.

In schizophrenia research, social cognition training programs teach emotion recognition step by step - directing attention to informative facial features, comparing expression pairs, and practicing with feedback - and trials show meaningful gains on emotion tasks, with some benefits carrying over to social functioning.

For autistic children, tools have ranged from computer games and interactive videos that teach expression labels to programs pairing face training with everyday practice.

A recurring finding across conditions is that trained tasks improve reliably, while generalization - the transfer of gains to untrained tasks and, above all, to real-world social life - is harder to achieve and harder to measure.

Training is therefore best combined with real social practice rather than delivered as a screen exercise alone.

The honest overall verdict is encouraging but modest: these programs help, most clearly on the skills they directly practice, and they are supports rather than cures.

20.4.3 Oxytocin and pharmacological modulation studies

Oxytocin is a hormone, a chemical messenger produced in the brain, long studied for its role in social bonding, and researchers have tested whether delivering it as a nasal spray can sharpen emotion recognition.

Early small studies were exciting: single doses appeared to improve facial emotion recognition in autistic participants and in people with schizophrenia, in some studies specifically boosting recognition of fear.

Larger and longer trials have been humbling.

The most rigorous long-term trial in autism to date, a large placebo-controlled study known as SOARS-B, found no evidence that weeks of intranasal oxytocin improved core social functioning, and many earlier small effects have failed to replicate.

A placebo-controlled trial is one in which some participants unknowingly receive an inactive spray, so that genuine drug effects can be separated from expectation.

The current scientific consensus is cautious: oxytocin remains a legitimate research tool for probing the social brain, but it is not an established treatment for emotion-recognition difficulties, and no currently available medication reliably is.

Where that leaves us is where this part of the book ends: for now, the most dependable helpers for the clinical face are time, therapy, skilled surgery where appropriate, informed and patient people on the other side of the conversation, and continuing research.

PART VIII: TECHNOLOGY, APPLICATIONS, AND FRONTIERS

Chapter 21: Automatic Facial Expression Recognition

This chapter explains how computers are taught to detect and label facial expressions, what these systems can genuinely do, and where they fall short.

It also looks at the reverse problem - how computers generate expressive faces for animation, avatars, and, more troublingly, deepfakes.

21.1 How Machines Read Faces

21.1.1 From hand-crafted features to deep neural networks

Early computer systems for reading faces relied on hand-crafted features, meaning that human engineers decided in advance which measurements the computer should extract, such as the distances between facial landmarks or the texture of skin wrinkles.

These measurements were then fed into simple statistical classifiers, which are programs that sort inputs into categories based on patterns in past examples.

Since roughly the early 2010s, the field has shifted to deep neural networks, which are layered computing systems loosely inspired by the brain that learn their own features directly from large collections of example images.

Instead of being told what to measure, a deep network discovers, through exposure to many thousands of labeled faces, which patterns of pixels tend to go with which labels.

This shift dramatically improved accuracy on standard tests, and it also moved the field from carefully posed laboratory photographs toward messier, more realistic images.

The trade-off is that deep networks are harder to interpret, because the features they learn are not designed by a person and can be difficult to explain in plain terms.

21.1.2 Action-unit detection versus direct emotion classification

There are two main strategies for automatic facial analysis, and the difference between them matters a great deal.

The first strategy, direct emotion classification, trains a system to output an emotion word such as happy, sad, or angry straight from the image.

The second strategy, action-unit detection, trains the system to report which facial muscle movements are present, using the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), the catalogue of anatomically defined movements called action units that was introduced earlier in this book.

Action-unit detection is the more cautious and more scientifically grounded approach, because an action unit is an observable physical event - a raised brow or a tightened lip corner - rather than a guess about an invisible inner state.

A recent survey of research from 2019 to 2024 highlights action-unit detection as an intermediate, physiologically grounded representation, and modern systems often segment the face into regions and model how action units co-occur.

Many researchers argue that machines should report movements and leave the interpretation of those movements to humans who know the context.

21.1.3 Datasets, benchmarks, and in-the-wild challenges

A dataset is a large collection of examples used to train and test a system, and a benchmark is a shared dataset plus an agreed scoring method that lets different research teams compare results fairly.

The earliest datasets contained posed expressions photographed under controlled lighting, which made the task artificially easy.

The field now emphasizes in-the-wild datasets, meaning collections of faces gathered from ordinary photographs and videos on the internet, with varied lighting, head poses, partial occlusions, and image quality.

Two widely used examples are the Real-world Affective Faces Database (RAF-DB), with roughly thirty thousand internet images labeled by trained human raters, and AffectNet, which contains about one million face images of which roughly four hundred twenty thousand were manually annotated with emotion labels and with continuous ratings of pleasantness and activation.

These in-the-wild collections expose persistent problems, including noisy labels, severe class imbalance - for instance, RAF-DB contains far more happiness examples than disgust examples - and demographic skew in who appears in the images.

Ongoing competition series such as the Affective Behavior Analysis in-the-wild (ABAW) challenges push systems to handle video, audio, and expression together under realistic conditions.

21.2 The Limits of Machine Reading

21.2.1 The inference gap: expression detected is not emotion felt

The single most important limitation of this technology is the inference gap, the logical distance between detecting a facial movement and knowing what a person actually feels.

A system can be excellent at spotting a smile and still be wrong about happiness, because people smile when embarrassed, when polite, when nervous, and when concealing distress.

A landmark 2019 review by Lisa Feldman Barrett, Ralph Adolphs, Stacy Marsella, Aleix Martinez, and Seth Pollak, published in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, examined the evidence and concluded that specific facial configurations are neither reliably nor specifically linked to particular emotions.

Reliability here means that people in a given emotional state do not always produce the expected face, and specificity means that the expected face is also produced in many other states.

The authors even suggested using neutral phrases such as facial configuration rather than emotional expression, to avoid building the conclusion into the vocabulary.

Any claim that software reads emotions should therefore be treated with caution - at best it reads facial movements that are loosely and variably related to emotion.

21.2.2 Bias across demographics and lighting conditions

Automatic face analysis systems do not perform equally well for everyone.

Research has repeatedly found higher error rates for people with darker skin tones and for groups underrepresented in training datasets.

A well-known 2018 study from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) Media Lab, called Gender Shades, found that commercial face analysis systems misclassified light-skinned men less than one percent of the time but darker-skinned women at rates above thirty percent - and while that study examined gender classification rather than emotion, follow-up work has documented related disparities in expression recognition.

Bias can enter through the data, when some groups appear rarely in training images, and through the physics of imaging, because cameras and lighting setups historically tuned for lighter skin capture less usable contrast on darker skin.

Studies auditing emotion datasets and testing recent large multimodal models continue to find performance differences across racial groups.

Because errors are unevenly distributed, deploying these systems in consequential settings risks systematically disadvantaging particular groups of people.

21.2.3 Context blindness of current systems

Human observers judge expressions using context - the situation, the body, the voice, what happened a moment ago, and what they know about the person.

Most deployed automatic systems see only a cropped rectangle of face pixels, frame by frame, and are therefore context blind.

The same wrinkled nose can signal disgust at a smell, concentration in bright sunlight, or the start of a laugh, and nothing in the pixels of the face alone distinguishes these.

Earlier chapters showed that human emotion judgments shift dramatically when the same face is placed on different bodies or in different scenes, so a system that ignores context discards exactly the information people rely on most.

Research on context-aware and multimodal recognition, which combines face, voice, body, and scene, is active, but it remains far from matching the flexible, knowledge-rich inference that people perform effortlessly.

Until then, context blindness is a structural limitation, not a bug that a bigger network automatically fixes.

21.3 Synthesis and Animation

21.3.1 Expression generation in avatars and animated characters

The reverse of recognition is synthesis, meaning the computer generation of expressive faces rather than the reading of real ones.

Film and game studios have long used blendshape rigs, which are digital face models whose surfaces can be smoothly deformed by sliders that often correspond to action units, so that an animator can dial up a brow raise or a lip corner pull.

Performance capture systems record the facial movements of a live actor, frequently through cameras and facial markers, and transfer them onto a digital character.

More recently, generative machine learning models can produce expressive avatar faces automatically, learning from examples rather than requiring an artist to sculpt every movement.

Research systems now aim at fine-grained avatar expressions that preserve a consistent identity while varying emotion, and at emotion-aware three-dimensional talking heads whose expressions can be edited after the fact.

The scientific payoff is that synthetic faces give researchers precise control - as the reverse correlation studies described earlier in this book demonstrated - while the commercial payoff is more lifelike virtual assistants, game characters, and film performances.

21.3.2 Action-unit-controlled face editing and talking heads

A talking head system takes a photograph or short video of a person plus an audio track, and generates a video of that person appearing to speak the audio with plausible lip movements and expressions.

Early systems of this kind were built on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which are pairs of neural networks in which one network generates images and the other critiques them, driving quality upward - examples include GANimation and related face editing methods.

Newer systems increasingly use diffusion models, which are generators that learn to turn random noise into realistic images step by step, and which currently produce the highest quality and most diverse results.

A key research theme is control, and here the Facial Action Coding System reappears - several methods let a user specify which action units should be active and at what intensity, so an expression can be edited in an interpretable, anatomically meaningful way rather than by vague style knobs.

This gives animators and researchers a shared, precise language for commanding synthetic faces, the same language that human coders use to describe real ones.

The same controllability, however, is exactly what makes malicious manipulation easier, which leads to the next topic.

21.3.3 Deepfakes and manipulated expression

A deepfake is a video or image synthesized or altered by machine learning so that a person appears to say or do something they never did.

Two families matter for facial expression - face swapping, which pastes one person's identity onto another's performance, and face reenactment, which keeps the person's identity but puppets their expressions, head movements, and lips from another source.

Reenactment is the more directly expression-focused manipulation, because it fabricates the emotional signal itself - a politician can be made to appear contemptuous, or a witness calm, without any change to identity.

Detection research tries to spot artifacts that generators leave behind, and some approaches use emotional cues themselves, asking whether the expression dynamics in a video are subtly unnatural, though studies from 2025 show that detectors trained on one generation technique often fail on newer ones.

Benchmarks specifically for talking-head and reenactment forgeries, such as TalkingHeadBench and ReenactFaces, have been created because these manipulations are increasingly common and increasingly convincing.

The practical lesson for a lay reader is simple - an expressive face in a video is no longer, by itself, strong evidence of what a person actually expressed.

Chapter 22: Applications and Ethics

This chapter surveys where automatic facial expression analysis is actually used, from advertising tests to hospital wards, and then turns to the uses that scientists and regulators consider unproven or harmful.

It ends with the rules and principles now emerging to govern the technology, including the European Union's new law on artificial intelligence.

22.1 Applications

22.1.1 Market research and media testing

The oldest commercial application of facial coding is market research, where companies measure viewers' facial responses to advertisements, films, and television programs.

A participant watches a video, typically through a webcam and with consent, while software logs smiles, brow furrows, and other movements moment by moment, producing a curve of apparent engagement across the material.

Firms such as Affectiva - founded from MIT Media Lab research and later acquired by the Swedish company Smart Eye - built businesses on this, and Affectiva has reported that a large share of the world's biggest advertisers have used its testing tools.

The appeal is scale and immediacy, because thousands of viewers can be measured cheaply without interrupting them with questionnaires.

The scientific caveats from the previous chapter apply fully - a logged smile indicates a facial movement, not necessarily enjoyment, and aggregate curves are best treated as one noisy signal among several.

Used that way, alongside surveys and sales data, facial coding can flag which scenes hold attention and which fall flat.

22.1.2 Driver monitoring and safety systems

A driver monitoring system is an in-car camera plus software that watches the driver's face for signs of drowsiness and distraction, such as slow blinks, drooping eyelids, yawning, and gaze drifting away from the road.

This is one of the most defensible applications, because the target states are visible behaviors with a direct safety consequence, not hidden emotions.

The European New Car Assessment Programme (Euro NCAP), the influential organization that awards safety star ratings to new cars in Europe, has made effective driver monitoring part of the requirements for top ratings, which has pushed the technology into mass production.

Vendors such as Smart Eye and Affectiva supply systems that track head pose, eye state, and facial movements in real time using infrared cameras that work in darkness.

Notably, the European Union's artificial intelligence law, discussed later in this chapter, carves out safety uses like drowsiness detection from its ban on workplace emotion inference, precisely because detecting a closing eyelid is a much better validated task than detecting a feeling.

22.1.3 Health monitoring: pain detection and mental-health screening

Pain assessment is a genuine clinical problem for patients who cannot describe their pain, including newborns, people with advanced dementia, and sedated patients.

Because pain produces a reasonably consistent cluster of facial actions - brow lowering, eye squeezing, nose wrinkling, and deepening of the fold beside the nose - automated facial analysis can help.

PainChek, an Australian smartphone application, automates facial pain scoring and has achieved regulatory clearance as a medical device in several jurisdictions, and a 2021 validation study of its infant version published in The Lancet Digital Health journal reported strong agreement with established observational pain scales.

Research systems for neonatal intensive care show similar promise, with one 2024 study reporting strong correlation between automated scores and expert ratings, though reviewers stress that current methods still struggle with partially covered faces, movement, and varied lighting.

In mental health, researchers are exploring whether reduced or altered facial expressivity, tracked passively over time from a phone camera, can help screen for depression or monitor treatment response - an approach called digital phenotyping, meaning the measurement of health-relevant behavior through personal devices.

These mental-health uses remain research tools rather than validated diagnostics, and systematic reviews call for larger and more diverse clinical studies before deployment.

22.1.4 Education and human-computer interaction

Affective computing, a term coined by Rosalind Picard of MIT in 1997, is the field that builds computer systems able to sense and respond to human emotional signals.

One long-running idea is the emotionally aware intelligent tutoring system, meaning educational software that adapts its teaching when a student's face and posture suggest confusion, boredom, or frustration.

Research systems have combined facial expressions, head movements, and body posture such as leaning forward to estimate engagement during learning, and some studies report useful predictions of self-reported interest and frustration.

Similar sensing has been proposed for video meetings, customer service kiosks, and social robots, so that machines respond less woodenly to the people using them.

The promise is real but modest, because the systems detect coarse behavioral signals rather than inner states, and their use on students raises consent and surveillance questions serious enough that, as discussed below, the European Union now prohibits emotion inference on students in educational institutions except for medical or safety reasons.

22.2 Contested and High-Risk Uses

22.2.1 Emotion recognition in hiring and workplace surveillance

For several years, some employers used software that scored job candidates partly on their facial expressions during recorded video interviews.

The most prominent vendor, HireVue, analyzed movements such as brow position and smiling as inputs to an employability score.

The practice drew intense criticism from scientists and civil-society groups, including a 2019 complaint to the United States Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the American consumer protection agency, on the grounds that there was no validated evidence linking facial movements to job performance and that the scoring risked discriminating against autistic candidates, people with facial differences, and people whose expressive styles differ culturally.

In January 2021 HireVue announced it had dropped facial analysis from its assessments, noting that the visual data added almost no predictive value.

Related tools have been marketed for monitoring employees' moods at their desks or in call centers, a form of workplace surveillance that scientists criticize on the same evidential grounds and that the European Union has now largely prohibited.

The episode is a case study in how a scientifically shaky inference can be packaged as objective technology and applied to high-stakes decisions.

22.2.2 Security screening and border control claims

Security agencies have repeatedly been drawn to the idea that faces betray hostile intent or deception.

The United States Transportation Security Administration (TSA), the agency that screens air travelers, ran a program called Screening of Passengers by Observation Techniques (SPOT), which from 2006 trained officers to spot behavioral and micro-expression cues partly inspired by Paul Ekman's work.

Government auditors and outside scientists repeatedly found that the program lacked scientific validation, and civil-liberties groups documented complaints that it enabled racial profiling.

In Europe, a research project called iBorderCtrl tested an automated deception detector at borders that scored travelers on thirty-eight facial features while they answered questions from a virtual guard, and independent researchers concluded that its statistical premise was unsound for mass screening.

The core problem is the inference gap again, compounded by base rates - when the condition being screened for is extremely rare, even a fairly accurate test generates overwhelming numbers of false alarms.

Decades of deception research, reviewed earlier in this book, show no reliable facial signature of lying, so face-based lie detection at borders rests on a claim the science does not support.

22.2.3 The scientific critique of emotion inference from the face alone

The contested applications above share one scientific foundation - the assumption that a person's emotional state can be read from their facial movements alone.

The 2019 review by Barrett and colleagues, written by authors from several rival theoretical camps, is the most thorough demolition of that assumption, concluding that how people communicate emotion varies substantially across cultures, situations, and individuals.

The review found, for example, that people in Western samples scowl when angry only a minority of the time, and that scowls also occur frequently outside anger.

This does not mean faces are meaningless - they are rich social signals - but it means the mapping from movement to meaning is many to many and context dependent.

The authors' recommendation to industry was blunt - systems that claim to detect emotion from facial movements are, at best, detecting the movements, and marketing them as emotion detectors outruns the evidence.

That critique now shapes regulation, procurement decisions, and the public conversation about this technology.

22.3 Regulation and Ethics

22.3.1 The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act's limits on emotion recognition

The European Union (EU) Artificial Intelligence Act, usually called the AI Act, is the world's first comprehensive law regulating artificial intelligence, and it entered into force in August 2024.

Its prohibitions became applicable on February 2, 2025.

Article 5 of the Act bans certain practices deemed to pose unacceptable risk, and among them, Article 5(1)(f) prohibits placing on the market or using artificial intelligence systems that infer the emotions of a person in the workplace or in educational institutions.

The ban has narrow exceptions for systems intended for medical or safety reasons, which is why a drowsiness detector in a truck cab remains lawful while a mood scanner in a call center does not.

Emotion recognition systems used in other settings are not banned outright but are classified as high risk, which triggers strict obligations around risk management, data quality, transparency, and human oversight.

The law's stated rationale is that emotion inference rests on weak scientific ground and threatens dignity and privacy, especially in relationships with a power imbalance such as employer and employee or school and student, and the European Commission issued practical guidelines on the prohibitions in February 2025.

22.3.2 Consent, privacy, and facial data

A face is not just an expression carrier - it is also a biometric identifier, meaning a measurable bodily characteristic that can identify a specific person.

Facial images therefore sit under data protection law in much of the world, including the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Europe's broad privacy law, which treats biometric data used for identification as a specially protected category generally requiring explicit consent.

In the United States, the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) requires informed consent before collecting biometric data and lets individuals sue over violations, and it has produced major litigation.

The most famous case involved Clearview AI, a company that scraped billions of face photographs from the public internet without consent to build a face search service - it was fined roughly twenty million euros by the Italian privacy regulator and over seven million pounds by the United Kingdom regulator, and it settled a United States class action in 2025 in a deal valued at roughly fifty-two million dollars.

These cases established that posting a photograph publicly does not amount to consenting to its use in a biometric database.

For expression research specifically, the ethical baseline is informed consent for recording, clear limits on reuse, secure storage, and honesty with participants about the fact that face data can identify them forever, unlike a password that can be changed.

22.3.3 Responsible research and deployment principles

From the scientific critiques and legal developments, a rough consensus on responsible practice has emerged.

First, claim only what is measured - report facial movements or behaviors such as eyelid closure, and do not relabel them as inner emotions.

Second, validate for the population and setting of actual use, testing accuracy separately across skin tones, ages, genders, cultures, and lighting conditions, and publishing the results.

Third, match the stakes to the evidence - a system that suggests which advertisement scene is boring can tolerate errors that a system influencing hiring, policing, or border decisions cannot.

Fourth, obtain meaningful consent, minimize data retention, and give people a way to contest automated judgments about them.

Fifth, keep a human in the loop for any consequential decision, using the machine's output as one fallible input rather than a verdict.

These principles echo both the recommendations of the 2019 Barrett review and the structure of the EU AI Act, and they give researchers, buyers, and citizens a practical checklist for judging any product that claims to read faces.

Chapter 23: Open Questions and Future Directions

This closing chapter takes stock of what the science of facial expression still does not know, and describes the new methods that may answer those questions.

It ends by sketching what an integrated science of the expressive face could look like.

23.1 Unresolved Scientific Questions

23.1.1 How tightly are expressions coupled to felt emotion

Perhaps the deepest open question in the field is the strength of the link between what a face does and what its owner feels.

The evidence reviewed throughout this book shows the link is real but loose - people in a given emotional state produce the textbook expression only some of the time, and the same expression appears across many states.

Quantifying that looseness precisely, and explaining when the coupling tightens - perhaps at high emotional intensity, or in genuinely social moments - and when it loosens, remains unfinished work.

A related open question runs in the other direction, through the facial feedback hypothesis, the idea that making an expression can influence the feeling itself.

A 2019 meta-analysis by Nicholas Coles, Jeff Larsen, and Heather Lench, pooling over one hundred thirty studies, found facial feedback effects that are real but small and variable, and the Many Smiles Collaboration, a 2022 multi-laboratory test with nearly three thousand nine hundred participants in nineteen countries published in Nature Human Behaviour, confirmed that posed smiles modestly increase reported happiness while a less obtrusive pen-in-mouth method gave inconclusive results.

So the face and feeling influence each other, but neither is a simple readout of the other, and mapping the true two-way coupling is a task for the coming decades.

23.1.2 Which components of expression are innate and which learned

The universality debate, traced across earlier chapters, has matured into a component-by-component question - not whether expression as a whole is innate or learned, but which pieces are which.

Evidence from newborns, from people blind since birth who produce recognizable spontaneous expressions, and from cross-species homologies suggests that some building blocks of facial behavior are part of our biological inheritance.

At the same time, work such as Rachael Jack and Philippe Schyns' data-driven studies shows that the fine temporal dynamics and some categorical distinctions differ between cultures, indicating learned accents layered on shared machinery.

Sorting expression into its innate scaffolding and its culturally tuned surface requires developmental studies, cross-cultural studies, and comparative animal studies that all use the same measurement language.

The action-unit level may be the right resolution for this project, since individual muscle movements can be tracked across infants, cultures, and species in a way that whole labeled expressions cannot.

The honest current answer is that the innate versus learned boundary has not yet been drawn with confidence for any expression.

23.1.3 Can production and perception findings be unified in one theory

The science of facial expression has two halves that do not yet fit together cleanly.

Production research asks what faces actually do during emotional episodes, and it finds variability, context dependence, and weak links to inner state.

Perception research asks what observers see in faces, and it finds fast, confident, fairly consistent emotion judgments - people readily read anger into a scowl even though scowls are unreliable anger signals.

A unified theory must explain both facts at once - why production is so variable and why perception behaves as if it were not.

Candidate frameworks include behavioral ecology views that treat expressions as social tools rather than emotion readouts, constructionist views that locate the categories in the perceiver's learned concepts, and signaling accounts on which partly reliable cues are still worth reacting to.

No current theory commands consensus, and building one that survives the production data, the perception data, the cultural data, and the developmental data together is arguably the field's central theoretical challenge.

23.2 Emerging Methods

23.2.1 Naturalistic, longitudinal recording of everyday faces

Most of what science knows about facial expression comes from brief laboratory sessions, which is a bit like studying weather only inside a greenhouse.

New tools now allow recording of real faces in real life over long periods.

These include wearable facial electromyography (EMG), the measurement of muscle electrical activity through small skin sensors, which a 2021 study showed can track the pleasantness of experience from cheek and brow muscles during ordinary daily situations.

They also include head-mounted and home cameras used in infant development research, workplace recording projects, and experience sampling methods, in which phones prompt people to report their feelings at random moments so that facial data can be paired with in-the-moment self-reports rather than distant recollection.

Longitudinal recording, meaning repeated measurement of the same people across weeks or years, can reveal how expressive style develops, how it varies with mental health, and how rare but important expressions actually occur in daily life.

The accompanying challenge is ethical, since continuous face recording is exactly the kind of pervasive surveillance the previous chapter warned about, so this research depends on strong consent and data protection practices.

23.2.2 Data-driven discovery of expression categories beyond the basic six

Rather than starting from six preselected emotion labels, data-driven approaches let large datasets and statistics reveal how many distinct expression categories people actually use.

Alan Cowen and Dacher Keltner, in work from 2017 onward, applied this strategy to thousands of emotional videos and images, finding that self-reported experience formed around twenty-seven categories linked by smooth gradients, and that naturalistic facial and bodily expression reliably conveyed roughly twenty-eight categories - far more than six - including states like awe, interest, contentment, and embarrassment.

Their framework, called semantic space theory, treats emotion as a high-dimensional space, meaning a map with many independent directions, rather than a short list of boxes, and reports that the classic six basic emotions capture under a third of the variation in people's responses.

Complementary work by Rachael Jack, Philippe Schyns, and colleagues used reverse correlation with animated random face movements and found that, over time, expressions transmit information hierarchically, with early broad signals that later refine, and that four rather than six patterns were common across the cultures tested.

These approaches disagree on the final count, which is itself informative - the number of categories depends on the method, the culture, and the granularity chosen.

The shared lesson is that the true landscape of human facial expression is richer than the six-face charts that dominated the twentieth century.

23.2.3 Cross-species facial coding and comparative signal analysis

Darwin's founding insight was that human expressions have evolutionary relatives in other animals, and modern tools now let that idea be tested rigorously.

Researchers led by figures including Bridget Waller and Lisa Parr have adapted the Facial Action Coding System to other species based on facial anatomy, producing ChimpFACS for chimpanzees - recently extended to bonobos - along with versions for macaques, gibbons, orangutans, marmosets, gorillas, dogs (DogFACS), cats (CatFACS), and horses (EquiFACS).

Because each system is anchored in muscles that are anatomically homologous, meaning inherited from a shared ancestor, scientists can compare facial signals across species movement by movement instead of by loose impression.

This work has already shown that chimpanzee facial musculature is far more similar to ours than once assumed, and that dogs produce certain brow movements more when humans are watching, hinting at communicative use.

Automated video analysis is beginning to make animal facial coding scalable, opening questions about pain and welfare assessment in livestock and pets.

Comparative coding turns the face into a place where the study of emotion, communication, and evolution meet with shared units of measurement.

23.3 Toward an Integrated Science of the Expressive Face

23.3.1 Bridging production, perception, culture, and computation

The field's likely future is integration - single studies and shared datasets that connect what faces do, what observers perceive, how culture shapes both, and what computational models can capture.

Computation is becoming the common workbench, since the same automated action-unit measurement can be applied to laboratory videos, home recordings, historical film, and animal footage, giving previously separate literatures one measuring stick.

Generative models supply the other half of the toolkit, letting researchers synthesize precisely controlled dynamic faces to probe perception in any culture, as reverse correlation studies already demonstrate.

Theory must integrate as well, treating an expression simultaneously as a possible readout of internal state, a social signal shaped by audience, a culturally accented behavior, and a physical event in muscle and skin.

Adversarial collaborations, in which theoretical rivals design studies together as happened in the 2019 Barrett review and the Many Smiles project, offer a working model for making progress on questions where camps have long talked past one another.

An integrated science would replace the question does the face express emotion with the better question - which facial movements carry which information, for whom, in which contexts.

23.3.2 The face as one channel in whole-body, in-context emotion communication

The final lesson of this book is a matter of perspective - the face, for all its richness, is one channel among many.

Human emotional communication also flows through the voice, posture, gesture, touch, gaze, timing, words, and the shared situation, and perceivers integrate all of it, usually without noticing.

Studies described earlier show that bodies and scenes can override faces, that intense joy and intense grief can look alike in the face alone, and that removing context makes even confident judgments unreliable.

Future research is accordingly moving toward whole-person, in-context measurement, recording face, body, and voice together in real interactions rather than isolated portraits.

For technology, this argues against face-only emotion inference and for multimodal, context-aware systems deployed with humility about what they can know.

For the rest of us, it restores the face to its proper place - not a window that displays the soul, but a subtle, flexible instrument of social life, best understood as part of a whole person acting in a particular moment.

PART IX: THE DIGITAL FACE: EMOJI AND EMOTIONAL EXPRESSION

Chapter 24: Emoji as Facial and Emotional Expression

When conversation moved onto screens, the expressive face did not disappear - people rebuilt it out of punctuation marks, pixels, and standardized symbols.

This chapter traces how emoji arose and how they encode both facial and non-facial emotional expression.

24.1 From Emoticon to Emoji

24.1.1 Text emoticons: the sideways Western smiley born on early computer networks

An emoticon is a facial expression built entirely from keyboard characters, such as the sideways smiley made from a colon, a hyphen, and a closing parenthesis.

The best-documented origin is a 1982 message-board post by the computer scientist Scott Fahlman at Carnegie Mellon University, who proposed the smiley to mark jokes and the frowning version to mark serious remarks.

Emoticons spread because plain text strips away the face, the voice, and the body all at once, leaving written words easy to misread.

A tiny punctuation face restored some of the missing emotional channel at almost no cost.

Notice that the Western emoticon is read sideways, with the head tipped ninety degrees to the left.

24.1.2 Kaomoji: upright Japanese text faces built around the eyes

Kaomoji, a Japanese word meaning face characters, are text faces that emerged in Japan in the 1980s and are read upright rather than sideways.

Where the Western smiley changes its mouth to change its emotion, kaomoji typically hold the mouth constant and vary the eyes, using characters from many alphabets to draw joy, tears, or effort.

This East-West difference mirrors a finding from cross-cultural expression research: perceivers in Japan tend to weight the eyes when reading emotion, while perceivers in the United States tend to weight the mouth.

The two emoticon traditions thus independently rediscovered the diagnostic face regions favored by their own cultures.

Kaomoji also embraced a wider emotional repertoire early on, including embarrassment, apology, and effortful determination.

24.1.3 Shigetaka Kurita's 1999 emoji set and its manga and pictogram roots

The word emoji comes from Japanese, combining e (picture) and moji (character), and by coincidence rather than design it resembles the English word emotion.

The first widely recognized emoji set was created in 1999 by the designer Shigetaka Kurita for i-mode, the mobile internet service of the Japanese carrier NTT DoCoMo.

Kurita drew 176 tiny pictures on a grid of twelve by twelve pixels, drawing on manga (Japanese comic) conventions, weather symbols, and existing emoticons.

His goal was efficient information, not only feeling: the set mixed hearts and faces with weather, transport, and time symbols.

The set was considered influential enough that the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired it for its permanent collection in 2016.

24.1.4 Unicode standardization and the worldwide spread of a shared emoji vocabulary

Unicode is the international standard that assigns a unique code number to every character so that text displays consistently across devices and languages.

Emoji entered the Unicode standard in 2010, which meant that a smiley sent from one phone maker's device could be recognized on another's.

Adoption exploded after Apple and Google added emoji keyboards, and by the mid-2020s the standard included several thousand emoji characters and sequences.

A milestone in cultural recognition came in 2015, when Oxford Dictionaries named the face-with-tears-of-joy emoji its word of the year.

Emoji are now a global vocabulary of emotional expression used across languages, which makes them a natural subject for the science of facial expression.

24.2 The Emoji Face Set

24.2.1 Mapping smiley faces onto the classic expression categories: happy, sad, angry, fearful, surprised, disgusted

The emoji face inventory contains clear counterparts to each of the six classic expression categories studied since Ekman's early work.

Happiness has the smiling faces, sadness the frowning and crying faces, and anger the red-faced scowl with lowered brows.

Fear appears in the screaming face, which borrows its open-mouthed, hands-on-cheeks pose from Edvard Munch's painting The Scream, and in the anxious face with widened eyes.

Surprise is the round-eyed, open-mouthed astonished face, and disgust appears in the nauseated green face and the vomiting face.

The mapping is real but loose: emoji designers were drawing readable cartoons, not coding action units, so many emoji blend or exaggerate features from several categories.

24.2.2 Faces beyond the basic six: tears of joy, heart-eyes, rolling eyes, smirk, pleading face, and grimace

The emoji set goes far beyond the basic six, encoding social and blended emotions that laboratory photograph sets rarely include.

The face with tears of joy captures overwhelming amusement, and the heart-eyes face captures adoration or desire.

The rolling-eyes face conveys contempt or exasperation, the smirking face conveys smugness or insinuation, and the pleading face with large glossy eyes performs the wide-eyed appeal of a begging child.

There are also faces for embarrassment (flushed face), awkwardness (grimacing face), skepticism (raised eyebrow), thoughtfulness (thinking face with chin on hand), and mixed overwhelm (loudly crying face, melting face).

In this respect the emoji inventory resembles the larger data-driven emotion taxonomies of modern research more than the older six-category scheme.

24.2.3 Exaggeration and stylization: emoji as caricatures of real expressions

Emoji faces are caricatures: they exaggerate the most diagnostic features of an expression and strip away everything else.

A caricature can be easier to recognize than the real thing, an effect perception researchers call the caricature advantage, because it moves a face further from ambiguous middle ground.

Emoji smiles curve impossibly high, emoji tears become waterfalls, and the angry face turns literally red, borrowing a bodily metaphor of heat and pressure.

This stylization is one reason tiny twelve-pixel and later vector faces read instantly at a glance.

It also means emoji encode folk beliefs about expression - what a culture thinks anger looks like - as much as they encode measured facial behavior.

24.2.4 What emoji faces lack: timing, intensity gradations, and Duchenne eye markers

Real facial expressions unfold over time, with an onset, a peak, and a fading offset, and their timing carries information about sincerity.

An emoji is a frozen snapshot with no dynamics, so everything research says about reading genuineness from timing is unavailable.

Emoji also come at fixed intensities: a sender chooses between discrete faces rather than dialing a smile from faint to beaming, although senders improvise intensity by repeating an emoji many times.

Most smiling emoji do not distinguish a Duchenne smile, the enjoyment smile that crinkles the eyes, from a polite social smile, though the smiling-face-with-smiling-eyes emoji comes close to encoding exactly that contrast.

Finally, an emoji is fully deliberate: there is no spontaneous, involuntary pathway to leak a concealed feeling, so the face on the screen is always the managed face.

24.3 Emotional Expression Beyond the Face

24.3.1 Gesture and body emoji: thumbs up, shrug, facepalm, and folded hands

Emotional expression is broader than the face, and the emoji set reflects this with hands, gestures, and whole-body poses.

The thumbs up signals approval, the shrug signals indifference or uncertainty, and the facepalm - a palm pressed to the face - signals exasperation or secondhand embarrassment.

The folded-hands emoji is used for please, thank you, and prayer, and the flexed biceps for strength or encouragement.

Gesture emoji are more culturally hazardous than face emoji, because hand signs that are friendly in one country can be rude in another.

Their popularity shows that users treat emoji as a full nonverbal channel, not just a face substitute.

24.3.2 Symbolic emotion marks inherited from manga: the sweat drop, the anger symbol, and steam from the nose

Manga developed a rich symbolic shorthand for emotion, and several of its marks passed directly into the emoji set through Kurita's original design.

The single large sweat drop signals nervousness, embarrassment, or relief, and appears both as a standalone droplet and on faces such as the downcast face with sweat.

The anger symbol, a red four-lobed mark representing bulging veins on a temple, marks irritation without any face at all.

The face with steam from the nose comes from the manga convention of a snorting, worked-up character and today often expresses frustration or determination.

These symbols demonstrate that a culture can build readable emotional signals that are not pictures of facial muscle movements at all.

24.3.3 Hearts, broken hearts, and objects as emotional signals

The heart is the most-used emotional emoji of all, and it is not a face.

The emoji set includes hearts in many colors, whose meanings have drifted into a folk code of love, friendship, grief, and loyalty, along with the beating heart, the growing heart, and the heart with an arrow.

The broken heart communicates sorrow and disappointment in a single symbol, and the red heart alone can carry the entire emotional content of a message.

Object emoji also do emotional work by association: fire for excitement or admiration, the party popper for celebration, sparkles for delight or gentle irony, and the skull for figurative death.

In the vocabulary of this book, these are pure emotion signals with no readout component: they express feeling without depicting a body at all.

24.3.4 Skin tones, gender variants, and identity in expressive emoji

Since 2015 the Unicode standard has allowed most human emoji to carry one of five skin-tone modifiers based on the Fitzpatrick scale, a dermatological classification of skin types.

Research on social media shows that most users who adopt skin tones pick ones matching their own appearance, using modified emoji for self-representation rather than commentary about others.

Gendered variants and later gender-neutral forms extended the same principle, letting the tiny expressive body on the screen resemble its sender.

Identity options interact with emotional signaling: a wave, a flexed arm, or folded hands feels more personal when it looks like the person sending it.

The expressive face in the digital world is thus also a chosen face, selected and customized in ways a biological face never is.

Chapter 25: How Emoji Are Read and Used

Emoji only matter because readers extract emotion from them, and the science of how they are read connects directly to the face-perception research of earlier chapters.

This chapter reviews evidence that emoji tap face-reading machinery, the ways they are misread, and their uses in research and industry.

25.1 Do Emoji Work Like Faces

25.1.1 Brain and bodily responses: emoji evoke reactions resembling responses to real faces

Studies using facial electromyography, the recording of electrical activity in facial muscles, find that people show mimicry-like muscle responses to happy and angry emoji resembling their responses to photographs of human faces.

Brain-recording studies report that emotional emoji engage face-sensitive processing and that emotion categories read from emoji overlap in their neural patterns with those read from human faces.

At the same time, responses to emoji are generally weaker and more dependent on learned convention than responses to real faces.

A cautious summary is that emoji borrow the face-reading system rather than fully activating it.

That borrowed power is exactly why a cartoon circle with two dots and a curve can change the felt tone of a sentence.

25.1.2 Emoji as substitutes for the missing nonverbal channel in text

Face-to-face conversation carries words plus expression, gaze, tone of voice, and posture, and text messaging deletes everything but the words.

Emoji restore a compact version of that lost channel, marking the writer's stance toward what the words say.

Linguists studying computer-mediated communication find that emoji rarely replace words; instead they accompany them, the way a smile accompanies a spoken sentence.

This is why the same emoji can accompany opposite messages - a smile after good news amplifies it, while a smile after a complaint softens it.

Readers, in turn, judge messages with well-chosen emoji as warmer and their senders as friendlier, though in some formal contexts emoji use can lower judgments of competence.

25.1.3 Pragmatic uses: softening requests, marking irony, and managing tone

Pragmatics is the branch of linguistics that studies how meaning depends on context and intent, and emoji are pragmatic tools above all.

A winking face converts a criticism into a tease, signaling that the literal words are not the full message.

A smiley after a request works like a polite smile at a counter, softening an imposition.

Emoji also close messages the way expressions close face-to-face encounters, replacing sign-offs, and they can defuse ambiguity in advance rather than repair it afterward.

Because these functions are conventional, they must be learned, and newcomers to a platform or age group can misfire even when they know what each face depicts.

25.2 Ambiguity and Misreading

25.2.1 Cross-platform rendering differences and the Miller interpretation studies

The Unicode standard defines an emoji's identity but not its artwork, so each platform draws its own version of every face.

In a well-known 2016 study, Hannah Miller and colleagues at the University of Minnesota showed people popular face emoji as rendered by different companies and asked them to rate the emotion conveyed.

The same emoji character could be read as clearly positive in one company's drawing and clearly negative in another's - the grinning-face-with-smiling-eyes emoji being the famous case, rendered joyful on some platforms and grimacing on others.

Even when viewers saw the identical drawing, their sentiment ratings disagreed substantially, and follow-up work found that surrounding text reduces but does not eliminate the disagreement.

Platforms have since converged their designs somewhat, partly in response to this research, but rendering differences remain a live source of miscommunication.

25.2.2 Context-free ambiguity and meaning drift: the skull as laughter

Many emoji are ambiguous even before platform differences enter, because a static cartoon face underdetermines the emotion behind it.

Corpus studies that ask people to label emoji without context find wide disagreement for a large fraction of the inventory, especially for faces depicting blends like the smiling face with tear.

Meanings also drift over time through slang: the skull emoji now commonly means dying of laughter, the loudly crying face can mean overwhelmed amusement, and the upside-down face marks irony or resigned chaos.

Such drift mirrors the lesson of the cross-cultural expression debate: the meaning of an expressive signal is fixed neither by anatomy nor by the picture, but by the community using it.

For the science of expression, emoji provide a natural laboratory where the conventionalization of emotional signals can be watched in real time.

25.2.3 Generational and cultural dialects of emoji use

Emoji use varies by age group in ways strong enough to cause real misunderstandings, echoing the dialect theory of facial expression from earlier chapters.

The plain thumbs up reads as friendly approval to older users but can read as cold or passive-aggressive to younger ones, and the classic slightly-smiling face is often used by younger writers to signal strained politeness rather than happiness.

Cultural differences also persist: the meanings attached to particular hearts, flowers, and gestures differ between countries, and some gesture emoji carry offense in specific regions.

Studies of large message corpora find national differences in overall emoji frequency and in the balance of positive and negative faces.

Reading emoji, like reading faces, is done with cultural expectations and not with the eyes alone.

25.3 Research and Applications

25.3.1 Emoji sentiment lexicons and automatic sentiment analysis

Sentiment analysis is the automatic estimation of the positive or negative feeling in a piece of text, and emoji are among its strongest signals.

Researchers led by Petra Kralj Novak built the first large emoji sentiment lexicon in 2015 by having human annotators label tens of thousands of tweets in multiple languages, yielding a sentiment score for hundreds of emoji.

Most emoji in such lexicons score positive, and emoji-bearing messages tend to be more emotional overall than plain-text messages.

Modern language models learn emoji meanings directly from data, and emoji often serve as weak labels that help systems learn emotion categories without hand annotation.

The caveats of the ambiguity research apply with full force: a lexicon built on one platform, language, or era can quietly go stale as meanings drift.

25.3.2 Emoji as measurement tools in mood, consumer, and health research

Because emoji are familiar and language-light, researchers use them as response scales, asking participants to report feelings by choosing faces instead of words.

Emoji scales are popular in consumer research on food and products, and in research with children and across language communities, where verbal emotion vocabularies differ.

In health settings, emoji-based scales descend from earlier pictorial instruments such as the faces pain scale, letting patients report pain or mood quickly.

Studies of everyday messaging also treat spontaneous emoji use as a window on mood, with shifts in a person's emoji mix accompanying shifts in wellbeing.

As always, convenience trades against precision: an emoji response compresses a rich feeling into one cartoon, and interpretation differences between users add noise.

25.3.3 Open questions: emoji as a still-evolving system of emotional expression

Emoji raise versions of every open question in this book: how tightly any expressive signal is coupled to felt emotion, how much of its meaning is innate resemblance versus learned convention, and how production and perception co-evolve.

They also raise new ones, such as what happens to emotional communication when the expression channel is fully deliberate, with no involuntary leakage at all.

The inventory itself keeps evolving through the Unicode proposal process, in effect a committee deciding which new emotional expressions the world's keyboards will offer.

Emoji even feed back into face research, serving as controllable stimuli for studying which visual features carry which emotions.

Whatever their future, emoji demonstrate the central claim of this manuscript: human beings are so built for facial and bodily emotional expression that, given only a keyboard, they will reinvent the face.

APPENDIX A: KEY TEXTBOOKS AND MONOGRAPHS

• Charles Darwin, The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals

This 1872 book founded the scientific study of expression by arguing that human expressions are evolved behaviors continuous with those of other animals.

• Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne, The Mechanism of Human Facial Expression

Published in 1862 and based on electrical stimulation of facial muscles, it mapped which muscles produce which expressions and gave its author's name to the genuine Duchenne smile.

• Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen, Unmasking the Face

This 1975 practical guide taught general readers to recognize the proposed universal expressions and remains the most accessible statement of the classic basic emotions view.

• Paul Ekman, Emotions Revealed, and Telling Lies

Emotions Revealed (2003) summarizes Ekman's mature account of emotion and expression for lay readers, while Telling Lies (1985) examines deception and the leakage of concealed feeling through the face.

• Paul Ekman and Erika Rosenberg (editors), What the Face Reveals

This edited collection, first published in 1997 and now in its third edition, gathers studies of spontaneous expression measured with the Facial Action Coding System and shows the method at work across clinical and social research.

• Alan Fridlund, Human Facial Expression: An Evolutionary View

This 1994 monograph presents the behavioral ecology view, arguing that facial displays are social tools directed at audiences rather than readouts of inner emotion.

• James Russell and Jose Miguel Fernandez-Dols (editors), The Psychology of Facial Expression, and The Science of Facial Expression

These edited volumes (1997 and 2017) collect the major competing perspectives and empirical reviews, serving as the field's standard debate books across two decades.

• Lisa Feldman Barrett, How Emotions Are Made

This 2017 book presents the theory of constructed emotion for a general audience, arguing that emotion categories are built by the brain from prediction and concepts rather than triggered as fixed biological programs.

• Rachael Jack and Philippe Schyns, work collected on dynamic face signaling and culture

Their body of work introduced data-driven reverse correlation with dynamic computer-generated faces, revealing cultural diversity in expression representations and the hierarchical unfolding of facial signals over time.

• Bridget Waller and colleagues, comparative facial coding systems (ChimpFACS, DogFACS)

These anatomically based coding systems extend the Facial Action Coding System to other species, enabling rigorous cross-species comparison of facial signals and their evolution.

• Marcel Danesi, The Semiotics of Emoji

This 2017 monograph treats emoji as a sign system, analyzing how a standardized picture vocabulary came to carry tone, emotion, and identity in digital writing.

APPENDIX B: LANDMARK PAPERS AND REVIEWS

• Duchenne, electrical stimulation studies of facial muscles (1862)

These experiments established the muscular basis of individual expressions and first distinguished smiles involving the eye-region muscle from smiles of the mouth alone.

• Darwin, The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872)

This work launched the evolutionary study of expression and posed the universality question that has driven the field ever since.

• Ekman and Friesen, Constants Across Cultures in the Face and Emotion (1971)

This study of the Fore people of Papua New Guinea reported that members of an isolated culture matched expression photographs to emotion stories, becoming the key evidence for universality claims.

• Ekman and Friesen, the Facial Action Coding System (1978)

This manual defined the action units that let any observable facial movement be coded objectively, providing the measurement foundation for both human research and modern computer vision.

• Ekman, Levenson, and Friesen, Autonomic Nervous System Activity Distinguishes Among Emotions (1983)

This Science paper reported that posing emotion-specific facial configurations produced distinct bodily response patterns, linking the face to physiology and energizing the facial feedback debate.

• Russell, Is There Universal Recognition of Emotion from Facial Expression (Psychological Bulletin, 1994)

This methodological critique showed how forced-choice formats and posed photographs inflated apparent universality, forcing a re-examination of the classic cross-cultural evidence.

• Fridlund, Human Facial Expression: An Evolutionary View (1994)

This book-length argument reframed expressions as audience-directed social signals, founding the behavioral ecology alternative to emotion-readout accounts.

• Haxby, Hoffman, and Gobbini, The Distributed Human Neural System for Face Perception (2000)

This review proposed the influential model separating brain pathways for invariant facial identity and changeable aspects such as expression, organizing two decades of face neuroscience.

• Adolphs, Recognizing Emotion from Facial Expressions (2002)

This review synthesized the psychology and neuroscience of how observers recognize emotion in faces, detailing the roles of the amygdala and cortical regions.

• Susskind and Anderson, Expressing Fear Enhances Sensory Acquisition (Nature Neuroscience, 2008)

This study showed that fear and disgust faces respectively widen and narrow sensory intake, supporting Darwin's idea that expressions originated in bodily function before becoming signals.

• Jack, Garrod, Yu, Caldara, and Schyns, Facial Expressions of Emotion Are Not Culturally Universal (PNAS, 2012)

Using reverse correlation with dynamic synthetic faces, this paper showed that East Asian and Western observers hold different internal models of emotional expressions, directly challenging strict universality.

• Crivelli, Russell, Jarillo, and Fernandez-Dols, The Fear Gasping Face as a Threat Display in a Melanesian Society (PNAS, 2016)

This fieldwork with Trobriand Islanders found the canonical fear face interpreted as an angry threat display, showing that even seemingly basic expressions can carry different meanings across cultures.

• Gendron, Crivelli, and Barrett, Universality Reconsidered (Current Directions in Psychological Science, 2018)

This review re-evaluated the cross-cultural evidence with modern methods and concluded that emotion perception shows meaningful cultural variation alongside partial commonality.

• Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez, and Pollak, Emotional Expressions Reconsidered (Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2019)

This comprehensive multi-author review concluded that facial configurations are neither reliable nor specific indicators of particular emotions, reshaping both the science and the debate over emotion-reading technology.

• Coles, Larsen, and Lench, meta-analysis and many-labs tests of the facial feedback hypothesis (2019-2022)

The 2019 meta-analysis found small, variable facial feedback effects, and the 2022 Many Smiles Collaboration confirmed across nineteen countries that posed smiles modestly increase reported happiness.

• Shigetaka Kurita, the original NTT DoCoMo emoji set (1999; acquired by the Museum of Modern Art, 2016)

This set of 176 twelve-by-twelve-pixel pictures, drawing on manga symbols and emoticons, launched the emoji as a standardized channel for emotional expression on screens.

• Miller, Thebault-Spieker, Chang, Johnson, Terveen, and Hecht, Blissfully Happy or Ready to Fight: Varying Interpretations of Emoji (2016)

This study showed that the same emoji character is read with different emotions across platforms and even across viewers of the identical drawing, establishing emoji ambiguity as a research topic.

• Novak, Smailovic, Sluban, and Mozetic, Sentiment of Emojis (PLOS ONE, 2015)

This paper built the first large emoji sentiment lexicon from human-annotated tweets, becoming a standard resource for emoji-aware sentiment analysis.
